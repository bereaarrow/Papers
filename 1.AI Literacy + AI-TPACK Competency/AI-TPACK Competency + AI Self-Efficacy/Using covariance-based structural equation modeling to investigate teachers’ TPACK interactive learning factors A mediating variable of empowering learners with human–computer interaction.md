# 1. Introduction

The Information and Communication Technology (ICT) sector has become a cornerstone of the global digital economy, driving industrial innovation and knowledge-intensive development (Claro et al., 2024; Hooshyar et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025). ICT-driven innovation requires the continual acquisition and integration of emer- ging technological knowledge to sustain competitiveness (Hou, 2024; Zhu et al., 2023). Recent forecasts indi- cate that artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) may replace as many as 85 million jobs worldwide, underscoring the profound in uence of ICT and AI on labor market dynamics (Chen et al., 2025; Petri Rocha et al., 2024; Vietnam Investment Review, 2023). As industries accelerate the deployment of 5G and other advanced tech- nologies, ICT is expected to become a core infrastructure across sectors (Amnuaysin et al., [2023)](#disclosure-statement). Global surveys reveal that automated employment in AI – and ICT-related ﬁelds already accounts for 60–70% of workforce activities, and nearly 80% of multinational corporations have intensiﬁed digital transformation strategies (Gökalp et al., 2024; Sofwan et al., 2024; White et al., 2024).

Against this backdrop, the labor market confronts signiﬁcant automation challenges, prompting organ- izations to design long-term professional development strategies. ICT not only propels economic growth but also reshapes human resource training and educational priorities (Chen et al., 2025; Shahzad et al., 2024). In technical and vocational education (TVE), ICT serves both as a foundational skill and as a pro- fessional tool for instruction and management. By enhancing knowledge dissemination, information acqui- sition, and active learning, ICT strengthens the capacity of education systems to cultivate EdTech-ready graduates (Kong & Wang, 2024; Yilmaz-Na & Sönmez, 2023). Consequently, the preparation of ICT-competent professionals in TVE has become critical for national and industrial development, motivating institutions

Research on ICT in education has traditionally emphasized teachers’ digital competence, its antecedents, and professional training (Chen et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023). Prior studies highlight both psychological factors and external supports that shape teachers’ willingness to integrate ICT into practice (Claro et al., 2024; Li & Li, 2024; Sary et al., 2023; Walter, 2024). However, much TPACK research continues to prioritize teachers’ use of digital tools for lesson plan- ning over their capacity to guide student-centered, skill-oriented activities (Claro et al., 2024; Madzamba & Matorevhu, 2024).

Emerging evidence suggests that teachers’ ICT teaching self-e cacy (ICT-TSE) and their ability to create collaborative, cognitively activating environments are central to innovative teaching behaviors within the TPACK framework (Alomiear, [2024;](#disclosure-statement) Liu et al., 2021; Mena-Guacas et al., 2025; Temirbekova et al., 2022). Yet existing studies often examine ICT-TSE and TPACK in isolation, overlooking the mediating in uence of empowering learners through human – computer interaction (EL-HCI) (Clipa et al., 2023; Gil-Flores et al., 2024; Mapisa & Makena, 2024; Xu et al., 2025). TPACK emphasizes that teachers should understand the content they teach, the most e ective teaching strategies for that content, and the technological tools that can facilitate learning. In the context of emerging ICT technologies such as artiﬁcial intelligence, aug- mented reality, and gamiﬁcation, TPACK ensures that teachers use ICT tools and integrate them into their teaching processes to support and enhance instruction (Almaiah et al., [2022;](#disclosure-statement) Azimkhan et al., 2025; Mena- Guacas et al., 2025). Incorporating EL-HCI acknowledges students’ diverse learning needs and leverages interactive technologies to foster critical thinking and higher-order problem solving (Madzamba & Mator- evhu, 2024; Stumbrienė et al., 2023).

Within contemporary classrooms, human – computer interaction (HCI) has emerged as a powerful means of promoting autonomy, critical thinking, and problem-solving skills. Teachers with high ICT-TSE conﬁdently integrate digital tools, guide students in navigating interactive systems, and address technical challenges – behaviors that elevate both teaching performance and student engagement (Bandura, 1997). The TPACK framework further provides an essential theoretical lens for harmonizing technology, pedagogy, and content (Mishra & Koehler, 2006). E ective interactive learning requires not only technological uency but also pedagogical and content expertise; lacking any dimension may limit the depth of student learning. Through thoughtful TPACK integration, teachers can transform interactive tools – such as real-time feedback systems, simulations, and game-based platforms – into catalysts for deep learning and collaborative exploration.

Building on these perspectives, the present study investigates how teachers’ ICT-TSE, EL-HCI, and TPACK collectively shape innovative instructional practices. Drawing on innovation di usion theory, technology acceptance models, and cognitive activation frameworks, we employ covariance-based structural equation modeling to examine how EL-HCI mediates the relationship between teachers’ ICT self-e cacy and their TPACK-driven integration of ICT in the classroom. This approach elucidates how empowering learners through rich human – computer interactions can strengthen teachers’ adoption of ICT and, in turn, enhance student participation and problem-solving outcomes.

## _1.1. Research purpose_

Teachers’ ICT-related teaching self-e cacy (ICT-TSE) and Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) are mutually reinforcing. ICT-TSE shapes teachers’ conﬁdence and adaptability in digital teaching environments, whereas TPACK o ers a conceptual framework for the strategic integration of technology that is not merely as an instructional tool but as a means to achieve learning objectives. Within a human – computer interaction (HCI)-driven interactive learning environment, these two components function synergistically to empower learners. Such synergy enables students to acquire knowledge while cultivating self-regulated learning and cross-disciplinary problem-solving skills through exploration, collaboration, and innovation. Accordingly, this study pursues three speciﬁc objectives:

## _1.2. Theoretical framework_

First, from the perspective of innovation di usion, exploring teachers’ TPACK in combination with innovative technology adoption involves analyzing the prior experiences and attributes that in uence their decision- making during the dissemination and promotion of new ideas (Chiu et al., 2025; Hava & Babayiğit, 2025). Teachers recognize the advantages of TPACK-based technological innovations, which facilitate rapid di usion. However, their use of ICT-integrated innovations is in uenced by factors such as time, cognitive attributes, decision-making styles, communication channels, school infrastructure, and student learning characteristics (Baran & AlZoubi, 2023; Chen et al., 2025; Hsiao et al., 2024).

Teachers’ acceptance of technology in educational settings plays a critical role in shaping their intention to integrate digital tools into teaching practices (Chen et al., 2025; Antonietti et al., 2022; Thomas et al., 2024; Xin et al., 2024). TPACK development builds on teachers’ ICT competence, encompassing knowledge acqui- sition, knowledge deepening, and knowledge creation, as well as professional expertise and technological complexity. Prior studies have found a positive and signiﬁcant relationship between teachers’ beliefs about their TPACK capabilities and their perceptions of both ease of use and the usefulness of technology for teaching, which in turn in uence their intention to use technology (Claro et al., 2024; Salehi et al., 2024; Thomas et al., 2024; Xin et al., 2024). 

H1: Teachers’ perceptions of ICT-TSE are signiﬁcantly related to TPACK.

Second, this study’s hypotheses are based on self-e cacy theory (Bandura, 1997) and human–computer interaction (HCI) theory. According to self-e cacy theory, an individual’s conﬁdence in their ability to com- plete a speciﬁc task directly in uences their behavior and decision-making (Bandura, 1997; Xu, 2025;Yang & Du, 2024). In the teaching context, teachers’ perceptions of information and communication technology teaching self-e cacy (ICT-TSE) determine their willingness and ability to interact and integrate technology in digital environments. Furthermore, HCI theory suggests that users’ interactive experience and technology acceptance in uence their cognitive load, operational uency, and learning outcomes (van Haastrecht et al., 2024; Xu, 2025). Combining these two theories, this study hypothesizes that teachers’ ICT-TSE positively in uences their perceptions of immersive and interactive learning environments (EL-HCI). Previous research indicates that teachers’ information technology teaching self-e cacy (ICT-TSE) positively in uences their acceptance of technology integration and human–computer interaction environments (Shen et al., 2024; Xu, 2025; Yang & Du, 2024). For example, Xu (2025) found that teachers with high ICT-TSE were more likely to adopt innovative digital teaching tools and conduct e ective human–computer interaction in the classroom. Yang and Du (2024) also pointed out that ICT-TSE is a key factor in promoting teachers’ accep- tance of new interactive learning environments, and its positive impact signiﬁcantly improves the quality of teaching interactions and student engagement. 

H2: Teachers’ perception of ICT-TSE is signiﬁcantly related to EL-HCI.

Third, Cognitive Activation Theory (CAT) provides a framework for examining teachers’ enactment of enhanced learning through human – computer interaction (EL-HCI). CAT posits that teachers’ responses to pressures such as ICT integration depend on their understanding of the technological context, their cognitive appraisal of expected teaching outcomes, and the behaviors required to meet these challenges (Alomiear, [2024;](#disclosure-statement) Liu & Liu, 2020; Von Kotzebue et al., 2020). Through human – computer interaction (HCI), students can access essential information via ICT systems to support their learning tasks. When designing TPACK-based learning experiences, teachers must incorporate ICT design elements that enhance the student learning experience (Hsiao et al., 2024; Stringer et al., 2023; Vellanki et al., 2024).

CAT applied to EL-HCI highlights the knowledge and strategies teachers use when facing challenging situ- ations. Cognitive activation in teaching refers to stimulating learners to think deeply and investigate topics

# 2. Literature review

### _2.1. Teachers’ TPACK and in_ _uencing factors_

Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) highlights that teachers’ professional competence lies in the integrated application of content knowledge, pedagogical knowledge, and technological knowl- edge (Madzamba & Matorevhu, 2024) . This framework emphasizes the dynamic interaction among these three domains. Teachers must not only possess a deep understanding of subject matter but also know how to teach it e ectively while leveraging technology to support and enhance the learning process (Hsiao et al., 2024; Li & Li, 2024; Madzamba & Matorevhu, 2024; Ren et al., 2025).

Teachers who are willing to integrate ICT into their teaching demonstrate a positive attitude toward tech- nology adoption, yet they also require su cient ICT competence and professional expertise (Mapisa & Makena, 2024; Thomas et al., 2024). Multiple factors in uence teachers’ decisions to adopt and integrate ICT, including the strategic importance of TPACK in education and the potential to transform classroom prac- tices with greater exibility, interactivity, and accessibility (Lawrence & Tar, 2018). From the perspective of CAT-driven human – computer interaction (HCI) tasks and self-regulated learning (SRL), teachers implement Experiential Learning – Human – Computer Interaction (EL-HCI) principles that correspond to the three core stages of SRL (Ge et al., 2025; Mejeh et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2024). 

1. _Forethought stage:_ Teachers focus on goal setting and motivation. Using CAT mechanisms, they design challenging, problem-oriented tasks that require learners to establish explicit learning goals and develop strategic plans under systematic prompts. SRL processes in this stage align with Zimmerman’s model of goal setting and self-e cacy. AI-based or automated feedback further helps learners reﬁne strategies and improve goal operability.
2. _Performance stage:_ Teachers emphasize metacognitive monitoring and strategy adjustment. Through real-time interaction, dynamic prompts, and adaptive di culty, CAT-based EL-HCI tasks encourage lear- ners to engage in self-testing and strategy reﬁnement, re ecting the SRL processes of “self-control” and “self-observation”.
3. _Self-re_ _ection stage:_ Teachers guide learners in self-evaluation and causal attribution. CAT mechanisms such as high-level questioning and automated learning logs prompt in-depth re ection and evaluation of strategies. This aligns with Zimmerman’s concept of “self-evaluation and causal attribution,” reinfor- cing internalized adjustments and future strategy revisions.

In this framework, CAT provides the external drive – cognitive challenges and interactive design – while SRL provides the internal regulation of goal orientation and self-monitoring. Together, they complement each other under EL-HCI principles: CAT’s high-level tasks trigger motivation and deep thinking, while SRL ensures these tasks translate into sustainable self-regulatory behaviors (Galindo-Domínguez et al., 2024; Hava & Babayiğit, 2025).

Building on this foundation, four key constructs capture teachers’ innovative teaching behavior within a TPACK context: 

1. _Digitization of course content:_ The extent to which teachers’ innovative teaching, grounded in TPACK, sur- passes the concepts or technologies it replaces. Indicators include teaching e ectiveness, institutional reputation, and student learning outcomes.
2. _Technology compatibility:_ The degree to which TPACK-driven innovations align with existing values, prior experiences, and students’ learning needs. Greater ICT proﬁciency reduces uncertainty for both teachers and learners, thereby strengthening innovation adoption and a nity.
3. _Teaching innovation:_ Any new concept, method, instructional plan, or technology designed to enhance educational quality. Teachers must acquire new ICT knowledge and skills to diversify teaching methods and media, thereby expanding instructional breadth.
4. _Digital teaching e_ _ectiveness:_ The ability to assimilate new information from ICT and adopt innovative tools or concepts to reshape existing cognition and behavior, ultimately improving instructional out- comes. Teachers with higher innovation acceptance exhibit stronger problem-solving abilities, greater adaptive capacity, and enhanced decision-making potential. 

## _2.2. Teachers’ ICT-TSE as a predictor of TPACK_

Teachers’ ICT – Technology Self-E cacy (ICT-TSE) refers to their perceived competence in using technology and their speciﬁc abilities in ICT-related tasks, including communication, collaboration, safety, and security (Li & Li, 2024; Ren & Wu, 2025; Rubach et al., 2023; Sawiji, 2024).

Empirical studies demonstrate that the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) e ectively explains teachers’ acceptance of technology and their intention to integrate ICT-based digital tools in vocational education. Notably, teachers’ beliefs in their own ICT capabilities are signiﬁcantly related to perceived ease of use when implementing technology in teaching (Hooshyar et al., 2024; Stringer et al., 2023). Xu and Zhu (2023) further argue that ICT self-e cacy, professional development focused on ICT, and ICT-oriented instructional practices directly in uence teachers’ technology-related behaviors. ICT literacy, positive prior experiences, and self-e cacy exert indirect e ects, whereas institutional support and technology-integration e cacy have direct impacts on ICT use in classrooms (Ng et al., 2023; Paetsch et al., 2023; van Haastrecht et al., 2024).

Four key factors – teachers’ attitudes (AT), self-e cacy (SE), digital competence (DC), and digital tool util- ization (DTU) – have been shown to signiﬁcantly shape teachers’ TPACK (Gökalp et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2023; Randall et al., 2023; Tzaﬁlkou et al., 2023). Clear curriculum guidelines and learner motivation also emerge as critical drivers for the integration of informatics. These concepts are elaborated below: 

1. _Digital thinking:_ The application of digital tools throughout the teaching process improves e ciency in tasks such as textbook preparation, class management, and homework design, while enhancing interac- tivity. This approach familiarizes students with digital tools and cultivates both digital literacy and inde- pendent learning.
2. _ICT-technology profession:_ ICT professionalism encompasses the integration of information and communi- cation technology into teaching, including programming, communication technologies, and computer literacy. By linking teaching expertise with knowledge creation, students gain access to digital resources and beneﬁt from emerging modalities such as distance learning, thereby improving overall educational e ectiveness.
3. _Usability cognition:_ Teachers’ subjective perception that ICT systems enhance their work performance strongly predicts adoption. When social in uence is high and systems are perceived as easy to use, tea- chers demonstrate greater willingness to implement TPACK-based practices.

## _2.3. Teachers’ EL-HCI as a mediator of TPACK_

Human – computer interaction (HCI) enables teachers to integrate voice and visual tools into their instruc- tion, presenting information on interfaces that are simple and clear. ICT strengthens HCI by employing voice prompts, contrast markings, images, and multimedia, while also supporting collaboration among teaching coaches, colleagues, and students (Gil-Flores et al., 2024; Lucas et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025). The primary goal of Experiential Learning – Human – Computer Interaction (EL-HCI) is to prioritize learners’ needs. Accord- ingly, teachers use EL-HCI to incorporate learner-based di erentiated instruction, technology-enhanced learning, and technology-enhanced classroom management, thereby tailoring instruction to diverse needs and enabling successful task completion (Fang et al., 2024; Sortwell et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024).

From the perspective of Cognitive Activation Theory (CAT), e ective teaching enhances students’ intrinsic motivation rather than relying solely on smart technologies (Randall et al., 2023; White et al., 2024). Teachers’ ICT application is closely linked to their teaching self-e cacy, students’ cognitive activation, and organiz- ational support at the school level (Gil-Flores et al., 2024; Stumbrienė et al., 2023). In addition, “digital tool use,” “digital content dissemination,” and “digital content creation” all signiﬁcantly in uence ICT capa- bility (Niyazova et al., 2023). Teachers’ ability-related beliefs about di erentiating student abilities and empowering learners are therefore critical to using technology to improve instructional quality (Sortwell et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024). Understanding these beliefs and their relationship with technology-enhanced teaching is essential for addressing learner diversity and fostering e ective, innovative instruction (Rungre et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024).

The social environment also shapes teachers’ technology-adoption behaviors through the mediating roles of teaching self-e cacy and outcome expectations (Alomiear, [2024;](#disclosure-statement) Lucas et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024). Research indicates that student-centered TPACK exerts a stronger impact on instructional innovation than teacher-centered approaches (Alomiear, [2024;](#disclosure-statement) White et al., 2024). Di erentiated learning that empow- ers students and encourages active participation emerges as a key dimension (Alomiear, [2024;](#disclosure-statement) Groß-Mlynek et al., 2022). Teachers’ self-e cacy in professional collaboration, team innovation, and fostering student engagement is strongly associated with students’ acquisition of technological knowledge (Fang et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024).

In smart education environments, cultivating students’ digital learning abilities requires coordinated interaction between students and technology rather than dependence on a single smart device (Gökalp et al., 2024; Lucas et al., 2024; Yan & Li, 2023). Drawing on CAT, teachers’ HCI-based instruction in uences the innovative adoption of TPACK across three critical dimensions (Lucas et al., 2024; Sen et al., 2022; Sortwell et al., 2024; Suzer & Koc, 2024; Xu et al., 2024): 

1. _Learner-based di_ _erentiated instruction:_ Focuses on identifying and addressing diverse learning needs. Teachers tailor content to individual students and employ ICT-di erentiated strategies that enable real-time instructional decisions based on students’ progress.
2. _Technology-enhanced learning:_ Leverages HCI to extend knowledge, boost motivation, and improve learn- ing e ectiveness. Teachers provide adaptive diagnostic tools that help students identify weaknesses and follow precise TPACK-based remedial pathways.
3. _Technology-enhanced classroom management:_ Centers on student-driven learning and enriched teacher – student interaction. By integrating technology and multimedia, teachers create personalized learning paths and generate detailed analytics for teachers, schools, and parents, supporting independent learn- ing and continuous progress.

3. Before the pilot test, the questionnaire underwent revisions for face and content validity. For face validity, six secondary-school teachers evaluated each item’s clarity and relevance. The open-ended items collected teachers’ experiences and feedback regarding the use of HCI to empower learners and integrate ICT into teaching. Responses were subsequently coded using a content-analysis classiﬁcation table (Appendix A).

## _3.2. Participants_

A total of 673 secondary school teachers participated in this study, selected using a stratiﬁed random sampling method. Stratiﬁcation was based on seven categories of professional certiﬁcates for in-service tea- chers (Statistics Division of the Ministry of Education, 2024a; 2024b). This approach employs independent sampling within each stratum, facilitating comparisons across di erent professional groups. In stratiﬁed random sampling, every member of a stratum has an equal probability of selection, which is particularly appropriate when the population exhibits substantial heterogeneity or uneven distribution, as it minimizes the potential for disproportionate in uence from speciﬁc units.

The study aimed to investigate teachers’ perceptions of innovative teaching behavior associated with TPACK. Teachers’ professional categories were classiﬁed into seven ﬁelds: humanities and arts; engineering, manufacturing, and construction; services; social sciences, commerce, and law; science; agriculture; and medicine, health, and social welfare. Participants were randomly selected based on school characteristics and gender, with proportional allocation to ensure that the sample distribution re ected the overall popu- lation of teachers in Taiwan.

For sampling calculations, schools were randomly selected by computer. Participants were stratiﬁed by school type (public/private), gender, and professional category. Sampling followed the Statistics Department of the Ministry of Education (2020) registry, identifying 508 public and private secondary schools with voca- tional-subject teachers nationwide, comprising 304 public schools and 204 private schools. The total popu- lation included 50,649 teachers across 17,858 classes, with 37,392 public school teachers and 13,257 private school teachers, and a gender distribution of 20,643 male and 30,006 female teachers (Statistics Division of the Ministry of Education, 2024a).

The study ultimately sampled 673 teachers from 25 secondary schools, including 17 public and 8 private schools (Statistics Division of the Ministry of Education, 2024a; 2024b). Sampling considered teacher-speciﬁc characteristics such as school type, gender, professional ﬁeld, and ICT experience. The minimum e ective sample size was calculated using the formula by Krejcie and Morgan (1970) and determined to be 387. 2. Assessment of multicollinearity

Because multicollinearity can bias parameter estimates and hinder interpretation, the study ﬁrst inspected variance in ation factors (VIFs). VIF represents the inverse of tolerance; values exceeding 5 – or more con- servatively 10 – are typically viewed as problematic. Correlation coe cients among the observed variables ranged from .30 to .71, indicating no concerningly high correlations. All VIFs fell between 1.50 and 4.15, well below the critical threshold of 10, conﬁrming that multicollinearity was not a threat to model estimation and met recommended standards for model ﬁt (Kyriazos & Poga, 2023; Vörösmarty & Dobos, 2020). 3. CB-SEM versus PLS-SEM

A brief comparison clariﬁes why CB-SEM was selected over partial least squares SEM (PLS-SEM). The two approaches di er fundamentally in purpose and underlying assumptions: 

1. _Research purpose_: CB-SEM is conﬁrmatory, testing whether an established theoretical model ﬁts the data. It is ideal when hypotheses are well speciﬁed and the goal is theory veriﬁcation. In contrast, PLS-SEM is predictive and exploratory, designed to maximize the explanatory power (R2) and predictive relevance (Q2) of endogenous variables when theory is still emerging.

1. _Model and data characteristics:_ CB-SEM relies on covariance matrix estimation, requires relatively large samples (commonly \> 200), and assumes approximate multivariate normality. It is best suited for re ective measurement models and complex theory-driven structures. Model adequacy is judged using global ﬁt statistics such as the chi-square test, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR. PLS-SEM employs iterative partial- least-squares estimation, tolerates non-normal data and smaller samples (≈ 50–100), and handles forma- tive indicators or high multicollinearity. Predictive accuracy is assessed via R2, f2, Q2, PLSpredict, and RMSE.

Given the present study’s well-established theoretical foundation, su cient sample size, and data meeting normality assumptions, CB-SEM was the appropriate analytic technique. It enabled simultaneous

## _3.5. Reliability and validity_

1. Reliability

Reliability analysis was conducted to assess the internal consistency and stability of the measurement items, which re ects the overall reliability of the scale. Higher reliability indicates greater measurement accuracy. In this study, SPSS was used for reliability analysis. The internal consistency coe cient (Cronbach’s α) of each sub- scale was required to exceed 0.60, and that of the overall scale to exceed 0.80 (Hussey et al., 2025; McCrae et al., 2011). The total scale, “In uencing Factors in Teachers’ Innovative Teaching Behavior Regarding the TPACK,” consisted of 50 items. The Cronbach’s α for the overall scale was 0.88, and the subscales ranged from 0.87 to 0.92, indicating that the instrument demonstrates satisfactory reliability and is suitable for research purposes. 

2. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

Pre-test data (N = 186) were used to perform exploratory factor analysis to explore the preliminary theoreti- cal structure. The 60 items retained after item analysis were subjected to EFA. Using the principal component analysis with varimax rotation, three factors were initially extracted to examine the factor structure. After deleting items with low loadings or cross-loadings, 50 items remained and underwent a second EFA. All items demonstrated factor loadings above 0.40, within the predeﬁned factor structure. The three factors explained 73.80% of the total variance, indicating good construct validity. The retained items are summar- ized in Appendices C, E, and G.

After removing items with loadings below 0.45 and facets with fewer than two items, a second analysis yielded a KMO of 0.868, factor loadings between 0.531 and 0.808, and a cumulative variance of 70.75%. The ﬁnalized ICT-TSE scale comprised 15 items across three constructs: Digital Thinking (4 items; α = 0.92), ICT-Technology Profession (5 items; α = 0.89), and Usability Cognition (5 items; α = 0.88), explaining 70.75% of the variance (Appendix C).

This study employs the maximum likelihood estimation (ML) method, using evaluation criteria categor- ized into three types: absolute ﬁt, incremental ﬁt, and parsimonious ﬁt, as classiﬁed by Hair et al. (1998). The analysis results are detailed below.

First, the overall evaluation results of the measurement model indicate that χ2 = 75.75, _p_ \< .05, which reaches the signiﬁcance level for this research model. This suggests a signiﬁcant di erence between the hypothesized model and the covariance matrix of the empirical data. However, since the χ2 test can be easily in uenced by sample size and data normality, it is crucial to consider additional indicators when eval- uating the model’s overall suitability.

In summary, all indicators of absolute ﬁt, incremental ﬁt, and parsimonious ﬁt meet the standards and are within the acceptable range. Overall, the data model ﬁt in this study is excellent; the model aligns well with the empirical data, indicating that the theoretical model e ectively explains the empirical observations.

## _4.6. Teachers’ use of HCI to empower learners and integrate ICT in teaching practices_

A total of 412 valid responses were collected from 673 teachers regarding their experiences of using human – computer interaction (HCI) to empower learners and to integrate information and communication technol- ogy (ICT) into teaching practices. The distribution of reported practices indicates that facilitating learners’ digital competence accounted for the largest proportion (23.3%), followed by integrating ICT-based teach- ing and learning strategies (21.3%), utilizing digital ICT resources (19.6%), engaging in professional develop- ment and collaboration (19.3%), and conducting ICT-supported assessment (16.5%).

These ﬁndings suggest that teachers view HCI not merely as a technical add-on but as a pedagogical enabler that strengthens students’ capacity for self-directed and technology-enhanced learning. From the perspective of

Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) theory (Mishra & Koehler, 2006), such practices re ect the dynamic interplay between teachers’ technological knowledge and their pedagogical strategies, enabling more meaningful integration of ICT into subject-speciﬁc contexts. Moreover, the emphasis on learners’ digital competence resonates with the European Digital Competence Framework for Educators (DigCompEdu), which highlights educators’ role in fostering students’ autonomous digital skills (Redecker, 2017).

The prominence of professional engagement and assessment further aligns with Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura, 1997), which underscores the importance of teachers’ self-e cacy in adopting innovative tools. Prior studies have shown that teachers with higher technology self-e cacy are more likely to implement interactive and student-centered ICT approaches (González-Prida et al., 2024; Hooshyar et al., 2024). Integrat- ing HCI in this way creates an interactive environment that enhances motivation, feedback, and learner agency, consistent with the principles of constructivist learning theory.

5. **Discussion**

First, the overall model demonstrated a strong ﬁt with the empirical data, indicating that the theoretical framework appropriately explains the observed relationships. From the perspective of innovation di usion, this study examined teachers’ TPACK in conjunction with innovative technology adoption, high- lighting how teachers’ prior experiences and characteristics in uence their decision-making when imple- menting innovations. Key factors include teachers’ perceptions of new technologies, evaluations of potential value, integration into teaching practices, and attitudes toward change. Understanding these elements is essential for promoting e ective technology adoption in education.

Second, examining the relationships among ICT-TSE, EL-HCI, and TPACK revealed that teachers’ ICT-TSE signiﬁcantly in uences both innovative teaching behavior and perceptions of the teaching environment, pri- marily through its e ect on EL-HCI. Additionally, EL-HCI has a direct and signiﬁcant impact on TPACK. Cog- nitive Activation Theory of Stress (CATS) emphasizes that stress is a complex process shaped by individual evaluation and response, a ecting cognitive processes and subsequent behavior.

Third, EL-HCI exerts a stronger in uence on TPACK perceptions than ICT-TSE. While ICT-TSE directly impacts TPACK, EL-HCI mediates this e ect, suggesting that teachers’ engagement in human – computer interaction is crucial for technology adoption. Positive correlations among ICT-TSE, EL-HCI, and TPACK were consistent with prior studies (Baran & AlZoubi, 2023; Chiu et al., 2025; Li & Li, 2024).

Moreover, successful EL-HCI – mediated TPACK implementation depends on teachers’ TPACK literacy, pedagogical strategies, and technological competence. Integrating TPACK with literacy-oriented approaches, such as STEAM, can enhance instructional design and foster student motivation, especially for low-achieving learners. Teachers’ design thinking supports re ective, iterative knowledge-building, pro- moting learner-centered solutions and exible teaching practices.

Finally, integrating ICT and HCI in teaching empowers learners and improves interaction and e ciency (Galindo-Domínguez et al., 2024; Hava & Babayiğit, 2025; Madzamba & Matorevhu, 2024; Ren & Wu, 2025). Digital tools, including LMS and interactive whiteboards, facilitate e ective instruction and enhance students’ engagement (Chen et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025).

In summary, while this model is comprehensive, unexplored factors may limit explanatory power. Future research should investigate potential missing variables and collect broader empirical data to enhance model validity.

# 6. Implications

First, teachers should enhance their ICT-related technological self-e cacy (ICT-TSE), as ICT competencies are fundamental professional skills that educators are expected to acquire. By leveraging these capabilities, tea- chers can strengthen their self-management e cacy and professional teaching competence, thereby improving instructional e ectiveness and student academic performance. Moreover, teachers should culti- vate a sense of creative teaching, adopt innovative instructional strategies, and engage students actively in the classroom to continuously enhance learning outcomes.

Second, in the context of human – computer interaction (HCI), the role of technology is characterized by “empowerment”. EL-HCI is applied in teaching practices through interactive learning activities designed

to enhance both learning e ciency and students’ interest in learning. Such tasks increase the interactivity and frequency of engagement during the learning process by integrating teachers’ pedagogical knowl- edge and skills with the interactive functions of technology. With the advancement of artiﬁcial intelli- gence, personalized learning systems are increasingly being adopted. These systems can analyze students’ learning behaviors in real time and adjust instructional content and pacing according to individ- ual learning needs.

Lastly, ICT facilitates the creation of more interactive learning environments, such as electronic white- boards, interactive software, and dynamic teacher – student engagement. Online platforms enable students to select learning content and schedules independently, free from the constraints of time and location. Data collected through human – computer interaction tools allow teachers to analyze student performance, identify learning bottlenecks, and adjust instructional strategies accordingly. By providing online resources and courses, teachers can encourage students to engage in self-directed learning, thereby enhancing their autonomy and motivation.

7. **Conclusion**

This study demonstrates that teachers’ ICT self-e cacy (ICT-TSE), technological pedagogical content knowl- edge (TPACK), and perceptions of enhanced learning through human – computer interaction (EL-HCI) are closely interconnected. Higher ICT-TSE fosters the development of TPACK and promotes more e ective tech- nology-enhanced learning environments through EL-HCI. Conversely, EL-HCI further reinforces TPACK adop- tion, underscoring the reciprocal nature of these constructs. Collectively, the validated impact model highlights how digital thinking, ICT-related competencies, and usability cognition shape innovative teaching and students’ learning outcomes.

Beyond empirical validation, the ﬁndings contribute to theory by clarifying the mediating and reinforcing roles of EL-HCI in linking ICT-TSE and TPACK, thereby extending prior research on teacher technology adop- tion. Practically, the results suggest that strengthening teachers’ digital competence, fostering positive atti- tudes toward technology, and aligning school culture and curriculum with ICT integration are critical strategies for improving instructional quality.

Nevertheless, the study is constrained by its cross-sectional design and the speciﬁc educational context of technical and vocational education, which may limit generalizability. Future research should consider longi- tudinal approaches, cross-cultural comparisons, and additional mediating variables to further reﬁne the proposed model. As digital transformation accelerates under Industry 4.0, enhancing teachers’ and students’ ICT competencies remains vital for sustaining innovation and meeting the demands of contemporary education.

## _7.1. Limitations and future research_

First, a disparity in ICT proﬁciency between teachers and students may negatively a ect teaching quality. This study acknowledges several limitations, including funding and equipment constraints, as well as privacy and security concerns. Future research should focus on developing communication platforms that facilitate the sharing of experiences and instructional strategies among teachers, enabling more e ective integration of ICT tools into classroom practices.

Second, while human – computer interaction (HCI) can enhance teaching e ectiveness, its potential is constrained by educational equity issues. Di erences in access to advanced ICT resources across regions and schools may result in unequal educational opportunities. To address these challenges, teachers can implement adaptive learning strategies tailored to learners’ individual needs, including di erentiated materials, instructional approaches, and varying levels of guidance. Moreover, employing diverse teaching resources – such as images, audio, and video – can accommodate students’ varied learning styles and preferences.

Third, teachers can leverage learning management systems (LMS) and intelligent instructional soft- ware to design customized learning plans based on students’ progress, thereby promoting more e ective outcomes. Real-time feedback functionalities in HCI platforms allow teachers to e ciently collect assignments and provide immediate guidance, helping learners identify and correct mistakes

Fifth, while this study replicated the established ICT-TSE – TPACK relationship, it did not propose new mediating variables, such as automated cognitive engagement analysis. Advances in automated detection of cognitive states during online discussions can enable EL-HCI systems to respond dynamically to learners’ needs. Cognitive engagement has primarily been assessed via facial expression analysis and text classiﬁ- cation models, with machine learning algorithms predicting engagement levels.
