# 1. Introduction

The integration of Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI) into education is no longer a futuristic prospect but a present reality. Its applications now span adaptive learning systems, intelligent tutoring, automated administration, and Generative AI (GenAI) tools for content creation and student inquiry (Miao et al., 2021; Sajja et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2024; Zawacki-richter et al., 2019). The pace and breadth of this transformation mean that engage- ment with AI has become a necessity for educational institutions (Chen et al., 2020; Ghatge & Parasar, 2023; Karataş et al., 2025; Virmani et al., 2026; Zafari et al., 2022). However, the e ective integration of these tech- nologies is not automatic. It is fundamentally mediated by teachers, who serve as the primary agents of ped- agogical change and the gatekeepers through which innovations reach classrooms (Brown et al., [2021; ](#disclosure-statement)Datnow, 2020; Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010). The success of any AI-driven initiative, therefore, hinges on teachers’ readiness, acceptance, and professional competence (Ayanwale et al., [2022; ](#disclosure-statement)Guilen & Oni, 2025; Vongkulluksn et al., 2018). The infusion of AI into education also signals a shift in the teacher’s role, moving it beyond traditional information delivery towards that of a learning orchestrator (Agarwal et al., [2025; ](#disclosure-statement)Schleicher, 2018). Teachers must now blend human-centric pedagogy with AI’s data-driven capabilities. This evolving role carries dual responsibility. Teachers must be adept at using AI to support instruction and also capable of teaching about AI, preparing students to navigate an AI-driven society critically and ethically (Fullan, 2023). To unlock the poten- tial of AI in education, merely providing access to technology is insu cient. The focus must instead be on devel- oping the speciﬁc, multifaceted competencies that teachers require.

This critical set of competencies is deﬁned in literature as AI literacy. The construct was prominently con- ceptualized by Long and Magerko (2020) as a set of competencies enabling individuals to critically evaluate

AI systems, communicate and collaborate e ectively with them, and use AI as a tool across diverse contexts. Subsequent research has reinforced that AI literacy is inherently multidimensional, extending beyond narrow technical proﬁciency or conventional digital literacy (Ng et al., 2021). Rather than focusing solely on operational skills, AI literacy encompasses the knowledge, practices, and dispositions required to engage with AI systems in informed and responsible ways. Broadly, the construct comprises three intercon- nected components. The ﬁrst is foundational knowledge of core AI concepts, such as machine learning, data, and algorithms, along with an understanding of AI systems’ capabilities and limitations. The second involves practical and procedural skills for applying AI tools to speciﬁc purposes, including problem solving, instruc- tional support, and content creation. The third incorporates a critical and ethical perspective on the societal and personal implications of AI, including concerns related to bias, privacy, transparency, and accountability (Laupichler et al., 2022). These components are not independent domains but operate in combination, re ecting the situated nature of AI use in real-world contexts. For teachers, this conception is particularly salient. Teachers are expected not only to use AI technologies to support instruction but also to make informed pedagogical judgments about when, how, and why such tools should be integrated into learning (Adams et al., [2022;](#disclosure-statement) Salas-Pilco et al., 2022). AI literacy, therefore, equips teachers to align AI use with instruc- tional goals, interpret AI-supported outputs, and model responsible engagement with emerging technol- ogies for their students.

Establishing what AI literacy encompasses also requires clarifying what it does not. Despite growing con- sensus around the deﬁnition outlined above, AI literacy remains susceptible to con ation with related but operationally distinct constructs (Chiu, 2025; Chiu, Ahmad, Ismailov, et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2021). In particular, three constructs frequently appear alongside AI literacy in the literature, namely AI readiness, AI compe- tence, and AI integration. Nevertheless, each captures a di erent aspect of teachers’ engagement with AI. This con ation is not merely a terminological concern. When these constructs are treated as interchange- able, the instruments designed to assess them inevitably measure di erent things under the same label, fragmenting rather than consolidating the evidence base. AI readiness refers primarily to an individual’s or institution’s preparedness to adopt AI-driven change (Ó Ceallaigh et al., 2025). It encompasses attitudinal orientation, structural support, and perceived capacity to adapt, without necessarily implying the possession of speciﬁc knowledge or skills (Vongkulluksn et al., 2018). AI integration, meanwhile, denotes a process of embedding AI tools into instructional practice rather than a property of the individual teacher (Almasri, [2024)](#disclosure-statement). Both constructs may co-occur with AI literacy or function as antecedents to it, but they operate on fundamentally di erent axes and do not capture the same underlying reality. However, the relationship between AI literacy and AI competence is more nuanced. Recent scholarship positions literacy as a founda- tional layer upon which competence is built (Chiu, 2025; Chiu, Ahmad, Ismailov, et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025). Literacy concerns what individuals know and understand about AI, whereas competence concerns how e ectively they can apply that understanding in situated professional contexts. The two are closely related and at times used interchangeably in the literature, yet they operate at di erent levels. AI literacy, as adopted in this review, refers speciﬁcally to the knowledge, skills, and critical dispositions that enable tea- chers to understand, evaluate, and engage responsibly with AI systems in educational contexts (Laupichler et al., 2022; Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021). These conceptual boundaries carry direct implications for measurement. Instruments designed to assess readiness or integration behavior operationalize di erent constructs than those targeting literacy and con ating them risks compounding the very fragmentation this review seeks to diagnose.

Given the established importance and multidimensional nature of AI literacy, there is a pressing need for educational stakeholders to assess this competency among in-service and preservice teachers systematically (Ng et al., 2023; Sperling et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2022). This assessment is not an end in itself but serves several functions. It provides essential baseline data to understand teachers’ current strengths and weak- nesses. It guides the design of targeted professional development interventions. It also allows for the empiri- cal evaluation of program e ectiveness (Guskey, 2000). To carry out these diagnostic, formative, and summative evaluations, institutions must be equipped with high-quality assessment instruments. Without valid and reliable tools, policymakers, teacher training institutions, and school leaders lack the evidence needed to make informed decisions about curriculum reform, resource allocation, or teacher readiness cer- tiﬁcation (Selwyn, 2019). The challenge, therefore, extends beyond deﬁning AI literacy. It requires the devel- opment and validation of robust instruments capable of accurately measuring the construct. This

methodological foundation is a prerequisite for any scalable and evidence-based strategy to improve teacher AI literacy.

This need is particularly acute as research shifts toward designing and evaluating interventions to enhance teacher AI literacy. While the surge in intervention studies is promising, it raises concerns about instrumentation (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Lintner, 2024; Shi, 2025). The validity of intervention outcomes depends entirely on the quality of the measurement tools used. It remains unclear whether instruments cur- rently deployed possess su cient psychometric rigor. Without a synthesis of existing validity evidence, edu- cational stakeholders risk investing resources in professional development programs based on ﬁndings from unveriﬁed metrics. A critical appraisal of measurement tools is therefore not merely a methodological exer- cise but a prerequisite for accountability in teacher education.

The conceptual breadth of AI literacy further complicates measurement. Di erent studies may adopt the same label for instruments that emphasize di erent facets of the construct. One instrument may focus on technical knowledge and operational skills, while another may prioritize ethical awareness or critical under- standing. While each approach may be theoretically defensible, such variation complicates cross-study com- parison. It also makes it di cult to evaluate the relative e ectiveness of di erent interventions. Although systematic reviews of AI literacy scales have recently been conducted (Almatraﬁ et al., [2024;](#disclosure-statement) Biagini, [2024;](#disclosure-statement) Lintner, 2024; Su et al., 2025), existing reviews have not speciﬁcally examined the measurement tools used to assess teachers’ AI literacy. Nor have they critically synthesized the psychometric properties of these tools. As a result, the ﬁeld lacks a consolidated understanding of how teacher AI literacy has been oper- ationalized, which dimensions are most commonly assessed, and what forms of validity evidence have been reported. It also remains unclear whether existing instruments have kept pace with the emergence of GenAI systems in educational settings. Instruments grounded in earlier conceptualizations may only partially capture the competencies required in contemporary classrooms. Collectively, these issues point to a meth- odological and conceptual gap that calls for a systematic examination of how teacher AI literacy is currently being measured.

To address this gap, the present study conducts a systematic review of the literature. The primary objec- tive is to identify, synthesize, and critically appraise the measurement tools developed or used to assess tea- chers’ AI literacy. This review focuses on empirical studies published between 2019 and 2025 to capture the most recent developments in this rapidly evolving ﬁeld. Speciﬁcally, it aims to categorize the available tools, map the AI literacy dimensions they measure, and evaluate the reported psychometric quality of the evi- dence. The review will o er a resource for researchers and practitioners selecting instruments. It will also highlight robust tools for future research and identify gaps where further instrument development is necess- ary. To achieve these objectives, this systematic review is guided by the following research questions: 

. RQ1. What types of measurement tools have been developed or used to assess teachers’ AI literacy from 2019 to 2025?

. RQ2. What dimensions or components of AI literacy are commonly measured among teachers? . RQ3. What evidence of reliability, validity, and measurement quality is reported for these tools? . RQ4. What gaps and trends can be identiﬁed in the current measurement of teachers’ AI literacy?

# 2. Methodology

To ensure a rigorous and replicable review process, this study’s methodology was structured around the Pre- ferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Page et al., 2021).

## _2.1. Search strategy_

A systematic search of the literature was conducted to identify all relevant articles published between January 2019 and August 2025. The lower bound of the time frame was intentionally set to 2019 to capture early scholarly e orts on AI literacy and related competencies in education prior to the widespread public di usion of GenAI tools in late 2022. Although large-scale empirical research on teachers’ AI literacy largely emerged after 2022, earlier conceptualizations, pilot instruments, and foundational measurement

attempts were expected to have appeared in the preceding years. Including this earlier period allowed the review to trace the evolution of measurement practices, avoid retrospective bias driven by recent techno- logical hype, and ensure that potentially overlooked early instruments were systematically examined.

The search was conducted across four key electronic databases relevant to education, technology, and com- puter science, namely Scopus, Web of Science (WoS), Education Resources Information Center (ERIC), and IEEE Xplore. This selection ensured comprehensive coverage of education-focused literature (ERIC) as well as technol- ogy-oriented and interdisciplinary research (Scopus, WoS, and IEEE Xplore) relevant to the assessment of AI- related competencies The search strategy was systematically developed by integrating three primary conceptual components using the Boolean operator AND, ensuring that retrieved studies addressed the teacher population, constructs explicitly linked to artiﬁcial intelligence (e.g. “AI literacy,” “AI competence,” “AI knowledge,” or “AI skills”), and the use of measurement or assessment instruments concurrently. Within each component, the Boolean operator OR was employed to include relevant synonyms and alternative terminologies.

To ensure high precision and relevance, the search strategy prioritized exact phrase searches over broad keyword combinations. This approach ensured that retrieved studies explicitly conceptualized literacy or competence in direct relation to artiﬁcial intelligence, thereby reducing false positives where “AI” and “lit- eracy” might appear in unrelated contexts. Truncation symbols (e.g. the asterisk \*) were utilized in databases such as Scopus and WoS to capture morphological variations of keywords (e.g. literac\* capturing literacy and literacies). However, for IEEE Xplore, the syntax was adapted by replacing truncations with full logical vari- ations (e.g. singular and plural forms) to comply with the database’s wildcard limitations while preserving conceptual equivalence. The ﬁnalized search strings were applied primarily to the title, abstract, and keyword/metadata ﬁelds across all selected databases. ## _2.2. Eligibility criteria_

To ﬁlter the results from the initial search, a precise set of eligibility criteria was established. Regarding the population, studies were selected if they focused explicitly on preservice teachers, in-service teachers, or teacher mentors. In terms of scope, the review prioritized studies in which the core objective was the measurement or assessment of AI Literacy, encompassing related constructs such as AI competency, knowl- edge, or skills, using speciﬁc instruments such as questionnaires, tests, or performance tasks. Crucially, the selected studies had to be empirical. To be included, the study needed to report on the development, vali- dation, or adaptation of an AI literacy instrument for teachers. This inclusive criterion captured studies that developed new measures, translated or adapted existing scales for speciﬁc educational contexts, and con- ducted assessment studies that provided speciﬁc psychometric data. Regarding publication characteristics, eligible records were restricted to peer-reviewed journal articles and proceedings that provided su cient instrument detail, published in English between January 1, 2019, and August 31, 2025.

Conversely, several categories of literature were excluded to maintain the speciﬁc focus on psychometric quality. Most critically, we excluded studies that utilized existing instruments solely for data collection

## _2.3. Study selection and screening_

The study selection process was managed using Mendeley for reference organization and the Rayyan web application (Ouzzani et al., 2016) for systematic screening. Following the initial retrieval, all records retrieved from the four databases were imported into Mendeley reference management software. The “Check for Duplicates’ feature within Mendeley was systematically used to identify, review, and merge all duplicate entries.

After this deduplication process, the resulting library of unique records was exported in RIS format and uploaded to Rayyan to facilitate the blind screening process. A two-stage screening was conducted by two independent reviewers. In the ﬁrst stage, the reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts using Rayyan’s blind mode to minimize bias. Decisions (“Include”, “Exclude”, or “Uncertain”) were recorded, and disagreements were resolved through a consensus meeting. To quantify the consistency of the screen- ing decisions, inter-rater agreement was assessed using both percentage agreement and Cohen’s kappa coe cient (Cohen, 1960). In the second stage, the full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and assessed against the criteria detailed in Section 2.2.

## _2.5. Quality appraisal_

To systematically evaluate the psychometric quality of the identiﬁed instruments, a bespoke appraisal frame- work was operationalized. Although the COSMIN checklist (Mokkink et al., 2018; Prinsen et al., 2018) is widely recognized as the gold standard for evaluating health measurement instruments, this review takes a di erent path by adopting a hybrid approach. Given that educational constructs di er ontologically from clinical symptoms, we utilized the criteria proposed by Terwee et al. (2007). These criteria were calibrated against the Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (American Educational Research Association et al., [2014)](#disclosure-statement) while retaining the rigorous conceptual domains deﬁned by COSMIN, such as structural validity and internal consistency. This integration allows for rigorous psychometric evaluation while remaining rel- evant to educational measurement contexts.

Rather than calculating a composite numerical score that might obscure speciﬁc deﬁcits like high reliability masking poor validity, this review employed a qualitative tiered grading system. Based on the reported empirical data, each domain was rated as Grade A (High/Strong Evidence), Grade B (Adequate/ Limited Evidence), or Grade C (Low/No Evidence). This approach allows for a domain-level visualization of each instrument’s strengths and limitations without reducing complex psychometric properties to a single number. To ensure that grading decisions are anchored in explicit, predeﬁned methodological thresholds rather than holistic impressions of study quality, the complete set of decision rules is formalized in the Decision Matrix (Table 3).

The evaluation focused on four core psychometric domains. Content Validity was assessed by examining the rigor of the item generation process, speciﬁcally whether studies provided quantitative evidence of expert agreement or relied solely on qualitative review. Structural Validity was evaluated by reviewing evi- dence supporting the instrument’s internal structure, including conﬁrmatory, exploratory, and latent-vari- able modeling approaches. Internal Consistency was analyzed by examining reported summary reliability metrics at both the overall scale and subscale levels. Finally, Fairness was scrutinized by determining whether studies provided evidence that the instrument functioned equivalently across relevant demo- graphic subgroups, through procedures such as measurement invariance testing or di erential item func- tioning (DIF) analysis.

The central design principle of this framework is its method-agnostic orientation. The included studies employ di erent analytical traditions, including covariance-based approaches (CB-SEM/CFA), variance- based approaches (PLS-SEM), and item response modeling (IRT/Rasch), each of which relies on distinct ﬁt statistics that are not directly comparable. For instance, CFA studies report global model ﬁt indices such as CFI and RMSEA, PLS-SEM studies report indicator-level metrics including outer loadings and Average Var- iance Extracted, and Rasch or IRT studies report item-level ﬁt statistics such as Inﬁt and Outﬁt MNSQ. A direct comparison of these metrics across traditions would therefore be misleading. The grading process instead adopted an intra-tradition benchmarking approach, in which each study was evaluated against the accepted thresholds within its own methodological paradigm. Established reference standards guided these evalu- ations, speciﬁcally Hu and Bentler (1999) for CFA, Hair et al. (2019) for PLS-SEM, and Bond et al. ([2021)](#disclosure-statement) for Rasch/IRT. Several procedural rules governed the application of this framework to ensure consistency across border- line and ambiguous cases. First, grading decisions were based exclusively on reported evidence. Where a study did not report a particular analysis, the absence was interpreted as a lack of evidence rather than as evidence of absence, and grades were assigned solely based on what was documented in the primary study. Second, when reported evidence only partially met the criteria for a given grade, the appraisal defaulted to the lower tier. A CFA model in which some ﬁt indices reached the Grade A threshold while others fell short, for example, was assigned Grade B rather than Grade A. The same conservative logic applied when psychometric evidence was reported inconsistently, such as discrepancies between narrative text and tabulated results, or when it was incomplete, such as reporting overall reliability without subscale- level values. Third, studies that employed analytical methods not directly covered by the predeﬁned criteria received particular scrutiny. Where regression-based validation was used instead of factor analysis, for instance, the study was evaluated on whether the reported evidence functionally addressed the psycho- metric property in question. The grade was then adjusted downward to re ect the reduced analytical rigor. All borderline decisions were agged during the grading phase and documented with explicit justiﬁ- cations to maintain an auditable record of the appraisal process.

# 3. Results

## _3.1. Study selection results_

The systematic search across four electronic databases yielded 923 records. Due to signiﬁcant content overlap between these databases, the deduplication process using Mendeley identiﬁed and removed 560 duplicate records. This left 363 unique records for the title and abstract screening phase. Conducted by two independent reviewers, this initial screening excluded 285 articles that were clearly irrelevant to the study’s scope based on their titles and abstracts. Subsequently, 78 full-text articles were retrieved and rig- orously assessed against the eligibility criteria detailed in Section 2.2. Inter-rater agreement was calculated across all 78 articles retrieved for full-text eligibility assessment. Agreement was substantial, with a percen- tage agreement of 88.5% (69 out of 78 articles) and a Cohen’s kappa coe cient of κ = .80 (95% CI \[.68, .92\]), according to the Landis and Koch (1977) benchmark. A total of nine articles required resolution through a consensus meeting before ﬁnal eligibility decisions were conﬁrmed. Most of these disagreements involved borderline cases in which one reviewer assigned an “Include” or “Exclude” decision. At the same time, the other designated the article as “Maybe”, rather than as a direct contradiction between “Include” and “Exclude” judgments. This pattern suggests that discrepancies were attributable to interpretive ambiguity at the eligibility boundary rather than systematic inconsistency in the application of the inclusion criteria. During this comprehensive review, 45 articles were excluded. The primary reason for exclusion was misalign- ment with the target construct, where 32 studies were removed for measuring unrelated concepts, such as general digital literacy or AI acceptance, rather than speciﬁc AI literacy competencies. Four studies were excluded because they focused on non-teacher populations. Regarding instrumentation, three studies were excluded because they involved the direct adoption of existing instruments without modiﬁcation or revalidation, thereby failing to contribute new psychometric evidence. Finally, six studies were excluded due to methodological and quality issues. Four of these were non-empirical works, such as conceptual papers or editorials, while the remaining two were excluded due to severe reporting inconsistencies that involved fundamental contradictions between the abstract and the full text, thereby precluding reliable data extraction. Consequently, a ﬁnal sample of 33 studies met all inclusion criteria and was retained for the narrative synthesis and quality appraisal. The complete study selection process is presented in the

### _3.3.2. Measured dimensions of AI literacy_ A thematic content analysis of the target constructs reveals that the measurement of teacher AI literacy is multidimensional, with notable variation in how the scope of AI technologies is conceptualized. A primary ﬁnding is that the measurement landscape remains overwhelmingly focused on General AI Literacy. The vast majority of the reviewed instruments (29 out of 33, 87.9%) conceptualized AI broadly, encompass- ing a wide range of technologies, including machine learning, robotics, intelligent tutoring systems, and algorithms. These instruments typically use terms such as “AI technologies” in generic ways (e.g. “I under- stand how AI works” or “I can use AI tools”), without distinguishing between predictive systems and genera- tive models.

In contrast, speciﬁc instrumentation for GenAI Literacy represents a nascent phenomenon. 4. **Discussion**

This systematic review set out to map the landscape of instrumentation for teacher AI literacy by synthesiz- ing evidence from 33 empirical studies. The ﬁndings reveal a nascent but rapidly accelerating ﬁeld of inquiry, with over 80% of the identiﬁed instruments emerging between 2024 and 2025. This rapid growth suggests a reactive academic response to the ubiquity of AI, where the pressure to produce immediate metrics may have prioritized speed over methodological diversity. A recurring pattern across the reviewed studies is what this review terms “methodological monotony,” in which the assessment landscape is overwhelmingly dominated by self-report measures at 93.9%, with very few objective knowledge tests. This heavy reliance on perception-based scaling raises a concern that the ﬁeld risks measuring perceived familiarity with AI rather than grounded pedagogical capability. Consequently, the current body of literature o ers a comprehensive view of teachers’ conﬁdence and perceived readiness, while largely leaving their actual competence unexamined.

The heavy reliance on self-report methodologies poses a substantial validity challenge for the ﬁeld, obscuring the critical distinction between perceived conﬁdence and veriﬁable capability. While instruments measuring perceptions provide valuable data on teacher disposition and anxiety, they are often poor pre- dictors of actual technical proﬁciency (Lai & Teng, 2011; Persky et al., 2020). This discrepancy is frequently attributed to the Dunning-Kruger e ect where individuals with limited knowledge tend to overestimate their competence due to a lack of metacognitive awareness (Kruger & Dunning, 1999). In the context of AI literacy, this cognitive bias creates an “illusion of competence,” leading teachers to report high conﬁdence based on surface-level familiarity with tools rather than a deep understanding of their underlying mechan- isms. This misconception is directly fueled by the highly intuitive design of modern GenAI platforms, which leads users to mistakenly equate their ability to generate simple outputs with technical comprehension (Izak et al., 2025). Such overconﬁdence becomes particularly problematic in a domain like AI, where conceptual understanding regarding model behavior, limitations, data biases, and interpretability is not directly obser- vable through self-perception alone. This vulnerability is not unique to AI literacy. It mirrors a well-documen- ted pattern in related ﬁelds such as digital and ICT literacy, where studies have consistently shown that teachers’ self-assessed competence signiﬁcantly exceeds their performance on objective tasks (Aesaert et al., [2017;](#disclosure-statement) Siddiq et al., 2016). The persistence of this perception-performance gap across adjacent technology domains suggests that the current measurement landscape for AI literacy may be repeating a known meth- odological limitation rather than drawing on lessons already documented in the broader educational measurement literature. This concern is further ampliﬁed by the reliance on single-method self-report designs, which are inherently susceptible to common method bias (Podsako et al., 2003).

Despite this risk, the review identiﬁed only two exceptions by Ding et al. (2024) and Schmidt and Happ (2024) that employed objective testing methods to verify factual knowledge. The remaining instruments used Likert scales, which inherently capture self-e cacy rather than mastery. This overreliance on subjective measures introduces an epistemic limitation. It might lead to positive attitudes or enthusiasm to be mistaken for actual competence, thereby in ating estimates of teachers’ readiness to engage meaningfully with AI technologies. Without triangulating these subjective ratings with performance-based metrics such as scen- ario-based tasks, knowledge tests, or diagnostic conceptual assessments, the current literature risks

con ating enthusiasm with expertise and o ering an overly optimistic portrayal of AI literacy levels among teachers. For instance, approaches such as scenario-based assignments that require teachers to evaluate AI- generated content for accuracy, or diagnostic probes that target understanding of model limitations such as hallucinations and biases, should also be considered. Such approaches move beyond perceived familiarity to assess what teachers actually know rather than what they believe they know.

Beyond the validity of response formats, the content analysis also reveals a signiﬁcant conceptual gap between the technologies teachers are currently using and the tools available to assess them. Although GenAI has fundamentally disrupted the educational landscape since late 2022, the measurement literature exhibits a distinct “conceptual lag”. The majority of instruments still conceptualize AI broadly by focusing on foundational knowledge of machine learning or robotics, re ecting the technological priorities of the past decade. This approach overlooks the paradigm shift toward generative models and suggests that many cur- rently available instruments lack ecological validity. They tend to assess teachers on theoretical concepts rather than the actual tools encountered in daily practice. As a result, key constructs are misaligned with the realities of classroom technology use, compromising the extent to which these instruments capture the competencies that truly matter for contemporary practice. This represents not merely a delay in instru- ment development but a fundamental mismatch in competency assessment. General AI literacy prioritizes theoretical understanding and acceptance, whereas GenAI demands active collaboration skills such as prompt engineering and the critical evaluation of synthetic content (Annapureddy et al., [2024;](#disclosure-statement) Chiu, Ahmad, Ismailov, et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). These competencies constitute procedural and judgment-based expertise that cannot be inferred solely from declarative knowledge. By relying on general metrics, the current literature implicitly assumes that knowing about AI equates to the ability to work with AI. This assumption is awed because it overlooks the unique risks of generative models, speciﬁ- cally hallucination and bias, leaving teachers assessed as “competent” by traditional standards yet unpre- pared for the actual GenAI tools that dominate schools today. Consequently, conventional measures risk producing misleading competence classiﬁcations, generating false positives that obscure genuine training needs.

Beyond methodological and technological considerations, the review also exposes a deeper conceptual challenge in how teacher AI literacy is deﬁned and operationalized across existing instruments. Seminal scholarship has consistently framed AI literacy as a multidimensional construct, encompassing foundational knowledge of AI systems, procedural competencies for meaningful use, and critical–ethical judgment in situ- ated contexts (Laupichler et al., 2022; Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021). For teachers speciﬁcally, AI literacy has been conceptualized as a professional competency that integrates pedagogical decision- making, re ective engagement with AI-supported outputs, and responsible instructional use (Adams et al., [2022;](#disclosure-statement) Salas-Pilco et al., 2022). However, despite this growing conceptual clarity in the theoretical litera- ture, the empirical measurement landscape remains highly fragmented. The 33 reviewed instruments vary substantially in construct labels, dimensional scope, and theoretical grounding, with measures alternately emphasizing awareness, self-e cacy, technical knowledge, ethical sensitivity, or professional engagement under similar terminological umbrellas. Importantly, this fragmentation should not be interpreted as an argument against context-sensitive or purpose-speciﬁc conceptualizations of AI literacy. Rather, it re ects a lack of explicit conceptual positioning when similar labels are used to operationalize substantively di erent constructs. Without clearer alignment between theoretical deﬁnitions and empirical operationali- zations, instruments purporting to assess “teacher AI literacy” may capture fundamentally di erent compe- tencies, limiting construct equivalence and hindering meaningful comparisons across studies. From a ﬁeld- building perspective, this disconnect complicates the development of cumulative knowledge. It weakens the interpretability of cross-study ﬁndings, risking the obscuration of the speciﬁc competencies that professional development and policy initiatives aim to target. Addressing this challenge requires not the imposition of a single deﬁnition, but greater transparency and theoretical coherence in how AI literacy constructs are speciﬁed, justiﬁed, and linked to measurement decisions.

Another pivotal trend identiﬁed is the consolidation of ethical awareness as a non-negotiable component of teacher AI literacy. The review found that 60.6% of the instruments explicitly integrated ethical dimensions ranging from data privacy to algorithmic fairness. This prevalence signals a shift in the conceptualization of the teacher’s role, from a passive operator of technology to an active mediator of ethical digital integration. More fundamentally, it re ects a broadening view of teachers as professionals responsible for navigating

trust, credibility, and legitimacy in AI-mediated knowledge production (Felix, 2021; Kamalov et al., 2023; Zag- kotas, 2025). However, a deeper analysis suggests that the current measurement of ethics is undergoing a necessary qualitative evolution. While earlier instruments focused primarily on compliance-based ethics such as adhering to privacy regulations or data protection laws, the emergence of GenAI has necessitated a “criti- cal turn” towards epistemic integrity. This shift extends ethical competence beyond rule-following toward evaluative judgment in contexts where AI outputs may be persuasive yet unreliable (Hagendor , 2024; Hamed et al., 2024). In an era where AI models can conﬁdently generate hallucinations and biased narratives, the competence to use AI is incomplete without the ability to critically evaluate its output. Recent instru- ments by Shi (2025) and Wang et al. (2025) re ect this by operationalizing ethics not just as adherence to rules but as the active capacity to evaluate risks and verify the veracity of synthetic content. This suggests that modern AI literacy is becoming closely linked with critical thinking, in that the educational value of an AI tool is determined not by its computational power but by the teacher’s ability to navigate its moral and factual limitations. Consequently, instruments that fail to capture this critical disposition risk construct underrepresentation, potentially legitimizing a teaching workforce that appears technically proﬁcient yet remains epistemically vulnerable in AI-rich classrooms.

Beyond issues of construct coverage and response format, the review also reveals a notable imbalance in the psychometric maturity of existing instruments. While researchers have successfully established internal consistency, as evidenced by consistently high reliability coe cients, the aspect of fairness remains largely unaddressed. This pattern re ects a disproportionate emphasis on score stability over interpretive validity, treating reliability as a su cient indicator of measurement quality rather than a necessary but incomplete condition (Kane, 2013; Messick, 1995). The appraisal results indicate that only a small fraction of studies con- ducted measurement invariance testing across demographic groups such as gender or teaching experience. In many cases, group comparisons were reported without examining whether the underlying constructs were measured equivalently across subpopulations. Few studies progressed beyond basic conﬁgural assumptions to empirically test metric or scalar invariance. As a result, the psychometric foundations required to support meaningful group-level inferences remain underdeveloped. This omission is methodo- logically signiﬁcant because it assumes that test items function identically for all participants without empiri- cal veriﬁcation. Such assumptions are particularly problematic in complex constructs like AI literacy, where prior exposure, professional roles, and contextual familiarity may systematically in uence how items are interpreted and responded to. Without evidence of measurement invariance, observed score di erences between groups may re ect artifacts of di erential item functioning or construct non-equivalence rather than true variation in teachers’ knowledge or skills (Byrne & Watkins, [2003;](#disclosure-statement) Milfont & Fischer, 2010; van de Schoot et al., 2012). From a measurement perspective, this imbalance signals a ﬁeld that has prioritized internal coherence over inferential rigor. High reliability coe cients, while necessary, may therefore mask deeper issues related to fairness and comparability, limiting the evidentiary strength of group-based con- clusions. This limitation suggests that the ﬁeld remains in an intermediate stage of psychometric develop- ment, characterized by technically sound but inferentially fragile instruments. It underscores the need to move beyond reliability-focused validation toward a more comprehensive framework of measurement quality. Taken together, these patterns re ect a ﬁeld in rapid transition, where methodological choices appear to be driven by urgency rather than by established consensus.

5. **Implications**

The ﬁndings of this review have important implications for the governance of teacher education and the accreditation of digital competencies. The pervasive reliance on self-report measures suggests that current estimates of teacher AI literacy may overrepresent perceived readiness while o ering limited insight into demonstrated competence. For educational policymakers and school leaders, this implies that existing data on teacher preparedness should be interpreted with caution, particularly when informing decisions related to certiﬁcation, resource allocation, or large-scale professional development initiatives. When used in isolation, perception-based metrics may obscure gaps in operational safety and ethical judg- ment, underscoring the need to complement self-report data with more objective assessment.

To address this gap, future instrument development should prioritize capturing teachers’ demonstrated competencies in authentic contexts. Scenario-based assessment tasks could present teachers with realistic 

INTERACTIVE LEARNING ENVIRONMENTS 23

classroom situations that require them to evaluate AI-generated content for accuracy and bias, or to identify hallucinated outputs before integrating them into lesson materials. Rubric-scored performance assessments could evaluate prompt engineering proﬁciency, with scoring criteria grounded in established prompt design frameworks. Diagnostic conceptual assessments, modeled on concept inventories in science education, could probe common misconceptions about AI mechanisms and provide a more granular mapping of knowledge gaps than Likert-based self-ratings permit. Such instruments would address the methodological monotony identiﬁed in this review while enabling researchers and policymakers to distinguish between surface-level familiarity and the deeper operational literacy required for responsible AI integration in teach- ing practice.

From an instructional and curricular perspective, the conceptual lag between general AI tools and the rea- lities of GenAI integration underscores the need to rethink how AI literacy is deﬁned and assessed in teacher education. As assessment tools struggle to keep pace with rapid technological change, anchoring AI literacy too closely to speciﬁc applications risks premature obsolescence. Instead, curriculum developers may need to place greater emphasis on enduring cognitive and epistemic competencies. Capacities such as epistemic vigilance, critical evaluation of AI-generated content, and ethical decision-making are more likely to remain relevant across evolving AI systems than instrument items tied to particular tools or platforms.

The limited application of measurement invariance testing also raises equity-related concerns for the broader adoption of AI literacy instruments across diverse educational contexts. The transfer of instruments validated in technologically dominant or homogeneous settings into other systems, without su cient evi- dence of fairness, may introduce systematic measurement bias. In practical terms, this means that instru- ments used to evaluate teachers across di erent demographic groups, such as those varying in gender, years of teaching experience, or geographic and cultural context, may yield scores that are not directly com- parable. If such instruments inform high-stakes decisions such as the targeting of professional development resources, the identiﬁcation of training needs, or the benchmarking of teacher readiness across regions, undetected bias could result in the systematic misallocation of support. Teachers from underrepresented or contextually distinct backgrounds may be inaccurately proﬁled as less competent, or their genuine train- ing needs may go unrecognized, depending on the direction and nature of the bias. Addressing these con- cerns requires greater attention to fairness-related analyses to ensure that AI literacy assessments support inclusive and responsible implementation across educational systems.

6. **Limitations**

The present review acknowledges several limitations that shape the interpretation of its ﬁndings and delimit the scope of the conclusions that can be drawn. First, the review was restricted to empirical studies pub- lished in English and indexed within four selected electronic databases. Although these databases provide broad coverage of education and technology research, this criterion may have excluded relevant measurement instruments developed and validated in non-English contexts. Given the substantial contri- butions to AI education research from regions such as East Asia and Europe, it is possible that robust instru- ments published in local languages were not captured. Consequently, the ﬁndings should not be interpreted as an exhaustive representation of global instrument development.

This limitation warrants particular attention given the geographic distribution of the included studies. China and Turkey collectively accounted for nearly half of all reviewed instruments (n = 16, 48.5%), yet these were captured exclusively through English-language publications. Given the scale and maturity of edu- cational technology research in both countries, it is highly probable that a substantial body of instrument development work exists in Mandarin and Turkish that was not captured by the present search strategy. The geographic concentration observed in this review may therefore paradoxically understate the full extent of these regions’ contributions to the global measurement landscape. Moreover, culturally speciﬁc approaches to operationalizing AI literacy, potentially re ecting distinct educational philosophies or curricu- lar priorities, may have been systematically excluded. Future reviews incorporating multilingual search strat- egies across Chinese- and Turkish-language databases would provide a more accurate representation of global instrument development. They may reveal measurement approaches that are not yet evident in the English-language literature. Separately, the search strategy’s use of exact phrase matching, while e ective at reducing false positives, may limit recall by excluding studies that operationalize AI-related 

24 M. A. ZAINAL ET AL.

competencies under alternative terminology not captured by the predeﬁned search strings. Future reviews could mitigate this by supplementing database searches with citation tracking or broader free-text queries.

Second, the exclusion of gray literature, including doctoral dissertations and conference proceedings, rep- resents a further limitation. As a rapidly evolving ﬁeld, AI literacy research may involve innovative measure- ment approaches that remain at the developmental or pre-publication stage. The omission of such sources may therefore bias the review toward more established instruments, limiting insight into emerging measure- ment trends. Expanding future reviews to include gray literature could help capture earlier-stage instrument development and methodological experimentation.

Third, the conclusions drawn regarding the characteristics of existing AI literacy instruments are inher- ently constrained by the nature of the studies included in the review. The predominance of self-report measures within the reviewed literature restricts the extent to which this review can make claims about tea- chers’ actual technical or procedural competence. As a result, the synthesis primarily re ects how AI literacy has been conceptualized and measured, rather than how it is enacted in practice. Current conclusions should accordingly be interpreted within the bounds of available evidence.

Fourth, the identiﬁcation of a conceptual lag between general AI measurement and the realities of GenAI integration is based on instruments available within the review period. Given the pace of technological change, it is likely that newer instruments are currently under development and have not yet been pub- lished. Accordingly, the review captures a snapshot of measurement practices at a particular moment in time rather than a deﬁnitive account. This temporal limitation suggests that the ﬁndings should be revisited as the ﬁeld continues to evolve.

Finally, the geographic concentration of included studies in speciﬁc regions limits the generalizability of the review’s conclusions across diverse educational contexts. The limited use of measurement invariance testing further constrains the extent to which cross-group comparisons can be meaningfully interpreted. As such, the review cannot determine whether observed di erences in AI literacy scores across contexts re ect true variations in competence or artifacts of measurement. The equity-related implications of this gap are discussed in Section 5. Taken together, these limitations suggest that the review should be read as a systematic mapping of prevailing measurement patterns rather than a deﬁnitive evaluation of all exist- ing AI literacy instruments.

7. **Conclusion**

This systematic review has critically examined the state of instrumentation for teacher AI literacy and reveals a ﬁeld that is rapidly expanding in volume yet uneven in methodological maturity. While scholarly attention to teachers’ preparedness for AI integration has increased substantially, the instruments currently employed remain only partially aligned with the complexities of contemporary educational technologies. The predo- minance of self-report measures suggests that existing evidence largely re ects teachers’ perceived conﬁ- dence, o ering limited insight into their demonstrated technical, pedagogical, and ethical competence. This imbalance raises the possibility that high self-e cacy scores may obscure important gaps in readiness, particularly regarding emerging forms of AI use. The review further highlights a persistent conceptual lag in which assessment practices continue to emphasize general AI knowledge, despite the growing prominence of generative technologies in educational contexts. As a result, teachers are frequently assessed on abstract or foundational understandings rather than on competencies required to critically engage with generative systems, including the evaluation of AI-generated outputs and informed instructional decision-making. At the same time, the increasing inclusion of ethical dimensions in AI literacy instruments represents a positive development. However, the limited application of fairness-related analyses limits the interpretability of ﬁndings across diverse populations. Taken together, these patterns point to a deeper issue of conceptual fragmentation, in which instruments using similar labels operationalize substantively di erent constructs of teacher AI literacy. This lack of convergence complicates cross-study comparability and weakens the cumulative development of knowledge in the ﬁeld. Addressing these challenges will require a more coher- ent and robust approach to measuring teacher AI literacy, including greater conceptual clarity, stronger attention to fairness, and assessment strategies that extend beyond perception-based surveys to methods that capture how teachers meaningfully and responsibly engage with AI in practice. Such e orts are essential to ensure that the integration of AI in education is supported not only by technological
