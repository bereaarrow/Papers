# 1. Introduction

Artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems that simulate human intelligence or outperform humans in speciﬁc tasks. It encompasses a broad range of technologies, including natural language proces- sing, image recognition, decision-making, and pattern analysis (Russell & Norvig, 2021). AI technologies, which have rapidly become integrated into daily life through applications such as navigation systems, grammar-checking software, and autocomplete features on mobile devices (Pretorius, 2023), have become one of the main drivers of profound transformations at both the individual and institutional levels over the past decade (Semerci Şahin et al., 2025). Education is among the ﬁelds in which the transfor- mative impact of AI has been felt most strongly. AI-supported systems have the potential to substantially reduce teachers’ workload by personalizing learning environments, automating assessment and evaluation, providing feedback, and facilitating classroom management (Coşgun, [2025;](#disclosure-statement) Miao et al., 2021). Since 2022, the widespread use of generative artiﬁcial intelligence (GenAI) tools such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and Microsoft Copilot has accelerated this transformation. Teachers have increasingly used these tools for lesson planning, content development, and personalized feedback (Huang & Zhang, 2025; Shi, 2025). GenAI refers to systems that can generate human-like content, including text, images, audio, and code, through deep learning models. As such, it creates new competency demands not only at the technical level but also in pedagogical and ethical terms.

This transformation is fundamentally redeﬁning teachers’ roles. Teachers are no longer seen merely as knowledge transmitters; they are increasingly expected to design, manage, and critically evaluate AI-sup- ported learning environments (Miao et al., 2021). This new positioning requires teachers not only to use AI tools technically but also to evaluate their pedagogical appropriateness and ethical implications (UNESCO, 2024). In addition to the opportunities o ered by AI, the emergence of risks such as algorithmic bias, data privacy, academic integrity, and digital inequality (Jongkind et al., 2025) makes it essential for tea- chers to develop a critical awareness of these technologies. In this context, two key concepts stand out in the literature: AI literacy and AI competency (Chiu, [2026)](#disclosure-statement). While literacy involves understanding information and

AI com- petency, by contrast, concerns the translation of this knowledge and awareness into practice; it focuses on the extent to which individuals can use AI tools e ectively, e ciently, and purposefully, while integratively encompassing technical, pedagogical, and ethical dimensions (Chiu et al., [2024)](#disclosure-statement).

Research on teachers’ AI literacy and GenAI competency is still emerging and contains important gaps. The comprehensive review conducted by Sperling et al. (2024) reveals that although AI literacy has attracted growing global research attention, it has not been adequately addressed in teacher education. It has been reported that teachers experience various di culties in integrating AI tools into classroom settings and need greater professional development support in this process (Kandır & Temel, 2025; Su et al., 2023). In the Turkish context, however, valid and reliable Turkish-language scales that jointly address primary school and preschool teachers and comprehensively assess both AI literacy and GenAI competency remain limited (Erdoğan & Ekşioğlu, [2024)](#disclosure-statement). Because education systems, instructional approaches, and attitudes toward technology vary across cultures, measurement tools developed in one context should be psychome- trically tested before being used in another. Such validation is methodologically essential (Velander et al., 2024).

Recent theoretical developments further emphasize that AI literacy and competency should not be con- sidered as isolated cognitive constructs but as integral components of interactive learning environments. In particular, the Human-Centric Artiﬁcial Intelligence Pedagogy (HCAP) framework (Chiu, [2026)](#disclosure-statement) extends the TPACK model by explicitly integrating AI literacy and AI competency into pedagogical design, highlighting the dynamic interaction among teachers, students, and AI systems. Within this framework, teachers are con- ceptualized not only as users of AI tools but as designers of human–AI collaborative learning environments. Similarly, the comprehensive framework proposed by Chiu et al. ([2024)](#disclosure-statement) conceptualizes AI literacy and com- petency as multidimensional constructs that encompass not only understanding and use, but also inter- action, collaboration, and ethical decision-making in real-world contexts. These perspectives suggest that teachers’ AI-related competencies should be interpreted in relation to how they structure and manage AI- supported interactions in classroom settings, including feedback processes, adaptive learning environments, and student engagement with AI tools.

## _2.2. Teachers’ GenAI competencies_

AI competency refers to the integrated knowledge, skills, conﬁdence, and capacity that enable individuals to understand the functioning and social impacts of AI technologies, use them ethically and responsibly, and communicate and collaborate e ectively with AI systems (Chiu et al., [2024)](#disclosure-statement). Its main distinction from literacy is that competency foregrounds the transformation of knowledge into action: individuals not only under- stand AI, but also use it in a context-appropriate, critical, and purposeful manner; moreover, they can ques- tion their own patterns of use (Chiu et al., [2024)](#disclosure-statement). Within this framework, GenAI competency emerges as a subdimension of this broader construct. GenAI encompasses systems that, through deep learning architec- tures, can generate human-like outputs such as text, images, audio, video, code, and simulations based on existing data and content (Lim et al., 2023). The capacities of this technology for autonomous content gen- eration, interactive dialogue, and creative production make its use in educational settings both appealing and complex. This complexity necessitates that teachers develop specialized pedagogical, technical, and ethical competencies speciﬁc to GenAI, beyond general AI literacy (Shi, 2025; Wu et al., 2025).

Strengthening teachers’ GenAI competencies serves a critical function for both the quality of instructional processes and students’ competencies with these technologies. Experimental evidence in the literature con- sistently supports this relationship. Yang and Markauskaite (2025) demonstrated that tasks requiring inter- action with GenAI signiﬁcantly improved students’ knowledge and skills related to these technologies. Likewise, in a participatory action research study conducted with graduate students, Aladsani ([2025)](#disclosure-statement) docu- mented that following a structured intervention program, participants showed signiﬁcant improvements in technical knowledge, ethical use, critical thinking, lifelong learning, and interpersonal skills. In the context of teacher education, Moorhouse et al. (2024) found that pre-service teachers’ professional GenAI competen- cies were strengthened in a course environment grounded in open educational practices. However, the lit- erature does not present only positive ﬁndings; some studies report that students experience marked di culties in generating content with GenAI tools and have serious concerns, particularly regarding ethical issues such as academic integrity and data privacy (Jongkind et al., 2025).

Empirically mapping teachers’ GenAI competency proﬁles is critical for both concretizing conceptual debates in the literature and designing education policies in an evidence-based manner. The large-scale scoping review conducted by Huang and Zhang (2025), covering 35 studies, revealed that GenAI makes

concrete contributions to teachers’ professional development, instructional performance, and work e ciency. At the same time, however, the review documented structural problems, including insu cient technological infrastructure, deﬁciencies in teacher competencies, limited training opportunities, unresolved ethical dilemmas, and uncertainties surrounding human–AI interaction. These challenges indicate the impor- tance of prioritizing GenAI integration and strengthening teachers’ pedagogical and digital competencies, rather than merely providing technical opportunities (Seven & Erümit, 2025). Measuring teachers’ current levels of GenAI competency using valid and reliable instruments will provide a basis for designing needs- based professional development programs, inform conceptual debates in the literature with empirical data, and o er an objective foundation for policy decisions regarding AI integration in education (Shi, 2025).

The distinction between AI literacy and AI competency should also be considered in relation to the cog- nitive and behavioral consequences of AI use. Emerging constructs such as AI dependency, AI addiction, and fear of being without artiﬁcial intelligence highlight the extent to which individuals rely on AI systems for cognitive and academic tasks (Özbay, 2026). From a cognitive perspective, this tendency is closely associated with the concept of cognitive o oading, whereby individuals delegate memory, problem-solving, and eva- luative processes to external systems such as AI. While such processes may enhance e ciency and perform- ance, they may also reduce deep cognitive engagement and critical thinking when overused (Chiu et al., [2024;](#disclosure-statement) Ng et al., 2021). In this respect, AI literacy and AI competency should not be conceptualized solely as enabling constructs that support e ective use, but also as regulatory capacities that help individuals cri- tically manage their interaction with AI systems.

## _2.3. Current study_

The growing integration of AI technologies into educational settings requires teachers not only to use these tools technically, but also to evaluate them pedagogically, ethically, and critically. This transformation has led to a redeﬁnition of teachers’ roles, shifting them from individuals who transmit knowledge to professionals who design, manage, and evaluate AI-supported learning environments. In this context, AI literacy, which encompasses teachers’ knowledge, skills, and awareness regarding AI, and GenAI competencies, which refer to the capacity to e ectively integrate this knowledge into instructional processes, emerge as key com- ponents of digital transformation in education (Erdoğan & Ekşioğlu, [2024)](#disclosure-statement). However, a review of the literature indicates that, at the national level, valid and reliable measurement instruments that comprehensively assess teachers’ AI literacy and generative AI competencies remain limited. Most existing studies have focused on students, pre-service teachers, or mixed samples, leaving in-service teachers underrepresented (Arslankara & Usta, [2024;](#disclosure-statement) Balıkçı & Yıldız Durak, [2025;](#disclosure-statement) Çelebi et al., [2023;](#disclosure-statement) Gökçe Tekin, [2025;](#disclosure-statement) Polatgil & Güler, 2023; Topal et al., 2025; Uluğ et al., 2025).

However, recent evidence from Türkiye indicates that AI-related scale development and adaptation studies have begun to diversify across di erent constructs and participant groups. For instance, Semerci Şahin et al. (2025) developed and validated a scale measuring secondary school students’ awareness of gen- erative artiﬁcial intelligence in terms of basic knowledge, positive attitudes, and concerns about its impacts. Similarly, Köse et al. (2025) adapted the Artiﬁcial Intelligence Attitude Scale (AIAS-4) into Turkish with a uni- versity student sample, thereby contributing a concise instrument for assessing general attitudes toward AI. In a related vein, Agaoglu et al. ([2025)](#disclosure-statement) adapted the Artiﬁcial Intelligence Ethics Scale into Turkish for nursing students, extending the national literature toward the ethical dimensions of AI. More recently, Uyar et al. (2026) developed an Artiﬁcial Intelligence Dependency Scale for educators, focusing on teachers’ and aca- demics’ dependency on AI in academic and educational processes. Taken together, these studies demon- strate that AI-related measurement research in Türkiye is expanding; however, this expansion remains fragmented across constructs such as awareness, attitudes, ethics, and dependency, and is concentrated largely on students, university populations, or broad educator samples rather than on in-service preschool and primary school teachers speciﬁcally. Therefore, validated tools that directly assess teachers’ AI literacy and generative AI competencies remain needed in the Turkish context.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to adapt into Turkish the AI Literacy Scale for Teachers (AILST; Ning et al., 2025), developed to measure teachers’ AI literacy, and the Teachers’ GenAI Competencies Scale (T- GAIC; Shi, 2025), which assesses teachers’ pedagogical, technical, and ethical competencies regarding GenAI, and to examine their psychometric properties. Considering these two scales together will allow for

### _3.2.2. Teachers’ GenAI competencies scale (T-GAIC)_

In this study, the T-GAIC (Shi, 2025) was used to assess teachers’ competencies in integrating GenAI technol- ogies into educational processes across pedagogical, technical, and ethical dimensions. The ﬁnal form of the scale consists of 36 items and ﬁve subdimensions: Technological Proﬁciency of GenAI (TP), Pedagogical Com- patibility of GenAI in Teaching (PC), Preparing Students with E ective Practices of GenAI (PS), GenAI-Related Professional Development and Communication (GPD), and Risk and Ethical Awareness of GenAI in Education (REA). The scale uses a 5-point Likert-type rating format (1 = Strongly disagree, 5 = Strongly agree), and higher scores indicate higher levels of teachers’ GenAI competencies. The EFA and CFA results conﬁrmed the ﬁve-factor structure of the scale, and the model ﬁt indices were acceptable (RMSEA = .078, CFI = .946, TLI = .934). For the original form of the scale, the internal consistency coe cient was quite high, with Cron- bach’s α reported as .98, and the reliability coe cients of the subdimensions ranging from .90 to .96.

## _3.3. Procedure_

The data were collected online via Google Forms. The data collection form consisted of three sections: (1) an information form on demographic variables, (2) the AILST, and (3) the T-GAIC. The form link was distributed to teachers across di erent provinces via social media and professional communication networks. In the initial stage, 452 forms were obtained. The only excluded form was removed because it contained systematic missing data in the demographic information section. The data collection process was completed over approximately four weeks. The study was approved by the Scientiﬁc Research and Publication Ethics Com- mittee of Nevşehır Hacı Bektaş Velı Unıversıyt University (Decision Nos: 2025.11.397; 2025.11.398, Date: 01.12.2025). Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants were informed in advance of the study’s purpose and scope. All participants provided informed consent. The data were used solely for research pur- poses, and participant conﬁdentiality was maintained throughout the study.

Before data collection, formal permission to adapt both scales into Turkish was obtained from the original authors. The adaptation process followed standard translation and cultural adaptation procedures. First, the items were translated from English into Turkish independently by two bilingual experts familiar with edu- cational terminology. The translated versions were then compared and reconciled into a preliminary Turkish form. In the next step, this form was back-translated into English by two independent bilingual experts who had not participated in the ﬁrst translation stage. The back-translated versions were compared with the original forms to evaluate semantic consistency and conceptual equivalence. Following the trans- lation stage, the preliminary Turkish versions were reviewed by an expert panel consisting of specialists in educational measurement, curriculum and instruction, early childhood/primary education, and educational technologies. Based on expert feedback, several wording revisions were made to improve clarity, cultural appropriateness, and conceptual relevance. The revised forms were then piloted with a small group of tea- chers to assess item comprehensibility and linguistic clarity before the main administration.

## _3.4. Data analysis_

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was not conducted in the present study, as both scales had previously estab- lished, theoretically grounded factor structures, supported by EFA and CFA in their original development studies (Ning et al., 2025; Shi, 2025). Therefore, conﬁrmatory factor analysis (CFA) was preferred to test 

INTERACTIVE LEARNING ENVIRONMENTS 7

whether the original factor structures could be replicated in the Turkish context. This approach is consistent with recommendations for scale adaptation studies when the underlying structure is well-deﬁned. Data analysis proceeded in two stages. In the ﬁrst stage, descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, arithmetic mean, and standard deviation, were calculated. In the second stage, CFA was conducted to test the construct validity of both scales. To assess the potential risk of common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted separately for both scales. For the T-GAIC and AILST, the ﬁrst factor accounted for 49.4% and 43.2% of the total variance, respectively, which is below the commonly accepted threshold of 50%, suggesting that common method bias is unlikely to be a serious concern for these scales.

The CFA analyses were carried out using the lavaan R package (Rosseel, 2012) via the SEMLj module in Jamovi 2.7 (Gallucci & Jentschke, [2021)](#disclosure-statement), with maximum likelihood (ML) as the estimation method. Model ﬁt was evaluated based on the _χ_2 statistic, Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). The threshold values adopted for acceptable ﬁt were CFI ≥ .90, TLI ≥ .90, RMSEA ≤ .08, and SRMR ≤ .08 (Hu & Bentler, [1999;](#disclosure-statement) Kline, 2016). For the T-GAIC data, the assumption of multivariate normality was violated (Mardia skewness _z_ = 34478, _p_ \< .001; kurtosis _z_ = 190, _p_ \< .001); this ﬁnding was taken into account when evaluating the reliability of the parameter estimates. Since the study was conducted within a single national sample, measurement invariance across cultural groups could not be tested.

# 5. Discussion

This study adapted two scales measuring teachers’ AI literacy and GenAI competencies into Turkish and exam- ined their psychometric properties. First, for the AILST, the tested four-factor model yielded acceptable ﬁt indices. These values indicate that the model ﬁts the data in line with the recommended threshold values and support the construct validity of the measurement model (Hu & Bentler, [1999; ](#disclosure-statement)Kline, 2016). Similar ﬁndings were also reported by the original developers of the scale, Ning et al. (2025); in the original study, the four-factor structure – AI-P, AI-A, AI-E, and AI-K – was conﬁrmed through CFA, and the goodness-of-ﬁt indices were reported to fall within acceptable limits. When the ﬁt values obtained during the adaptation process are compared with those of the original scale, the structure appears to retain its validity in the Turkish teacher sample. The non-signiﬁcant loading of item E2 may be attributed to its conceptual ambiguity and its potential sensitivity to cultural interpretations of AI trust. A similar issue was also reported in the original study, where certain items within the ethics dimension were removed due to theoretical inconsistency (Ning et al., 2025). Despite its low loading, the item was retained in the present study to preserve the conceptual cover- age of unconditional trust in AI, which is considered an important component of ethical awareness.

Similarly, the ﬁt indices obtained for the T-GAIC also indicate that the model generally demonstrated a good ﬁt. The fact that the RMSEA value was slightly above the threshold may be considered acceptable given the model’s complex structure (Browne & Cudeck, [1993)](#disclosure-statement). This ﬁnding is consistent with the values reported by Shi (2025), the developer of the original form of the T-GAIC. In both studies, the ﬁve-factor struc- ture – TP, PC, PS, GPD, and REA – was conﬁrmed, and all item loadings remained above the .50 threshold, presenting a consistent picture regarding construct validity. Although the RMSEA value (.084) slightly exceeds the conventional threshold of .08, it remains within the range considered acceptable for complex models with large numbers of parameters (Kline, 2016). Given that other ﬁt indices such as CFI (.928), TLI (.922), and SRMR (.039) indicate good model ﬁt, the overall model can be considered acceptable. This interpretation is consistent with the literature, which suggests that RMSEA should not be evaluated in iso- lation but rather in conjunction with other ﬁt indices.

Item-level analyses showed that most factor loadings across both scales were high and statistically signiﬁ- cant (Kalaycı, 2016; Sharma, 2016). In the original AILST study, it was also reported that the factor loadings met the .50 threshold, composite reliability (CR) values ranged from .891 to .914, and Cronbach’s alpha coe - cients ranged from .851 to .943 (Ning et al., 2025). The similarity between the internal consistency coe cients obtained in the present study and those reported for the original AILST suggests that the Turkish form retains satisfactory reliability. However, the fact that some items (e.g. U1 and E2) showed low or non-signiﬁ- cant loadings suggests that their contribution to the measurement model may be limited. Indeed, during the original development process, Ning et al. (2025) had to remove items A1, A2, E2, and E6 from the scale on the grounds of inconsistency with the theoretical framework; this suggests that the functionality of some items may need to be re-examined during cultural adaptation. In the T-GAIC, by contrast, it is noteworthy that the factor loadings ranged from .65 to .96. In the original T-GAIC study, Shi (2025) reported that the standardized item loadings ranged between .52 and .82.

When evaluated in terms of reliability, it is highly meaningful that the total Cronbach’s alpha value of the T-GAIC overlaps with the value reported in the original T-GAIC study (_α_ \> .90) and that the subdimension coe cients (TP = .90; PC = .97; PS = .97; GPD = .97; REA = .95) remain at a high level. Similarly, the reliability coe cients of the AILST also present a consistent picture when compared with the dimension-based values of the original AILST (AI-P α = .909; AI-A _α_ = .943; AI-K _α_ = .931; AI-E _α_ = .851). In terms of convergent validity, the AVE values calculated for the AILST exceeded the .50 threshold, consistent with the original AILST

ﬁndings; Ning et al. (2025) reported AVE values ranging from .508 to .545. For the T-GAIC, Shi (2025) reported that the AVE values for all ﬁve factors remained above .50 and that the composite reliability coe cients (CR) were measured as TP = .913, PC = .952, PS = .953, GPD = .948, and REA = .898, which is consistent with the values obtained in the present study.

When the relationships among the factors were examined, the associations among the AILST dimensions were found to be in the expected direction and at the expected level. In particular, the high positive relation- ship between awareness and usage skill (_r_ = .851) and the strong positive relationship between AI-P and AI-A (_r_ = .906) indicate that the cognitive and a ective components of AI literacy are closely interrelated. In the original AILST study, it was also reported that the correlation coe cients among the four subdimensions ranged from .167 to .444, and that all _p_-values were below .001 (Ning et al., 2025). Taken together, these ﬁndings indicate that AI literacy is not limited to knowledge alone but rather represents an integrated con- struct encompassing attitudes, anxiety, and usage skills (Chiu et al., [2024;](#disclosure-statement) Long & Magerko, 2020; Satorra & Bentler, 1994). The very high correlation observed between some factors (e.g. _r_ = .962 between PC and PS) may raise concerns regarding discriminant validity. Such high correlations suggest that these dimensions may not be entirely distinct and could re ect a higher-order construct. This ﬁnding may be explained by the fact that teachers tend to perceive pedagogical integration and student-oriented application of GenAI as closely intertwined processes. In line with this interpretation, previous studies have also conceptualized GenAI competencies as a holistic and integrated structure (Shi, 2025).

The high inter-factor correlations obtained within the scope of the T-GAIC (_r_ = .798–.962) indicate that GenAI competencies converge under a holistic higher-order structure. In the original T-GAIC study, inter- factor correlation coe cients were likewise reported to range from .240 to .574; however, all AV values (.724–.860) exceeded the intercorrelations, thereby providing evidence of strong discriminant validity (Shi, 2025). The very high inter-factor correlations obtained in the present study may suggest that the Turkish sample perceives these competency dimensions in a more integrated manner; indeed, cultural context plays a determining role in shaping perceptions of and attitudes toward AI (Velander et al., 2024). In particu- lar, the very strong relationship between practical application and the dimensions of ethical and conscious use (_r_ = .962) demonstrates that the use of generative AI is not conﬁned to technical skills alone but is also intertwined with ethical awareness and responsible use. This ﬁnding is consistent with theoretical models proposing that GenAI competencies constitute a multilayered structure encompassing technical, pedagogi- cal, and ethical dimensions together (UNESCO, 2024).

Beyond conﬁrming the overall factor structures, the ﬁndings also point to several issues that are theoreti- cally and practically signiﬁcant. The weaker performance of some items suggests that not all components of the original scales transfer equally well across cultural contexts. In particular, items related to unconditional trust in AI or self-reported mastery of speciﬁc applications may be interpreted di erently by Turkish teachers depending on their professional identity, technological experience, and concerns about the role of AI in teaching. The strong clustering of several competency dimensions may re ect the way teachers encounter AI in practice. Rather than separating technical, pedagogical, and ethical aspects, teachers may experience these domains as interdependent components of a single professional challenge.

Although this study focuses on teachers’ self-reported AI literacy and GenAI competencies, these con- structs gain their full meaning within interactive learning environments where teachers, students, and AI systems dynamically interact. From this perspective, higher levels of AI literacy and competency may enable teachers to design more adaptive, responsive, and student-centered learning environments sup- ported by AI tools. In line with the HCAP framework (Chiu, [2026)](#disclosure-statement), teachers’ competencies can be interpreted as foundational elements shaping teacher–student–AI interactions, such as facilitating dialogue with AI systems, guiding students’ critical engagement with AI-generated content, and orchestrating collaborative problem-solving processes. Similarly, AI-supported environments such as intelligent tutoring systems, gen- erative AI-based content creation tools, and adaptive learning platforms require teachers to actively mediate interactions rather than passively use technology.

## _5.2. Limitations and future research_

Although this study makes an important contribution by adapting the AILST and T-GAIC into Turkish, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study employed a cross-sectional survey design, which limits 

12 A. EROL ET AL.

the ability to examine temporal changes in teachers’ AI literacy and GenAI competencies and therefore restricts causal interpretation (Creswell, [2014)](#disclosure-statement). Future longitudinal studies could provide a more nuanced under- standing of how these competencies develop over time and in relation to professional experience and techno- logical exposure. In addition, because the data were collected through self-report measures, the ﬁndings may have been in uenced by subjective evaluation bias. The sample was limited to primary school and preschool teachers working in Türkiye. This restricts the generalizability of the ﬁndings to other teacher groups, edu- cational levels, subject areas, and cultural contexts. Moreover, participants were recruited through convenience sampling, which, although practical in reaching a relatively large number of teachers, may have introduced sampling bias and reduced the representativeness of the sample. The participants may not fully re ect the diver- sity of teachers in Türkiye in terms of regional distribution, institutional context, or access to technological resources. For this reason, the ﬁndings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should employ more representative sampling strategies, such as stratiﬁed or random sampling.

Several limitations relate speciﬁcally to the adaptation process and the psychometric interpretation of the scales. Measurement invariance was not tested because the study relied on a single-group sample; therefore, the results provide evidence of construct validity within the Turkish context rather than full cross-cultural equivalence. Future studies should examine conﬁgural, metric, and scalar invariance across di erent teacher subgroups and cultural settings. In addition, the weaker performance of some items suggests that not all components of the original scales may transfer equally well across contexts. In particular, items related to unconditional trust in AI or self-reported mastery of speciﬁc applications may carry di erent meanings depending on teachers’ professional identity, technological experience, and perceptions of AI’s role in education. This indicates that scale adaptation should be considered not only a linguistic process but also a conceptual and contextual one. Another issue concerns the strong clustering observed among some competency dimensions. Rather than re ecting clearly separated domains, these patterns may indicate that teachers experience technical, pedagogical, and ethical aspects of AI as highly interrelated components of a broader professional challenge. This may be particularly likely in contexts where structured AI-related professional development is still limited. Future studies should therefore examine discriminant validity more rigorously and explore whether higher-order or alternative measurement models provide a better representation of these constructs.

Taken together, these limitations also point to important directions for future research and practice. Further validation studies involving di erent educational levels, pre-service teachers, and international samples would strengthen the generalizability of the scales. At the practical level, the ﬁndings suggest that teacher education and in-service training should address AI literacy and GenAI competency in a more integrated way. These areas should not be approached solely as matters of technical proﬁciency; they also involve critical evaluation, ethical awareness, pedagogical decision-making, and re ective use. In this respect, the adapted scales may serve not only as measurement tools but also as diagnostic instruments for identifying teachers’ professional development needs.

# 6. Conclusion

The results obtained for the AILST indicate that the four-factor structure of the scale was conﬁrmed and that the model yielded acceptable ﬁt values. The generally signiﬁcant and adequate factor loadings indicate that the items represent their intended constructs well. However, the fact that some items displayed low or non- signiﬁcant loading values suggests that the scale may be further improved in future studies. When the direc- tion and magnitude of the relationships among the factors are examined, it becomes clear that AI literacy represents a multidimensional construct integrating cognitive, a ective, and behavioral components. The ﬁndings obtained for the T-GAIC, on the other hand, show that the ﬁve-factor structure of the scale was strongly conﬁrmed. The high factor loadings and very strong internal consistency coe cients indicate that the scale has substantial measurement power.
