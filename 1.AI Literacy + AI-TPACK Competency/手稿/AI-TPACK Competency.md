**The Impact of <a name="ole_link3"></a><a name="ole_link5"></a>AI Literacy on AI-TPACK Competency: The Mediating Role of AI-Assisted Creativity and AI Self-Efficacy**

**Abstract**

This study aims to address the significant research gap in understanding the relationship between AI Literacy** and AI-TPACK Competency in K-12 schools. While both concepts have gained increasing attention, their interconnections remain unexplored. Leveraging the UTAUT-TPACK and AI-TPACK models, this study investigated how AI Literacy, mediated by AI-Assisted Creativity and AI Self-Efficacy, influences English teachers’ ability to integrate AI into their classrooms effectively. The findings of this study will provide valuable insights into the factors that promote effective AI integration in English education, ultimately contributing to the development of strategies to enhance teacher preparedness and student learning outcomes in the age of AI.

**Keywords**: AI Literacy; AI-TPACK Competency; AI-Assisted Creativity; AI Self-Efficacy; Intelligence; AI Teaching

**1. Introduction**

<a name="_toc371077877"></a>As an emerging field of computer science, artificial intelligence (AI) has profoundly impacted education (Celik, 2023; Montebello, 2018). Recently, there has been growing interest in exploring the interactions between people and AI and AI education systems. <a name="_hlk182076296"></a>Nonetheless, research on AI in education has focused on the technical aspects of AI development (An et al., 2023; Divekar et al., 2022), with little attention paid to the factors that shape its use in English education settings (An et al., 2023; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019). Also, there exists a scarce number of studies that drive the use of AI technologies into educational theories <a name="_hlk181793444"></a>and teacher AI teaching (Su et al., 2023). However, as Lindner and Berges (2020) claimed, in the ﬁeld of AI, there is a dearth of investigations focusing on teacher AI teaching.

The AI shift has generated challenges and opportunities for <a name="_hlk182745997"></a>AI teaching (Ding et al., 2024; Pan & Wang, 2025; Wijaya et al., 2024). Although AI has great potential to facilitate learning, the integration of new technologies into classroom teaching generally requires teachers to overcome multilayered barriers (Al-Abdullatif & Alsubaie, 2024; An et al., 2023; Tsai & Chai, 2012). Some challenges in introducing AI into education include teachers’ lack of AI knowledge and skills, low confidence, lack of curriculum design, and lack of teaching guidelines (Su et al., 2023; Su, 2024; L. Zhao et al., 2022). However, research has shown that some teachers have low AI-TPACK competency levels in AI teaching environments (Chan & Tang, 2024; Fan et al., 2025). 45% of teachers experience challenges and stress when integrating AI technology that they have encountered or expect to experience in the near future (Ding et al., 2024). A crucial aspect of this issue is teachers’ lack of AI literacy and AI-TPACK competency in effectively integrating AI tools into classrooms (Crompton & Burke, 2023; Ding et al., 2024).

First, although the potential for practicing AI teaching has grown as a result of AI, there is a notable lack of both theoretical and empirical research on teacher AI Literacy (Lu et al., 2024; Pan & Wang, 2025; Wu et al., 2024). To date, the exploration of teachers’ AI Literacy in integrating AI for instructional guidance has been limited (Lu et al., 2024), and teachers have received inadequate training in AI Literacy (Deshen et al., 2026). Few empirical studies have systematically examined the multidimensional analysis of language teachers’ AI literacy. This lack of theoretically grounded measurement methods hinders the ability to assess teachers’ AI-assisted creativity and AI self-efficacy, as well as to explore the relationship between AI literacy and AI-TPACK competency (Nabhan & Habók, 2026).

Second, although interest in <a name="ole_link1"></a>AI Literacy and AI integration in education is increasing, the precise relationship between AI Literacy and AI-TPACK competency remains largely unexplored (Kaya et al., 2025). Moreover, despite extensive documentation on the significance of teaching AI to teachers, empirical studies on how AI Literacy affects AI-TPACK competency remain scarce, particularly in establishing direct connections (Al-Abdullatif, 2024; Almatrafi et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2023; Su et al., 2023). <a name="_hlk182857383"></a>Furthermore, while these aspects have been studied individually, few studies have integrated AI literacy, AI-TPACK competency, AI-Assisted Creativity and AI self-efficacy into a research framework. This integration is vital for gaining a thorough understanding of effective AI teaching practices (Wu et al., 2024).

Although Taiwan’s Ministry of Education has actively promoted the Digital Learning Enhancement Plan and incorporated generative AI into national digital learning platforms for primary and secondary schools, systematic AI curricula, teacher training resources, and empirical evidence on teachers’ AI-related competencies in Taiwanese elementary, junior high, and senior high schools remain scarce (Wong et al., 2020; H.-G. Zhao et al., 2024). Most existing AI literacy and AI-TPACK studies conducted in Taiwan have focused on university students or pre-service teachers, leaving a notable gap regarding how in-service English teachers in K-12 settings perceive, develop, and apply AI literacy in their daily instruction.

<a name="_hlk182857069"></a>To address the identified research gaps, this study investigates the impact of AI Literacy (Independent Variable) on AI-TPACK Competency (Dependent Variable) through the mediating effects of AI-Assisted Creativity and AI Self-Efficacy (Mediator Variables) in a mediated-effects research model. Based on the UTAUT-TPACK Model and AI-TPACK Model, this study aims to bridge these gaps by investigating the interrelationships between these constructs.

**2. <a name="_hlk182304335"></a>Literature Review and Hypothesis**

<a name="_hlk180926700"></a>*2.1. AI Literacy<a name="_hlk181610681"></a> (AIL)*

The increasing incorporation of AI into teaching and learning environments has sparked interest in AI literacy (Al-Abdullatif & Alsubaie, 2024; Wang et al., 2023). One approach to AI education is to improve AI literacy (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Chung et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the effective classroom integration of AI demands that student be AI-literate, encompassing understanding, utilization, monitoring, and critical reflection on AI applications in teaching (Ding et al., 2024).

AI literacy is a newly emerging concept that seeks to define, teach, and evaluate the knowledge and skills related to AI. It refers to an individual's ability to comprehend, utilize, monitor, and critically evaluate AI applications and AI-generated content, even without programming knowledge or the ability to develop AI models (Long & Magerko, 2020; Wu et al., 2024).

The term “AI literacy” was first coined by Burgsteiner et al. (2016) and Kandlhofer et al. (2016), who described the competencies required to understand the basic knowledge and concepts of AI and to use AI technologies. In addition, Long and Magerko (2020) defined it as a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate, communicate, and collaborate effectively with AI and use it as a tool, online, at home, and in the workplace. Furthermore, Ng et al. (2021b, 2021a) added AI to every student’s AI literacy in work settings and everyday life and proposed it as a fundamental skill for everyone, not just computer scientists. He incorporated the model of Bloom’s Taxonomy, Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK), and AI concepts, practices, and perspectives into the instructional design of AI literacy education (Ng et al., 2021b, 2021a).

Carolus et al. (2023) stated that AI literacy covers “competencies needed to interact with AI technology in a self-determined and rational manner.” Pinski and Benlian (2023) provided a definition of General AI literacy, describing it ashumans “socio-technical competence consisting of knowledge regarding human and AI actors in human-AI interaction, knowledge of the AI process steps, that is, input, processing, and output, and experience in AI interaction.” Laupichler et al. (2023) define the concept of AI literacy as “competencies that include basic knowledge and analytical evaluation of AI, in addition to the critical use of AI applications by ordinary non-expert individuals.” They did not include (programming skills) in their definitions because they considered them a separate set of competencies beyond AI literacy (Laupichler et al., 2023).

Recently, several studies have developed AI literacy frameworks for education (Kong et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2021b; Wong et al., 2020). AI literacy does not require detailed technical expertise in AI applications. However, it spans four dimensions: understanding and comprehension of AI, practical application of AI, assessment of AI applications, and ethical considerations of AI (L. Zhao et al., 2022).

Long and Magerko (2020) provided a conceptual framework of AI literacy composed of five overarching themes derived from the literature (Long & Magerko, 2020).Wang et al. (2023) proposed a theoretical framework to conceptualize AI Literacy, comprising four constructs: Usage, defined as the ability to exploit AI technology to proficiently perform tasks; Awareness, the ability to identify and comprehend AI systems during interactions; Evaluation, the capacity to critically analyze and assess AI applications and their outcomes; and Ethics, which involves recognizing responsibilities and risks associated with using AI systems. 

*2.2. <a name="_hlk180937265"></a>AI-TPACK Competency (AITC)*

<a name="_hlk182167715"></a><a name="_hlk181117029"></a>Teachers’ AI-TPACK competency plays a significant role in nurturing AI literacy (Velander et al., 2024). Recently, Celik (2023) developed an AI-TPACK framework to facilitate the integration of AI-based applications into educators’ teaching practice. In essence, the AI-TPACK framework serves as a tool for evaluating teachers’ competence in utilizing appropriate AI tools to effectively achieve educational objectives within a specific domain while also aligning with pedagogical strategies. 

In this study, AI-TPACK Competency is defined as teachers’ professional knowledge and capability to ethically and effectively select, evaluate, and integrate AI-based tools with their pedagogical strategies and subject-matter content knowledge in order to design, implement, and assess AI-supported teaching and learning activities (Celik, 2023). This integrated knowledge base encompasses not only technical familiarity with AI applications but also the pedagogical judgment required to align AI affordances with instructional goals, subject content (e.g., English language teaching), and the ethical considerations of using AI-generated outputs in the K-12 classroom.

The AI-TPACK framework consists of five fundamental components: AI technological knowledge (AI-TK), AI pedagogical content knowledge (AI-PCK), AI technological content knowledge (AI-TCK), AI technological pedagogical knowledge <a name="_hlk182249927"></a>(AI-TPK<a name="_hlk182249941"></a>), and AI technological pedagogical content knowledge<a name="_hlk182249912"></a>(AI-TPACK) (Celik, 2023).

AI-TK <a name="_hlk182128577"></a>encompasses teachers’ awareness and understanding of AI-based applications, their ability to interact with these applications, and their proficiency in utilizing their basic functions. AI-TK tackles knowledge to interact with AI-based tools and uses their fundamental function. This component aimed to measure teachers’ familiarity with the technical capacities of AI-based tools<a name="_hlk181432451"></a> (Celik, 2023; Ning et al., 2024). AI-PCK encompasses the knowledge required to select appropriate teaching methods and strategies designed to suit specific instructional content, including the ability to reconfigure and present information to improve pedagogical outcomes <a name="_hlk182294585"></a>(An et al., 2023; Celik, 2023).

AI-TCK <a name="_hlk182250302"></a>requires teachers to utilize AI-based applications to enhance their expertise in their respective fields. AI-TCK focuses on knowledge of field-specific AI-based tools. This measure assesses the extent to which teachers use AI-based tools to update their content knowledge. This component also addresses teachers’ understanding of particular technologies that are more suitable for addressing subject matter learning in their fields <a name="_hlk182294597"></a>(An et al., 2023; Celik, 2023). <a name="_hlk182168461"></a>AI-TPK encompasses teachers’ awareness of the pedagogical potential inherent in artificial intelligence applications, including the ability to monitor students, interpret messages, and deliver immediate feedback <a name="_hlk182168794"></a>(Schmid et al., 2020). The AI-TPK addresses the pedagogical affordances of AI-based tools, such as personal and timely feedback and monitoring students’ learning processes. In addition, the AI-TPK measures teachers’ understanding of alerting (or notification) and interpretation of messages using AI-based tools (Celik, 2023; Hava & Babayiğit, 2025).

<a name="_hlk181708217"></a>AI-ТРАСK is a combination of AI-TK, AI-TCK, and AI-TPK. AI-TPACK refers to knowledge for teaching with <a name="_hlk182244458"></a>AI technology, which requires an understanding of how AI technologies can support teaching subject matter (Schmid et al., 2020). AI-TPACK is regarded as a core knowledge domain in the TPACK framework. It measures teachers’ professional knowledge to select and use suitable AI-based tools (e.g., intelligent tutoring systems) to implement a teaching strategy (e.g., monitoring and timely feedback) to accomplish instructional goals in a specific domain. This knowledge enables them to perform teaching tasks independently or collaboratively alongside human educators (Celik, 2023).

AI-TPACK represents a nuanced and specialized form of knowledge that emerges from the intersection of three distinct areas: disciplinary knowledge (content expertise), pedagogical knowledge (teaching methods and strategies), and artificial intelligence technology knowledge (An et al., 2023; Celik, 2023). This perspective underscores the importance of integrating AI technology not only as a supplementary tool but also as an integral component of the teaching and learning processes (An et al., 2023).

*2.3. AI-Assisted Creativity (AIAC)*

<a name="_hlk225775580"></a>Mok et al. (2025) define AI-assisted creativity as the innovative ability of teachers to collaborate with AI systems to jointly produce outputs that are both original and valuable. AI-assisted creativity occurs when AI serves as a partner collaborating with individuals to generate creative works or as an assistant to help individuals enhance their creativity in various ways (Mok et al., 2025). By leveraging AI-assisted creativity, teachers can integrate human and artificial intelligence to facilitate creative teaching, seamlessly connecting vast amounts of human data and analyses to supplement human intelligence. This allows both to play to their respective strengths, thereby creating high-quality instructional content (Al‐Rousan et al., 2025).

AI-assisted creativity refers to a collaborative process in which human creativity is enhanced through interaction with artificial intelligence systems. However, existing research on AI-supported education remains limited by conceptual ambiguity, a predominant focus on tool usage rather than cognitive mechanisms, and a lack of well-established mediating models (Mok et al., 2025). In particular, the role of AI-assisted creativity in teacher education contexts, such as its integration with AI-TPACK frameworks, remains underexplored (Al‐Rousan et al., 2025).

*2.4. <a name="_hlk181117082"></a>AI Self-Efficacy (AISE)*

AI self-efficacy refers to confidence and individual perception of the ability to employ AI effectively to achieve personal and professional goals (Morales-García et al., 2024). This includes not only the technical handling of AI-based tools but also the ability to integrate these tools to solve specific challenges, adapt to changes, and overcome difficulties through the innovative use of AI (Morales-García et al., 2024).

AISE is more than one’s judgment of one’s own capability to apply technological skills; it is a self-assessment of the specific individual skills involved in using AI technologies or products. Accordingly, based on previous technology-related self-efficacy research, the AISE can be defined as an individual’s general belief in their ability to use and interact with AI. AISE can be viewed as a holistic perception or belief in multiple elements of the school environment. Furthermore, in this research context, AISE concentrates on the variable itself rather than the model evaluation procedure or response, which facilitates the processing of AISE as a single variable independent of various predecessors or consequences (Bewersdorff et al., 2025).

*2.5. The UTAUT-TPACK Model and AI-TPACK Model*

This study’s theory is rooted in two fundamental approaches to teachers’ knowledge: <a name="_hlk225762654"></a><a name="_hlk182248198"></a>the UTAUT-TPACK theory model by Tang et al. (2024) and the AI-TPACK theory model by Celik (2023). AI-TPACK underscores the significance of teachers’ knowledge and skills in utilizing technology to enrich teaching and learning experiences. The UTAUT-TPACK theory model is a technology acceptance model proposed to explain teachers’ AI teaching behaviors (Tang et al., 2024). 

UTAUT-TPACK has been used to study the effects of people’s perceptions of technology on their behavioral intentions to use technology (Tang et al., 2024). In contrast, AI-TPACK studies assess teachers’ knowledge of technology integration with teaching (Zhou et al., 2017), which has been regarded as an important supplement to UTAUT-TPACK (Lim & Harwati, 2021). The UTAUT-TPACK accounts for usability and usefulness factors pertaining to technology from users’ perspectives, whereas the AI-TPACK framework accounts for teachers’ knowledge and design expertise required for technology integration in teaching. The UTAUT-TPACK model is widely used because its explanatory power for user behavior is nearly 70%, which is far higher than that of other technology acceptance models (Tang et al., 2023, 2024).

The UTAUT-TPACK theory emphasizes four key constructs: 1) <a name="_hlk182240683"></a>performance expectancy (AI-TPACK competency), 2) <a name="_hlk182240707"></a>effort expectancy (AI self-efficacy), 3) <a name="_hlk182240745"></a>social influence (AI Literacy), and 4) facilitating conditions (AI-Assisted Creativity) <a name="_hlk182247596"></a>(An et al., 2023; Celik, 2023). Performance expectancy refers to an individual’s belief regarding the extent to which a system will enhance their job performance. Effort expectancy denotes the degree of ease associated with using a system to complete tasks. Social Influence refers to the degree to which an individual perceives that important others believe he or she should use the new system, and Facilitating Conditions refer to the degree to which an individual believes that an organizational and technical infrastructure exists to support the use of the system <a name="_hlk182239277"></a>(Venkatesh et al., 2003).

UTAUT-TPACK is typically used to study users’ behavioral intentions to use new technology and related factors <a name="_hlk182237152"></a>(An et al., 2023). This study adopted the UTAUT-TPACK model as the theoretical basis for external factors influencing teachers’ AI literacy and AI-Assisted Creativity and AI-TPACK model as the theoretical basis for teachers’ internal factors related to AI-TPACK competency and AI self-efficacy. The UTAUT-TPACK model provides the main influential factors of technology usage behavior; therefore, it may be used to explain and predict teachers’ AI teaching behavior (Tang et al., 2023). According to UTAUT-TPACK and AI-TPACK, <a name="_hlk182240670"></a>AI Literacy (corresponding to social influence in UTAUT-TPACK) has a significant impact on teachers’ <a name="_hlk182239417"></a>AI-TPACK competency <a name="_hlk182240697"></a>(corresponding to performance expectancy in UTAUT-TPACK) through the mediation of AI-Assisted Creativity (corresponding to facilitating conditions in UTAUT-TPACK) and AI self-efficacy (corresponding to effort expectancy in UTAUT-TPACK) (An et al., 2023; Celik, 2023).

Teachers’ AI Literacy can be viewed as an internalized social influence that pushes them to adopt and apply AI technology in their teaching. The impact of teachers practicing AI Literacy promotes AI-TPACK competency by utilizing AI resources and influences AI-Assisted Creativity and AI Self-Efficacy <a name="_hlk225786844"></a>(Celik et al., 2026; Kaya et al., 2025). Teachers with higher AI-TPACK competency and stronger AI Literacy may be more confident in their ability to use AI tools effectively, thus increasing their performance and effort expectations for AI technology. AI literacy is seen as a “facilitating condition.” AI literacy directly impacts teachers’ perceptions of technology, which, in turn, affects performance and effort expectations (An et al., 2023; Celik, 2023).

<a name="_hlk182676356"></a>*2.6. The Relationship Between AI Literacy and AI-TPACK Competency: The Mediating Role of AI-Assisted Creativity and AI Self-Efficacy*

<a name="_hlk181987411"></a><a name="_hlk225791160"></a><a name="_hlk182060256"></a><a name="_hlk180949425"></a><a name="_hlk182313351"></a>First, teacher AI literacy is a crucial factor affecting teachers’ growth in AI-TPACK competency (Chiu et al., 2024; Younis, 2024). Recent studies have <a name="_hlk182045251"></a>highlighted the positive role of AI Literacy as a crucial aspect of AI-TPACK competency, especially in the educational domain (Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021b; Schiavo et al., 2024). Teacher development of AI literacy aspects is positively associated with AI-TPACK competencies (Hava & Babayiğit, 2025; Ning et al., 2024; Velander et al., 2024). The literature suggests that teacher AI Literacy is closely related to teacher AI-TPACK competency <a name="_hlk181901743"></a>(Chiu et al., 2024; Ding et al., 2024; L. Zhao et al., 2022), with AI literacy being a significant predictor of AI-TPACK competencies (Hava & Babayiğit, 2025).

Al-Abdullatif (2024) highlighted a direct and robust relationship between AI literacy and the AI-TPACK competency. Similarly, Kabakci Yurdakul (2018) identified significant positive correlations between teachers’ AI-TPACK competencies and their levels of AI Literacy. This study also found that AI Literacy significantly predicted AI-TPACK competency. <a name="_hlk181895711"></a>Milutinović (2022) observed that AI Literacy had a substantial impact on the intentions of prospective teachers to incorporate technology in their instructional process, particularly concerning their perceived usefulness and ease of use of technology. A study conducted in China found that the degree of AI Literacy significantly influenced teachers’ AI self-efficacy in technology education (Hava & Babayiğit, 2025; Huang, 2024). <a name="_hlk182158078"></a>This reflects the significant influence of different levels of AI literacy on the AI-TPACK competency (Ng et al., 2021b; Wijaya et al., 2024).

Second, AI literacy was a strong predictor of teachers’ AI-TPACK competency and AI self-efficacy. Empirical research indicates that AI literacy serves as a predictor of AI-TPACK (Gökbulut, 2026). Higher levels of AI literacy facilitate a greater understanding of the advantages and capabilities of AI, which in turn promotes a positive attitude towards AI-TPACK competency and AI self-efficacy in educational settings (Al-Abdullatif, 2024; Ng et al., 2021b; Wang et al., 2023). Sanusi et al. (2022) assert the significance of AI literacy in achieving AI-TPACK competency, highlighting that AI self-efficacy reflects an individual’s self-assessment of their AI literacy an AI-TPACK competency to engage in activities necessary to confront future teaching challenges.

AI literacy is related to AI self-efficacy (Hornberger et al., 2023; Mansoor et al., 2024). Some researchers have linked AI literacy to AI self-efficacy (Almatrafi et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026).<a name="_hlk181979257"></a> Ng et al. (2023) stated that AI literacy is correlated with AI self-efficacy. AI technology has become an integral part of education, and AI-TPACK competency impacts all aspects of teaching and learning, including enhancing teachers’ AI self-efficacy (Celik et al., 2026; Kaya et al., 2025). AI self-efficacy captures the various AI-TPACK competencies required to interact with human intelligence under different conditions(Yuan et al., 2024). AI-TPACK competency aims to assess teachers’ AI self-efficacy, which is necessary for the integration of AI into their pedagogical work (Lintner, 2024).

<a name="_hlk182676754"></a>Third, AI literacy is essential for AI-assisted creativity, as it influences and facilitates the evaluation of AI-assisted creativity (Su, 2024; Wu et al., 2024). Additionally, AI self-efficacy significantly predicts AI-assisted creativity(El-Sayed et al., 2025). AI literacy also leads to significant improvements in AI self-efficacy and AI-assisted creativity (Kong et al., 2024). Furthermore, AI literacy has a positive impact on AI-assisted creativity, which in turn positively influences AI self-efficacy, ultimately benefiting AI education (Chun et al., 2025). Previous research findings also indicate that AI self-efficacy and AI-assisted creativity play complementary and partial mediating roles between AI literacy and AI-TPACK competency (Ayanwale, 2024; Thakur et al., 2026; Uluğ et al., 2025). Thus, we hypothesize the following:

*H1*. <a name="ole_link4"></a><a name="_hlk182060517"></a>AI Literacy has a positive effect on AI-TPACK Competency.

*H2*. AI Literacy has a positive effect on AI-Assisted Creativity.

*H3*. AI Literacy has a positive effect on AI Self-Efficacy.

*H4*. <a name="_hlk182060486"></a>AI-Assisted Creativity has a positive effect on <a name="_hlk182060566"></a>AI-TPACK Competency.

<a name="_hlk182678905"></a>*H5*. <a name="_hlk182060464"></a>AI Self-Efficacy has a positive effect on AI-TPACK Competency.

*H6*. AI-Assisted Creativity has a positive effect on AI Self-Efficacy.

H7. AI-Assisted Creativity mediates the effect of AI Literacy on AI Self-Efficacy.

H8. AI-Assisted Creativity mediates the effect of AI Literacy on AI-TPACK Competency.

H9. AI Self-Efficacy mediates the effect of AI Literacy on <a name="_hlk182677112"></a>AI-TPACK Competency.

Based on the <a name="_hlk182236094"></a>theory of UTAUT-TPACK and the AI-TPACK model, this study introduces the independent variable (AI Literacy), mediating variables (AI-Assisted Creativity and AI Self-Efficacy), and dependent variables (<a name="_hlk182158501"></a>AI-TPACK Competency) to propose a research model of the impact of AI Literacy on AI-TPACK Competency. **3. Methodology**

*3.1 Sample*

Hancock and Feng’s (2026) nmax calculator was applied to evaluate whether the sample size was sufficient for SEM. Assuming a minimum standardized path coefficient of γ = 0.20 and moderate multicollinearity among predictors (ρc = 0.30), the required minimum sample size is 213.

*3.2. Demographics*

A total of X English teachers from K-12 schools in various cities in Taiwan willingly participated in this study.

<a name="_toc888"></a><a name="_toc17957"></a><a name="_toc684762611"></a><a name="_toc27837"></a>*3.3. Measures*

To measure the level of teachers’ AI literacy, Zhao et al.’s (2022) scale was AI literacy. The scale included 20 items that sought to collect information on four separate dimensions of teachers’ AI literacy: Knowing and Understanding AI (KUAI), Applying AI (AAI), Evaluating AI Application (EAIA), and AI Ethics<a name="ole_link2"></a> (AIE).

This study employed the AI-TPACK scale developed by <a name="_hlk180933120"></a>Celik (2023) to assess teachers’ competency in integrating AI applications into the teaching process. The scale comprises 27 items evaluated on a 7-point Likert scale and is divided into five subscales: AI-TK, AI-TCK, AI-TPK, AI-TPACK, and Ethics.

To measure teachers’ levels of AI-assisted creativity, we adopted the AI-assisted Creativity Scale developed by Mok et al. (2025). The questionnaire consists of 16 items designed to quantify creative potential in AI-assisted activities (Mok et al., 2025).

<a name="_hlk182145480"></a>This study used the scale developed by Chiu et al. (2024) to assess AI Self-Efficacy. The scale considers teachers’ comprehension of AI, engagement in learning about AI, and ability to use AI for educational purposes. The scale consists of six dimensions: AI knowledge, AI pedagogy, AI evaluation, AI ethics, Human-centered education, and professional engagement. The scale is suitable for K-12 teachers and may also be applicable to higher-education instructors.

*3.5. Data Analysis Procedure*

This study will employ a structural equation modeling (SEM) approach using RStudio (version 2026.07.1) to analyze the data, including the measurement model, structural model, and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA).

First, the variables will be examined for outliers using z-values. To describe the data, we will utilize the “psych” and “dplyr” packages (Revelle, 2026; Wickham et al., 2014). For the main analysis, which involves Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), we will employ the “lavaan” package and the “semPlot” package (Epskamp, 2015; Revelle, 2026). The Lavaan package will be used to define the measurement model and to conduct CFA and the main effects SEM model, while the Modsem package will be used to test any moderated (interaction) effects specified in the model (Rosseel, 2012; Slupphaug et al., 2 Model fits will be evaluated with the robust comparative fit index (CFI), robust root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR).

CFA will be conducted with initial data collected from teachers to assess the structural or factorial validity of the Questionnaire. CFA is chosen because of its unique ability to test whether the hypothesized factor structure adequately represents the relationships between latent constructs and their observed indicators in pre-defined, theory-derived models (Tavakol & Wetzel, 2020). CFA will be conducted from the lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012). Before conducting CFA, data screening procedures will be conducted, including the examination of missing data, outliers, and univariate and multivariate normality.

SEM will be conducted through the following steps: model specification, model identification, model estimation, model testing, and model modification (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). The model will be specified and identified based on the conceptual framework outlined in the literature section. A bootstrapping method with robust maximum likelihood estimation will be used for model estimation. Robust maximum likelihood estimation with bootstrapped standard errors was selected, rather than PLS-SEM, because this combination maintains stable and unbiased parameter estimates even at moderate sample sizes, without the interpretive limitations associated with PLS-based composite modeling (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). Initially, the measurement model estimations will be examined to verify whether all factor loadings are significant. Subsequently, model fit indices will be examined. We will use Chi-square, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR as fit indices. For a good model fit, CFI and TLI values should be 0.90 or above, while RMSEA and SRMR values should be below 0.08 (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). The mediation analysis using structural equation modeling (SEM) with the lavaan package in RStudio will be utilized to examine the direct, indirect, and total effects. After evaluating the path coefficients, the results will be reported.
