ABSTRACT
Generative artificial intelligence (GAI) is quickly changing the foundations of design education. It alters how creativity is under -
stood, created and judged in higher education. This issue is especially important in design fields, where creativity involves not 
just productivity and fast idea generation but also the ability to handle uncertainty, engage in reflective experimentation, and 
think independently. In this context, the current study examined the relationships among teachers' acceptance of AI, their AI lit-
eracy, students' motivation to learn, and creativity in AI-  supported design education at Chinese universities. The research gath -
ered data from randomly sampled teachers and students at 10 universities known for their art and design programmes, which 
include visual communication, industrial design, digital media, animation and environmental design. This study contributes to 
the growing research on AI in design education by shifting the focus from celebrating technological advances to a more critical 
perspective on how humans interact with AI in creative contexts. The findings suggest that AI can both enhance and restrict 
creativity, depending on how it affects students' independence, cognitive engagement, and motivation. Thus, the future of design 
education involving AI should not only focus on technological complexity or adoption rates, but also on its ability to maintain 
human originality, reflective thought, and the uncertainty that is vital to creativity.
1   |   Introduction
The rise of generative artificial intelligence (GAI) has sparked 
a major change in higher education. It has reshaped teaching 
methods, creative processes and the ways knowledge is taught 
and learned. As a result, higher education institutions are not 
just adding digital technologies to existing teaching models; 
they are rethinking learning environments around AI-  driven 
interaction, personalized experiences and collaboration be -
tween humans and machines. This shift aligns with the goals of 
Education 4.0 and the skills needed in the 21st century (Wang 
et  al.  2023; Almogren et  al.  2024; Wu and Zhang  2025; Hao 
et  al.  2026). In this context, GAI not only enhances academic 
performance but also promotes deeper learning outcomes such 
as creativity, engagement, self-  confidence, innovation, emo -
tional resilience, happiness and motivation. These outcomes are 
vital for developing today's workforce (Lin and Chen 2024; Chen 
et al.  2025; Chen  2025; Wang et al.  2026; Oruç et al.  2026).
The rapid growth of research into GAI in education shows 
a rising awareness that future learning environments need 
students to go beyond simply absorbing information. They 
require skills like analytical reasoning, creative problem-  
solving, interdisciplinary thinking and adaptive innovation. 
New evidence suggests that AI-  supported learning settings 
can provide personalized feedback, generate dynamic con -
tent, offer immersive design experiences and encourage self-  
directed learning. These features can strengthen students'

motivation and creative engagement across higher educa -

Niharikha Shree et al.  2025). Additionally, AI-  enhanced ed -

creative thinking, in-  class focus, innovative behaviour and 
digital confidence (Rong et al.  2022 ; Hwang and Wu  2025; Ji 
et  al.  2025; Hao et  al.  2026). More importantly, recent stud -

ogy, especially in fields where idea generation, visualiza -

(Zhang et al.  2025; Herath et al.  2026 ; Ge and Shin  2026).
However, the increasing research on AI acceptance and 
knowledge reveals several important gaps. First, many studies 
mainly focus on students' views and intentions regarding AI, 
while less attention has been given to how teachers perceive 
AI acceptance and knowledge within specific academic fields 
(Börekci and Çelik  2024 ; Khoso et al.  2025; Wang et al.  2025). 
Second, while frameworks for technology acceptance, such 
as TAM, UTAUT and TPACK, have provided useful insights 
into AI adoption behaviours, many studies are overly focused 
on the technology's functionality. They often neglect broader 
educational, ethical and creative aspects that arise when in -
tegrating AI into higher education (Chen and Feng  2025; 
Zhu et  al.  2025; Jin and Rui  2026). Third, most existing lit -
erature fails to explore the connection between teachers' AI 
skills and students' emotional and creative learning outcomes, 
even though there is growing evidence that factors such as 
classroom innovation climates, teacher beliefs and teaching 
methods significantly influence students' creative engage -
ment and motivation (Jiang et al.  2025; Ocado  2025; Zhou and 
Peng  2025).
These gaps are particularly noticeable in higher education and 
design- related fields in China. The country's rapidly grow -
ing digital education landscape and focus on innovation have 
increased the use of GAI in creative industries and university 
programmes, especially in art, design and visual communica -
tion. Despite the growing use of AI-  assisted creative platforms 
among Chinese university students, there remains a limited 
understanding of how teachers' acceptance and knowledge of 
AI affect students' creativity and motivation in design educa -
tion (Wang et  al.  2025; Qiu et  al.  2026; Wu et  al.  2026). This 
limitation is important because design education demands 
learning environments that promote both technological exper -
imentation and critical, reflective and ethically sound creative 
inquiry. To prepare future designers who can critically engage 
with AI-  generated content, educators must themselves possess 
a strong understanding of AI and confidence in using GAI tools 
in their teaching (Bartlett and Camba  2024; Zhu et  al.  2026; 
Yanmeng  2024).
Therefore, the current literature often overlooks the specific 
cognitive demands, ethical considerations, and creative expec -
tations involved in the use of GAI in creative fields such as art 
and design education. This issue is particularly concerning in 
art and design disciplines, where creativity, originality, critical 
interpretation and aesthetic judgement are not just extra learn -
ing goals but essential teaching priorities. In these areas, GAI 
serves both to enhance creativity and to create educational 
tensions. AI tools for image generation, co-  design systems and 
creative assistance can support processes of idea generation, 
experimentation and innovation. However, they also raise im -
portant questions about originality, authorship, ethical reliance 
on technology, and the commercialization of artistic thinking 
(Bartlett and Camba  2024; Jasmer  2024; Liu and Chew  2025). 
Recent research has shown that AI-  driven design settings can 
change creative thought, alter intuitive design practices, and af -
fect how students understand originality and ownership of their 
creative work in educational environments (Zhang et al.  2025; Li 
et al.  2025; Ha  2026). At the same time, there are concerns that 
over- reliance on AI-  generated results might diminish genuine 
creative exploration and critical artistic thinking if not properly 
managed (Han and Piaw  2025; Yin et  al.  2026). Thus, under -
standing GAI's role in design education requires more than a 
positive view of technology; it calls for a careful examination of 
how AI knowledge, ethical considerations, and teaching meth -
ods affect learning outcomes in creative fields.
For these reasons, this study aimed to assess teachers' percep -
tions of AI acceptance and their knowledge of AI. It also ex -
amined how these factors relate to students' motivation and 
creativity among design students in Chinese universities. By 
focusing on design education in the Chinese higher education 
context, this study addresses calls for more nuanced and crit -
ical investigations into how GAI is integrated into education. 
Importantly, it seeks to go beyond simplistic views of technology 
to examine whether teachers' AI skills are essential to shaping 
students' motivation and creative experiences in AI-  enhanced 
design learning environments.
2   |   Related Literature Review
2.1   |   Generative Artificial Intelligence and Design 
Education
The relationship between generative artificial intelligence (GAI) 
and design education is significant. Thus, the connection between 
GAI and design education is meaningful because both areas cen-
tre on creative generation, interpretive flexibility, and the transla-
tion of abstract ideas into tangible forms (Wilhelmsen et al.  2024; 
Gao et  al.  2025; Chen and Hung  2026). Unlike fields that focus 
on fixed procedures, design education relies on imaginative explo-
ration, aesthetic interpretation, and conceptual ambiguity. This 
makes GAI particularly influential in creative learning settings 
where success is defined by multiple solutions instead of single an-
swers (Lee  2025; Liu et al.  2025). GAI's role in design education is 
linked to its ability to externalize imagination and speed up visual 
thinking. With text-  to- image systems, generative visualization 
tools, and AI- assisted conceptual modelling, students can quickly 
turn abstract design ideas into visual prototypes. This reduces the 
gap between thinking and realizing ideas (Fleischmann  2024; 
Huh et al.  2025). This change fundamentally alters traditional cre-
ative workflows. Students no longer face limitations in technical 
skills during the early stages of exploration. Instead, GAI enables 
them to engage in frequent experimentation, generating, evaluat-
ing, and refining multiple aesthetic options in a shorter timeframe 
(Li et al.  2024; Alamasi et al.  2026). This acceleration is important 
because creativity in design education typically needs exposure to 
variation, experimentation, and speculative thinking, rather than

forms of collaboration between humans and AI, changing design 
students from solo creators to co- curators, interpreters, and evalua-

teaching by shifting the focus from purely technical production to 
higher- level creative judgement, conceptual reasoning, and critical 
assessment of generated material (Lee et al. 2023; Huang  2025). In 
this context, GAI has a unique role in design education compared 
to other fields. Creative disciplines require visual communication, 
symbolic interpretation, aesthetic sensitivity, and speculative in-
novation. These needs align closely with the diverse generative 
capabilities of AI systems (Jung and Suh 2024; Gao et al.  2025). As 
a result, GAI not only supports design education but actively par-
ticipates in the cognitive processes of creative practice.
Research suggests that GAI can significantly improve student 
engagement, confidence, and exploratory learning by reduc -
ing creative anxiety and enhancing quick idea visualization. 
Students who struggle to express their conceptual ideas visually 
may find AI-  assisted tools useful in guiding their creative pro -
cesses without the immediate worry of technical shortcomings 
(Fleischmann  2024; Lee  2025). In fields such as architecture, 
interior design, and product design, generative systems enable 
learners to explore a range of structural designs, aesthetic op -
tions, and spatial ideas that may be beyond reach with traditional 
studio methods (Liu et  al.  2025; Alamasi et  al.  2026). GAI- 
supported design settings have also shown promise in strength -
ening collaborative discussions, interdisciplinary thinking, and 
sustainable innovation by enabling students to interact dynam -
ically with visual and conceptual alternatives (Fathoni  2023; Li 
et  al.  2024). However, integrating GAI into design education 
also raises significant epistemological and ethical issues that 
challenge the core assumptions of creative learning. A key con -
cern is that GAI may commercialize creativity by turning deep 
artistic processes into algorithm-  driven production tasks.
This concern becomes even more urgent because generative sys-
tems usually rely on predicting and recombining existing visual 
patterns rather than on genuine originality. Therefore, students 
might unconsciously adopt the standardized aesthetic styles pres-
ent in the training data, which could limit their divergent thinking 
and lead to repetitive creative outcomes (Bartlett and Camba 2024; 
Pektaş  2025). From a teaching perspective, this creates a conflict 
where tools meant to foster creativity may also restrict original 
thinking if students depend too much on machine-  generated in-
spiration. Thus, the challenge for design education is not whether 
GAI boosts productivity, but whether it nurtures true creative 
thinking or merely speeds up the process of creating visually ap-
pealing work. The role of teachers in AI- driven design education 
is becoming increasingly complex and demanding. Traditional 
design teaching has relied on critique- based approaches, mentor-
ship, discussions, and process- focused evaluations. However, GAI 
complicates these structures as students can now produce sophis-
ticated visuals with little technical difficulty. This complicates 
the assessment of originality, skill development and authentic au-
thorship (Ratum  2025; Bartlett and Camba  2024). Educators find 
it increasingly challenging to distinguish between students' inde-
pendent contributions and AI- assisted creations. This generates 
considerable uncertainty about the validity of assessments, aca -
demic integrity, and the measurement of genuine creative skills in 
AI- supported design contexts. For students, the biggest challenge 
in integrating GAI is maintaining intellectual independence and 
reflective creativity amid increasingly automated design practices. 
While GAI can boost productivity and creative exploration, it may 
also lead to a passive acceptance of generated work if students 
lack strong critical judgement and conceptual discipline (Ouma 
et al.  2025; Pektaş  2025). This risk is especially high for beginners 
who may confuse algorithmic skills with true creativity, thus em-
phasizing visually appealing results over meaningful conceptual 
progress. Such dependence could undermine students' persever-
ance in problem- solving, reflective inquiry and independent idea 
generation, which remain crucial skills in professional design 
work (Wilhelmsen et al.  2024; Gao et al.  2025).
2.2   |   The Association Between Teacher 
AI- Acceptance and AI- Literacy
The connection between teachers' acceptance of AI and their 
AI literacy is crucial for successfully integrating generative ar -
tificial intelligence (GAI) in higher education. This is especially 
true in fields that focus on creativity, innovation and complex 
problem- solving. Recent studies view AI literacy not just as a 
narrow skill set, but as a broad ability encompassing critical 
evaluation, ethical awareness, adaptability in teaching, under -
standing algorithms and thoughtful judgement about technol -
ogy. At the same time, AI acceptance goes beyond simply having 
a positive view of technology. It includes teachers' perception of 
AI's usefulness in teaching, their professional trust, emotional 
readiness and willingness to incorporate AI into their teach -
ing methods. Research shows that these factors support each 
other rather than exist alone. Higher AI literacy tends to lead to 
greater acceptance and more meaningful use of AI in teaching 
(Yim and Wegerif  2024; Jin and Rui  2026; Lin et al.  2025; Zhu 
et al.  2026).
The quick rise of GAI in education has heightened the gap be -
tween what technology promises and what educators are ready 
for. Universities seek innovative teaching methods enhanced by 
AI, yet many teachers feel unprepared to effectively manage, 
evaluate, and use AI tools in real classrooms. As a result, a lack 
of AI literacy often leads to anxiety about technology, ethical 
concerns, reduced confidence, and resistance to using AI. In 
contrast, better AI literacy can boost teachers' confidence, flex -
ibility, and willingness to experiment with new methods (Yao 
and Wang  2024; Börekci and Çelik  2024; Bilbao-  Eraña and 
Arroyo-  Sagasta  2025). In this context, AI acceptance is funda -
mentally about understanding how AI can aid or limit teaching 
practices. Studies on technology acceptance show that AI liter -
acy greatly influences how teachers view AI's usefulness and 
ease of use, as well as their intentions to adopt it. Teachers with 
a strong understanding of AI are more likely to see GAI as a tool 
to enhance their teaching rather than as a threat to their inde -
pendence or creativity (Jin and Rui  2026; Luo et  al.  2025; Lin 
et al.  2025). On the other hand, those with limited literacy often 
worry about issues such as originality, academic honesty, and 
reliance on algorithms, which can undermine their acceptance 
and hinder innovation in teaching (Bartlett and Camba  2024; 
Jasmer  2024; Zhu et al.  2025).
Importantly, the connection between AI literacy and acceptance 
goes beyond just familiarity with technology. Recent studies

in this research area, various conceptual and contextual gaps 
remain. Much of the existing research has examined AI liter -

interact in real classroom environments. Additionally, most 
empirical studies focus on general educational settings, while 
research specific to design education remains limited. This 
gap is important to address because design disciplines involve 
unique ways of thinking centred around creativity, visual 
understanding, and aesthetic judgement, which significantly 
influence how AI literacy and acceptance are developed and 
enacted politically (Huh et al.  2025; Wu et al.  2026 ; Ge and 
Shin  2026). Therefore, more research is needed to explore the 
relationship between teachers' AI literacy and acceptance 
within design education to understand how educators can bal -
ance technological innovation with pedagogical creativity and 
ethical responsibility.
2.3   |   The Relationship Between Teachers 
AI- Acceptance and AI- Literacy With 
the Association Between Students Learning 
Motivation and Creativity
The relationship between teachers' AI acceptance and AI 
literacy with students' learning motivation and creativity is 
increasingly conceptualized as a reciprocal pedagogical eco -
system rather than a linear technological effect. Emerging 
scholarship suggests that students' creative engagement with 
AI is not primarily determined by technological access it -
self, but by teachers' epistemic confidence, pedagogical read -
iness, and critical AI literacy, which collectively shape how 
AI is framed, scaffolded, and normalized within learning 
environments. Teachers with higher AI acceptance are more 
likely to integrate generative AI into authentic, exploratory, 
and student-  centred learning activities, thereby cultivating 
stronger motivational orientations and creative risk-  taking 
among learners. Conversely, low teacher acceptance often 
leads to restrictive or compliance-  oriented AI practices that 
suppress experimentation and diminish creative agency. (Yim 
and Wegerif  2024 ; Jin and Rui  2026 ; Zhu et  al.  2026 ; Lin 
et al.  2025).
The literature increasingly demonstrates that AI literacy func -
tions not merely as technical competence, but as a mediating 
cognitive infrastructure that enables motivational persistence, 
self- efficacy, and innovation-  oriented learning behaviours. 
Students exposed to AI-  literate instructional environments ex -
hibit stronger intrinsic motivation, higher creative confidence, 
and greater willingness to engage in iterative problem-  solving 
and in human–AI co-  creation. Such findings challenge tech -
nologically deterministic assumptions by revealing that the 
educational value of AI is contingent on pedagogical context 
rather than inherently transformative. (Ji et al.  2025; Weikang 
et al.  2025; Ocado  2025; Hao et al.  2026). Importantly, the rela -
tionship between teacher AI acceptance and student creativity 
appears to operate through psychological and environmental 
mechanisms rather than direct technological exposure. Studies 
grounded in TAM, UTAUT and self-  determination frameworks 
consistently show that teachers' positive attitudes toward AI fos-
ter learning environments characterized by autonomy support, 
experimentation, and reduced technological anxiety, thereby 
strengthening students' creative cognition and motivational 
engagement. In contrast, inadequate teacher literacy often in -
tensifies uncertainty, ethical ambiguity, and cognitive overload, 
undermining students' creative performance despite the avail -
ability of sophisticated AI tools. (Khoso et al.  2025; Hwang and 
Wu  2025; Yao and Wang  2024; Bartlett and Camba  2024).
In design, art, and media education, educators' acceptance of 
AI significantly influences whether generative AI is positioned 
as a mechanistic productivity tool or as a catalyst for divergent 
thinking and creative exploration. Evidence suggests that stu -
dents demonstrate higher engagement, originality, and design- 
thinking capacity when teachers intentionally integrate AI into 
collaborative ideation, reflective inquiry, and iterative design 
processes. However, several studies caution that uncritical AI 
adoption may lead to superficial creativity, algorithmic depen -
dency, and diminished originality when educators lack critical 
AI literacy and pedagogical intentionality. (Zhang et  al.  2025; 
Herath et  al.  2026; Li et  al.  2025; Liu and Chew  2025; Han 
and Piaw  2025). Moreover, motivational outcomes are ampli -
fied when AI-  supported instruction enhances students' sense 
of competence and personal relevance. AI-  enhanced learning 
environments that promote adaptive feedback, personalization 
and co- creative participation have been associated with stronger 
engagement, persistence and creative self-  efficacy across music,

3   |   Research Method
The current study used a quantitative cross-  sectional design to 
examine the relationship between teachers' acceptance of AI 
and students' creativity. It looked at how AI literacy and learn -
ing motivation influenced this relationship among students in 
design- related fields at Chinese universities. A cross-  sectional 
approach was appropriate, as the study aimed to examine the 
connections among psychological, teaching, and technology-  
related factors in a real educational setting at a single point in 
time. This design allowed for the evaluation of both direct and 
indirect pathways among the underlying variables using struc -
tural equation modelling (SEM). SEM is especially good for test-
ing intricate mediation mechanisms and well-  founded causal 
ideas in educational technology research.
3.1   |   Participants and Research Setting
The current study was conducted across 10 Chinese universi -
ties known for their strong art and design programmes at the 
bachelor's, master's and doctoral levels. These universities were 
chosen because of their focus on creative fields and their grow -
ing use of digital technologies, artificial intelligence (AI), and 
AI- supported teaching practices. The selected institutions of -
fered programmes in visual communication, industrial design, 
digital media, animation, creative arts, environmental design, 
and other related areas. This variety provided a suitable setting 
for exploring AI-  related educational experiences in design ed -
ucation. The study used random sampling within the defined 
target population to ensure that all eligible participants had an 
equal chance of being chosen from the participating institu -
tions. The target population included students and teachers in 
design- related programmes with experience in AI-  supported 
educational environments. Participants were selected from 
this population using a random selection process based on in -
stitutional records. This method ensured that sampling did not 
rely on the researcher's choice or voluntary self-  selection. After 
obtaining ethical approval from the Shenzhen university's re -
search ethics committee, sampled teachers and students were 
required to provide written informed consent.

qualification. Regarding teaching experience, a large por -

Participants were contacted through institutional coordina -
tion channels and academic networks, which served as struc -
tured ways to reach the defined sampling frame, not as the 
sampling method itself. These channels made it easier to com -
municate with randomly selected eligible participants across 
various departments and institutions. Inclusion criteria were 
set to ensure contextual relevance: students had to be enrolled 
in design-  related programmes with previous exposure to AI-  
assisted learning tools or generative AI, while teachers needed 
experience in teaching design-  related subjects and familiarity 
with AI-  supported educational technologies. These criteria 
ensured that all selected participants had enough background 
to respond meaningfully to the study variables. By randomly 
selecting participants from multiple institutions, academic 
levels, and professional backgrounds, the study improved the 
representativeness and diversity of the sample. This approach 
enhanced the ability to explore the relationships among AI 
acceptance, AI literacy, learning motivation, and creativ -
ity within the context of Chinese higher education design 
programmes.
3.2   |   Measurements
3.2.1   |   Teachers' AI Acceptance Scale
Teachers' acceptance of AI was measured using a modified ver -
sion of the Technology Acceptance Model developed by Fred 
Davis. The scale included five areas: perceived ease of use, per -
ceived usefulness, perceived trust, AI literacy, and intelligent 
technological pedagogical content knowledge (iTPACK), along 
with behavioural intention toward GenAI acceptance. The tool 
contained items examining teachers' views on the usability, ef -
fectiveness, trustworthiness, pedagogical integration, and fu -
ture use of generative AI tools in teaching.
3.2.1.1   |   Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU).  In the Technol -
ogy Acceptance Model (TAM), perceived ease of use is a core 
determinant of technology adoption, as users are more likely to 
adopt systems they find easy to use and learn. In educational AI 
contexts, ease of use is especially important because it reduces 
cognitive load and technical anxiety, thereby increasing willing -
ness to integrate AI tools into teaching practices (Davis 1989).
3.2.1.2   |   Perceived Usefulness (PU).  Perceived useful -
ness is defined as the extent to which teachers believe that using 
GenAI will enhance their job performance, teaching effective -
ness and productivity.
3.2.1.3   |   Perceived Trust.  Teachers' confidence in the reli-
ability, safety, ethical integrity and accuracy of GenAI systems.
3.2.1.4   |   Attitude Toward AI.  It refers to teachers' knowl -
edge, understanding, and ability to effectively interact with 
AI systems, including GenAI tools, which enables teachers to 
make informed decisions about integrating AI into pedagogy 
and reduces fear or misunderstanding of AI technologies.
3.2.1.5    |   Behavioural Intention Toward GenAI Accep -
tance. In TAM, behavioural intention is the immediate 
antecedent of actual system use and is shaped by perceived use -
fulness and perceived ease of use (Davis 1989). It represents a 
psychological commitment to adopting the technology.
Sample items included ‘Learning how to use GenAI tools 
is easy for me’ and ‘I plan to use GenAI tools in my future 
teaching’. The instrument was adapted from earlier studies 
on technology acceptance and AI integration in education, 
drawing on the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis 1989) 
and the TPACK-  related AI integration literature (Mishra and 
Koehler  2006 ). The scale's validity was tested using confirma -
tory factor analysis. The results confirmed the scale's factor 
structure ( x2 = 2.63, RMSEA = 0.078, NFI = 0.92, IFI = 0.95, 
CFI = 0.95). The overall Cronbach's alpha for the scale's inter -
nal consistency is 0.94. The internal consistency coefficients 
for its subscales range from 0.87 to 0.95. The scale calculates 
the total score. Higher scores suggest increased acceptance 
of AI.
3.2.2   |   AI Literacy Scale
Students' AI literacy was assessed using a modified AI literacy 
competency tool derived from previous educational technology 
studies on AI literacy and the TPACK framework. The scale 
contained four dimensions: content knowledge (CK), peda -
gogical content knowledge (PCK), technological pedagogical 
content knowledge (TPACK), and technological content knowl -
edge (TCK).
3.2.2.1    |   Content Knowledge (CK).  Refers to an individ -
ual's foundational understanding of AI-  related concepts, prin -
ciples and applications within a specific domain. In educational 
contexts, this includes knowledge of what AI is, how AI systems 
function, their capabilities and limitations, ethical consider -
ations, and their relevance to disciplinary learning (Mishra 
and Koehler 2006).

meaningfully incorporate AI into teaching and learning pro -

instructional strategies, and student needs (Koehler et al. 2013). 
Within AI literacy research, TPACK is increasingly viewed as 
a critical competency because it reflects the capacity to move 
beyond simple AI use toward informed, ethical, and pedagogi -
cally purposeful AI integration.
The instrument evaluated participants' understanding of 
AI concepts, computational thinking, teaching strategies 
for AI learning, and practical skills in using AI tools such 
as Teachable Machine, Scratch, and generative AI systems. 
Example items included ‘I have sufficient knowledge of AI’ 
and ‘I can design and teach lessons that effectively combine 
AI, technologies, and teaching methods’. The instrument 
was adapted from the TPACK theory proposed by Punya 
Mishra and Matthew J. Koehler, along with current AI liter -

AI literacy scale was confirmed ( x2 = 2.11, RMSEA = 0.067,

of its subscales ranges from 0.87 to 0.96. The scale is used to 
calculate the total score. Higher scores indicate a greater ac -
ceptance level of AI literacy.
3.2.3   |   Learning Motivation Toward AI- Powered 
Learning Scale
Learning motivation was measured using a modified intrinsic 
motivation scale designed to assess students' motivation while 
engaging with AI- powered learning environments. The tool was 
based on Self-  Determination Theory, developed by Edward L. 
Deci and Richard M. Ryan. The scale included 10 items measur-
ing enjoyment, curiosity, engagement, intrinsic interest, creative 
stimulation, and willingness to keep learning with AI- supported 
systems. Sample items included ‘Learning with the AI chatbot 
was fun’ and ‘Using the AI chatbot made me want to explore 
design ideas further on my own’. The instrument focused specif-
ically on students' intrinsic motivation within AI- assisted design 
learning activities. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) showed 
that the factor loadings for positive attitudes ranged from 0.47 
to 0.78. The loadings for negative attitudes ranged from 0.41 to 
0.75. The Cronbach's alpha for internal consistency for the pos -
itive attitudes scale is 0.88. For the negative attitudes scale, the 
Cronbach's alpha is 0.83. The scale measures positive and neg -
ative attitudes separately. Higher scores on the scale indicate a 
strong attitude toward the subdimension.
3.2.4   |   Creativity Scale
Students' creativity was evaluated using a modified product- 
based creativity scale. This scale was adapted from established 
frameworks created by Stein in 1953 and later expanded in de -
sign and product creativity research. Unlike self-  efficacy or self- 
perception measures, this tool specifically assesses the creative 
qualities of finished design outputs through structured semantic 
criteria.
Participants' design products were rated using a 15-  item se -
mantic differential scale. Trained raters assessed each product 
along bipolar adjective dimensions that capture key indicators 
of creative performance. These dimensions included originality, 
usefulness, elaboration, refinement, organization and innova -
tion, which are widely acknowledged in creativity research as 
key indicators of both novelty and appropriateness in creative 
work. Each item received a rating on a five-  point semantic dif -
ferential scale, featuring opposing descriptors (e.g., Ordinary- 
Original, Conventional-  Innovative, Basic- Well- crafted). Higher 
scores indicated greater perceived creativity, including original -
ity, technical refinement and design innovation. This measure 
was not designed to capture general self-  assessments of cre -
ativity. Instead, it aimed to define creativity as an observable 
product- level construct. This approach is consistent with estab -
lished methods in design education research, where creative 
performance is typically assessed through structured ratings of 
artefacts based on novelty and usefulness criteria. Prior studies 
suggest that design creativity is best viewed as a multidimen -
sional product attribute rather than a purely subjective trait.
The scale's validity was tested using confirmatory factor anal -
ysis. The factor structure of the creativity scale was confirmed 
(x2 = 1.82, RMSEA = 0.04, GFI = 0.96, NFI = 0.95, CFI = 0.98). 
The overall Cronbach's alpha internal consistency coefficient 
of the scale is 0.85. The internal consistency coefficients of its 
subscales range from 0.72 to 0.76. The scale is used to calculate 
the total score. Higher scores on the scale indicate higher AI 
literacy.

context. Afterward, these aggregated teacher-  level constructs 
were combined with the student dataset using a common group 
identifier (e.g., class, programme, or institution code). This en -
sured that each student record matched the corresponding in -
structional environment defined by teacher-  level AI acceptance 
and AI literacy. This method allowed teacher variables to serve 
as contextual predictors of student-  level outcomes (learning 
motivation and innovativeness) within a single integrated SEM 
framework.

model, where:
• AI acceptance and AI literacy (aggregated teacher-  level 
constructs) were exogenous variables,
• Learning motivation and innovativeness (student- level con-
structs) were endogenous variables.
Although a full multilevel SEM would be ideal for explicitly mod-
elling nested variance, this study used an aggregation- based SEM 
approach due to constraints imposed by the sample structure and 
uneven teacher- student ratios. This approach is consistent with 
previous educational research, which models contextual teacher 
effects using aggregated indicators linked to student outcomes. 
This method ensures that the observed relationships reflect shared 
instructional environments rather than isolated individual-  level 
effects from teachers or students. It improves the interpretability 
of cross- group influences in AI- supported learning contexts.

4.2   |   Test of the Measurement Model
In this study, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used 
to assess the validity of the variables in a research model 
grounded in theoretical foundations that have been tested 
in previous studies. CFA checks if the predicted factor struc -
ture aligns with the observed variables. Within this study, 
CFA established the construct validity of the measurement 
model, which included AI acceptance, AI literacy, learning 
motivation and creativity scales. Additionally, the issue of 
standard-  method variance during data collection was ex -
amined. Standard-  method variance refers to the systematic 
errors that can arise when the same person completes mul -
tiple scales within the same timeframe in surveys. Harman's 
single-  factor test method was employed to identify these sys -
tematic errors and manage their influence. This test looks at 
how much variance a single factor explains. If a single factor 
accounts for more than 50% of the total variance, it suggests 
possible method bias, which could threaten measurement 
validity. Conversely, if a factor accounts for less than 50% of 
the variance, it indicates low method bias and suggests that 
the measurements are trustworthy. In this study, the analy -
sis showed that a single factor explained only 43% of the total 
variance. This finding indicates a low risk of method bias, 
ensuring the validity of the scales used. Within the research 
framework, several model alternatives were compared to test 
the construct validity of the variables and the model's fit with 
the data. Table  1 presents the goodness-  of- fit values for the re -
search's four-  factor measurement model and alternative mod -
els (Model 1, Model 2, Model 3 and Model 4).

models. Each fit criterion was applied to assess how well the 
models matched the data. Upon analysing Table  2, the four-

better fit indices than the alternative models. The fit indices for 
this model (χ 2/df = 2.01, RMSEA = 0.04; CFI = 0.93; TLI = 0.93; 
SRMR = 0.07) meet general acceptance criteria and indicate a 
low risk of standard method variance. The alternative models 
show higher error and lower fit than the research model. These 
findings suggest that the research model is the most suitable

5   |   Discussion
The findings of this study add to the limited understanding 
of how AI acceptance and AI literacy relate to creativity and 
learning motivation in design education, especially in Chinese 
higher education. Here, creativity is not just a learning target 
but a job requirement. Previous research has often viewed gen -
erative AI as a tool that inherently boosts creativity in design 
fields. However, this study challenges that view by showing 
that the connections between AI, motivation, and creativity are 
much more complex and context-  dependent than typically be -
lieved. This understanding is particularly important in design 
education, where originality, experimentation, tolerance for 
ambiguity, and independent thinking are essential to creative 
work rather than straightforward outputs generated by algo -
rithms. (Bartlett and Camba  2024; Wilhelmsen et al.  2024; Gao 
et al.  2025; Han and Piaw  2025).
The demographic results show that female teachers had signifi -
cantly higher AI acceptance and AI literacy compared to male 
teachers. This finding complicates past assumptions that tech -
nological confidence and AI engagement are primarily associ -
ated with male educators. Instead, the results suggest that the 
integration of AI in creative fields may depend more on teaching 
flexibility, collaboration, and a willingness to experiment with 
new technologies than on traditional gender stereotypes. Recent 
evidence supports this idea, indicating that AI acceptance is 
more closely tied to teaching beliefs, professional adaptability, 
and perceived relevance than to demographics alone. (Yim and 
Wegerif  2024; Jin and Rui  2026; Zhu et al.  2026). Additionally, 
the lack of significant differences across educational levels indi -
cates that having formal academic qualifications alone does not 
fully explain readiness for AI. This finding challenges the belief 
that higher academic achievement automatically leads to better 
adaptation to new technologies. It suggests that competence in 
using AI in design education may develop more from hands-  on 
experience and ongoing exposure than from formal degrees. 
(Luo et al.  2025; Lin et al.  2025).
Further, the finding that teaching experience significantly af -
fects AI literacy but not AI acceptance shows an important dif -
ference between being ready to use AI and actually having the 
skills to do so in education. In this study, AI acceptance was 
conceptualized as a motivational and attitudinal construct en -
compassing perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, be -
havioural intention, and trust in AI systems. This aligns with 
the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis 1989; Zhu 
et  al.  2025). Based on this, H1 was supported; AI acceptance 
significantly and positively predicted teachers' AI literacy. This 
indicates that positive attitudes toward AI are linked to a stron -
ger foundational understanding of AI-  related skills. However, 
AI literacy was defined as a broad skill set based on the TPACK 
framework, which includes CK, PCK, TCK and TPACK (Mishra 
and Koehler 2006; Koehler et al. 2013). This distinction clarifies 
that while acceptance shows willingness to engage with AI, lit -
eracy reflects the depth of pedagogical and technological knowl-
edge necessary for meaningful application.
The strong positive links between AI acceptance and AI literacy 
further support TAM principles, confirming H1 at the student 
level, where perceived usefulness, ease of use, trust and positive 
attitudes encourage engagement with AI systems (Davis 1989; 
Zhu et al.  2025; Wang et al.  2025; Chen and Feng 2025). Students 
with higher AI acceptance showed stronger AI literacy across 
CK, PCK, TCK and TPACK, suggesting that a willingness to en -
gage with AI contributes to a deeper understanding. However, 
this study builds on previous research by showing that such en -
gagement does not automatically lead to improved creativity or 
sustained motivation. Instead, the benefits of acceptance dimin -
ish when AI use shifts from exploratory engagement to routine 
reliance on algorithmic outputs.
With this background, the difference between AI acceptance 
and AI literacy, as seen through teaching experience, is easier to 
interpret. Teaching experience deepens AI literacy, particularly 
PCK and TPACK, through ongoing involvement in instructional 
design and classroom practice. However, this does not automati-
cally lead to stronger AI acceptance, which might be influenced 
more by institutional discussions or general attitudes toward 
new technologies. In this regard, the results confirm that H2 was 
also supported, as AI acceptance positively affected students' 
learning motivation. This suggests that favourable views of AI 
boost learners' willingness to engage in their studies, even if this 
motivation does not always lead to deeper cognitive or creative 
results. This separation is important because much of the ex -
isting literature confuses acceptance with competence. It over -
looks that educators and students can have a positive attitude 
toward AI while lacking the deeper analytical and pedagogical 
skills needed for effective integration (Börekci and Çelik  2024; 
Yao and Wang  2024; Salhab and Aboushi  2025). Moreover, mul-
tivariate patterns that show variation in motivation and creativ -
ity by gender and age indicate that involvement in AI- supported 
design learning is not equal across learner groups. These differ -
ences make sense when viewed through the lens of AI literacy 
dimensions. For example, stronger CK and TCK may foster crit -
ical engagement with AI systems, increasing technical aware -
ness while potentially reducing excitement driven by novelty. 
Variations in TPACK may further shape how students use AI 
in creative processes, affecting their autonomy and ownership. 
These findings highlight that AI-  related learning outcomes are 
influenced by psychological, cognitive and contextual factors, 
not just exposure (Hwang and Wu  2025; Jiang et al.  2025; Yin 
et al.  2026).
Importantly, the lack of significant differences across academic 
levels suggests that advancing in higher education does not nec -
essarily improve students' creative use of AI. This raises con -
cerns that growing familiarity with AI tools may normalize and

However, this direct effect contrasts with the indirect pathways, 
in which increased AI literacy and reduced motivational en -

encourages initial openness and exploration, its benefits fade 
when mediated through deeper AI engagement processes.
Furthermore, the positive link between learning motivation 
and creativity supports established theories that emphasize the 
role of intrinsic motivation in creative performance (Kadyirov 
et  al.  2024; Jiang et  al.  2025). This confirms the motivational 
aspect within H5 at the structural level, although its overall 
mediating strength remains limited. Creativity still relies on 
psychological engagement, autonomy and curiosity, even in AI- 
supported environments. Finally, the SEM findings highlight 
the contradictory nature of AI use in design education. While 
H3 is supported through a direct positive pathway, the broader 
model shows that AI literacy introduces a paradox that could 
suppress creativity through over-  optimization and reliance 
on automated outputs. In this way, AI literacy serves both as 
a means of understanding and as a potential constraint on cre -
ative divergence. This challenges the assumption in H6 that 
gaining knowledge naturally leads to increased motivation and 
creativity. Ultimately, these findings suggest that the relation -
ships among AI acceptance, AI literacy, learning motivation, 
and creativity are non-  linear and context-  dependent. Instead of 
following a straightforward path, the six hypotheses collectively 
reveal a dynamic system in which positive attitudes toward AI 
can both enable and limit creative outcomes, depending on how 
AI literacy and motivation interact within design education 
settings.
6   |   Research Implications
This study adds to the growing conversation about generative 
artificial intelligence (GAI) in design education. It provides real- 
world evidence from Chinese higher education, where creativ -
ity, originality, and innovation are essential goals. Unlike much 
existing research, which mainly views AI as a tool to enhance 
learning and creativity, the current findings reveal a more com -
plex relationship among AI acceptance, AI literacy, learning mo-
tivation, and creativity in design fields. These conclusions have 
significant policy and practical implications for universities, 
curriculum developers, educators and policymakers who want 
to use AI in creative education responsibly and sustainably.
6.1   |   Policy Implications
The findings suggest that higher education policies regarding 
AI in design disciplines should go beyond mere technology 
adoption. They should focus on balanced teaching methods and 
AI frameworks that are sensitive to creativity. Current policies 
often prioritize technological readiness, operational efficiency, 
and digital change but do not fully consider how overreliance 
on AI might weaken originality, intrinsic motivation, and inde -
pendent creative thinking. The study shows a negative indirect 
effect of AI literacy on creativity, suggesting that policies should 
not automatically equate AI skill with creative growth. Instead, 
AI governance policies in design education should address is -
sues like cognitive dependence, creative sameness, ethical de -
sign practices and overreliance on algorithms.
The results also highlight the need for specific AI integration 
policies across disciplines rather than a one-  size- fits- all digi -
tal strategy. Design education is fundamentally different from 
many STEM fields because it requires creative ambiguity, exper-
imentation, subjective interpretation and aesthetic exploration. 
As a result, policies that encourage broad AI adoption without 
protecting human-  centred creativity may weaken the founda -
tional principles of design education. Universities should create 
thoughtful AI guidelines that view AI as a supportive tool for 
co- creation rather than a replacement for human imagination 
and artistic inquiry. Moreover, the significant role of teachers' 
AI acceptance and literacy underscores the need for national 
and institutional educational policies that invest in ongoing pro-
fessional development for design educators. These programmes 
should go beyond just technical AI training and cover critical AI 
teaching methods, ethical AI use, creative facilitation strategies

6.2   |   Practical Implications
From a practical standpoint, the findings recommend that de -
sign educators take a balanced approach to AI use in their teach-
ing. While AI acceptance can enhance creative engagement, the 
negative effects of AI literacy suggest that heavy reliance on AI 
tools may hinder independent creative exploration. Design edu -
cators should avoid teaching methods that focus solely on mas -
tering AI skills. Instead, AI activities should be integrated into 
reflective, inquiry-  based, and human- centred creative processes 
that uphold students' autonomy and originality.
The findings also suggest that AI-  supported design learning 
environments should focus on developing the creative process 
rather than just the final products. Students should be encour -
aged to critically assess, reinterpret, and challenge AI-  generated 
suggestions rather than accept algorithmic outputs. Techniques 
such as iterative critique, reflective journaling, collaborative 
brainstorming, and concept justification may help students re -
main intellectually engaged and think independently while 
using AI technologies. The relationship between AI literacy and 
learning motivation should be carefully examined in light of 
the overall model results. In these results, learning motivation 
had only a small indirect effect on creativity. This means that 
while improved AI literacy may affect how motivated students 
feel—especially if they see creative tasks as being increasingly 
supported by AI—this change in motivation does not necessar -
ily lead to lower creative performance in the structural model. 
Still, shifts in motivation are important for teaching because 
they can influence the quality of student engagement, their 
persistence, and their willingness to explore new learning pro -
cesses. Educators should create AI-  supported learning environ -
ments that allow students to maintain control and ownership of 
their tasks. Assignments should promote experimentation, orig -
inality, and thoughtful reasoning rather than just focusing on ef-
ficiency. Even if motivation isn't the main driver of creativity in 
the model, it remains crucial for fostering meaningful engage -
ment and enhancing the deeper learning processes essential to 
creative thinking in AI-  integrated classrooms.
The study also highlights the need to rethink assessment prac -
tices in design education. Traditional assessments that heavily 
weight final products may unintentionally promote excessive 
reliance on AI. Instead, assessment strategies should evaluate 
creative processes, conceptual development, critical reflection 
and evidence of independent design thinking along with final 
outputs. This approach may help maintain authentic creativ -
ity in AI-  enhanced learning environments. Finally, the find -
ings suggest that AI integration in design education should be 
viewed not just as a technological challenge but also as a change 
in pedagogy and philosophy that affects how creativity is under -
stood and nurtured. Educational practitioners should be wary of 
overly simplistic solutions that see AI as a quick route to innova-
tion. Sustainable AI integration in design fields requires a care -
ful balance between technology and human creative agency, 
ensuring that AI supports rather than undermines the develop -
ment of genuine creative skills.
