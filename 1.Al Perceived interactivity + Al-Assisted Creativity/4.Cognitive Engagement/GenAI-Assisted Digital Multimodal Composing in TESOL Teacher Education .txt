Abstract
This study explored how pre-service TESOL teachers integrated
GenAI tools into a Digital Multimodal Composing (DMC) project,
composing a website, at a Hong Kong university. Speciﬁcally, it
explored their decision-making and cognitive engagement in the
DMC process when they had autonomy in the use of GenAI tools.
Drawing on a social practice perspective of literacy, the research
explores how GenAI supports or complicates multimodal composing
and the professional learning outcomes for TESOL pre-service
teachers. Data were collected through group and individual inter-
views with stimulated recall and analysis of group project websites.
Findings reveal that all ten participating groups used GenAI tools,
though in selective and strategic ways inﬂuenced by perceptions of
tool efﬁciency, capabilities, authenticity, meaning-making, and
desired degree of involvement. GenAI was employed both for creat-
ing multimodal content (e.g., images, videos, and summaries) and as
a partner in the composing process (e.g., idea generation and feed-
back). The DMC project fostered creativity, digital literacy, and criti-
cal reﬂection, with participants reporting increased conﬁdence in
multimodal and AI-assisted pedagogies. However, concerns about AI
reliability and contextual constraints in future teaching environments
were identiﬁed. The study highlights the importance of experiential
learning, explicit guidance, and ethical awareness for integrating AI

INTRODUCTION
T
he development of GenAI tools is disrupting contemporary com-
posing processes (Godwin-Jones, 2024). A range of GenAI-
supported literacy practices is emerging, compelling language teachers
and learners to rethink composing in general and multimodal com-
posing speciﬁcally. Consequently, it is increasingly important for lan-
guage learners to develop AI, digital, and multimodal literacies to
engage successfully in these new literacy practices (Jiang & Lai, 2025).
However, to meet these needs and facilitate students’ development of
these literacies, language teachers must develop expertise in GenAI-
assisted, multimodal practices (Kohnke, Zou, Ou, & Gu, 2025). The
integration of Digital Multimodal Composing (DMC) projects into
TESOL teacher education programs, with their emphasis on combin-
ing multiple semiotic modes to create a meaningful multimodal
text for a speciﬁc audience (Jiang, 2017), has been suggested as a way
to facilitate language teachers’ literacy development (Jiang &
Hafner, 2024).
GenAI tools both complicate and open new opportunities for DMC.
GenAI-assisted DMC may reduce some barriers to implementing DMC
by automating aspects of text composing (Jiang, 2024). At the same
time, GenAI tools require speciﬁc competencies, for example, prompt-
ing skills, and learners need to be able to use the tools strategically
(Moorhouse, 2024). Equally, depending on how they are used, GenAI
tools may expand or constrain opportunities for learning and
meaning-making within composing processes (Liu, Zhang, & Biebri-
cher, 2024). Given the complex context, there is a need to understand
how pre-service teachers use GenAI as part of the multimodal compos-
ing processes and the implications of this use for their professional
learning. In particular, while there is a growing body of research exam-
ining teachers’ and learners’ GenAI use in TESOL (e.g., Kohnke
et al., 2025; Warschauer et al., 2023), there is a need to better under-
stand how pre-service teachers make agentive decisions about when,
how, and for what purposes to integrate (or deliberately not integrate)
GenAI when they have autonomy during a substantive DMC task.

tools in a DMC group project. The project tasked students with

Digital and Multimodal Literacies
In this study, we adopt a social practice approach to language and
literacy education, one that aligns with the theoretical perspectives of
the new literacy studies (New London Group, 1996; Street, 1984).
According to this perspective, literacy is more than a cognitive process
of encoding and decoding texts (Gee, 1989). Rather, literacy is a social
practice enacted in various literacy events and involves the interper-
sonal and social processes that contribute to making meaning, build-
ing relationships, constructing identities, and critically understanding
how power operates through positioning (Jones & Hafner, 2021). The
concept of digital literacies aligns with this sociocultural view of liter-
acy practices, focusing on the ways technological innovations and digi-
tal media create new contexts for social literacy practices (Liu, 2025;
Weninger, 2023).
Scholars of language and literacy education have long argued that L2
learners need to develop multimodal and digital literacies in order to be
able to engage with digital media through the interpretation and utiliza-
tion of diverse semiotic resources (i.e., textual, aural, linguistic, spatial,
and visual) to communicate and make meaning in their target language
(Kress, 2003). TESOL pedagogies have been developed to reﬂect this
approach. One such pedagogy is DMC. DMC, as a pedagogical approach,

AI-Assisted DMC
GenAI tools can generate a wide range of monomodal and multi-
modal content (e.g., images, videos, code, and audio) and perform
human-like tasks, such as text summarization, translation, and senti-
ment analysis (Barrot, 2023; Moorhouse, 2024). In addition,
“AI-assisted” features are being embedded in many digital tools utilized
by language teachers and learners for multimodal composing (e.g.,
Canva). These developments have led many educators to speculate
about their utility in language teaching (Pack & Maloney, 2024).
Recently, there has been an increase in empirical studies exploring
the integration of AI into TESOL (e.g., Kohnke et al., 2025;
Warschauer et al., 2023). Most studies exploring the integration of
GenAI tools offer promising results with reservations.
Regarding AI-assisted DMC in TESOL, there has been scant
research. Scholars have speculated that multimodal GenAI can offer
writers opportunities to engage more effectively in multimodal literacy
practices (Kang & Kim, 2023). One study conducted by Jiang and
Lai ( 2025) compared the composition processes and products of 14
GenAI-assisted groups and 10 non-GenAI-assisted groups that partici-
pated in a one-semester-long DMC project at a Hong Kong secondary
school, in which L2 learners composed video projects. The researchers
found that the groups that utilized GenAI within their DMC processes
scored signiﬁcantly higher in the development of purpose, outline/
navigation skills (organizing in genre-appropriate stages) and their
overall DMC outcomes, but had a similar performance to the non-AI
group in the expression of base units (e.g., through linguistic or visual
modes), layouts and rhetorical relationships. Jiang and Lai ( 2025)
devised a taxonomy of four types of affordances of GenAI-assisted
DMC over non-GenAI-assisted DMC: (1) generating and composing
purpose-related ideas/topics; (2) constructing and composing
outlines/navigation ﬂows; (3) generating scripts and feedback for cus-
tomized writing; (4) offering a range of customized resources/
templates for special effects and personalization in DMC. Lin and col-
leagues, also in Hong Kong (Lin, Zhou, Li, & Sun, 2025), explored
the use of GenAI as part of a 2-week-long DMC project in a Chinese-
as-a-second-language classroom. They found students used GenAI in
ﬁve key areas: content generation, feedback and revision, multilingual
support, critical thinking and visual representation.

The two studies suggest that AI-assisted DMC could involve two
broad categories of AI use:
1. as a tool for creating/generating resources, materials, and multi-
modal representations/meaning making that form part of the ﬁnal
product (e.g., images, videos, soundtracks, visuals, and graphs);
2. as a resource/partner/guide of the DMC process. AI tools can
assist with research, brainstorming, drafting, providing feedback,
editing, translation, and summarizing.
Understanding the decisions learners make in using AI in the above
ways could shed light on the utility of the tools and how they shape
DMC processes. However, beyond identifying uses, it is important to
examine how such decisions are shaped by the social and pedagogical
purposes of DMC (e.g., audience design, genre expectations, and cred-
ibility), particularly in teacher education where participants are devel-
oping professional judgment about future classroom practices.
AI-Assisted DMC and TESOL Teacher Education
There is a clear understanding that teachers need to recognize the
beneﬁts of DMC and feel conﬁdent and competent to implement it in
their classrooms (Jiang & Hafner, 2024). Therefore, effective incorpo-
ration of multimodal pedagogies into teacher education is essential
(Li, 2020). It is likely that to get TESOL teachers “on board” with
AI-assisted DMC, they should participate in a DMC project as part of
their teacher preparation.
While there do not appear to be any studies conducted in TESOL
teacher education on AI-assisted DMC, Smith, Shimizu, Burriss, Hund-
ley, and Pendergrass ( 2025) examined the processes and perspectives
of 21 pre-service teachers from a diversity of majors as they composed
multimodal texts in a literacy education course. During a 3-hour class
session in two different literacy educator courses, the students were
tasked with retelling or remixing a text (e.g., a novel, play, or article)
into a multimodal composition using GenAI. They found that, as well
as using GenAI tools to create multimodal texts (songs, images), they
also used them as “thought partners” that spurred new ideas. The
pre-service teachers had mixed views on whether GenAI should be
incorporated into the classroom. Those who had negative perceptions
seemed concerned that GenAI might reduce opportunities for mean-
ingful learning experiences. Smith and colleagues’ study offers valu-
able insights. However, the intervention was limited in scope, and the

Clearly, there needs to be exploration of TESOL pre-service

Theoretical framing: GenAI as a mediational resource in social liter-
acy practices. Consistent with the social practice view of literacy (New
London Group, 1996; Street, 1984), we conceptualize GenAI not simply
as a productivity tool but as a mediational resource that can shape how
writers make meaning, design for audiences, and negotiate authorship
and responsibility within situated composing practices (Godwin-
Jones, 2024; Moorhouse, Wan, Wu, Wu, & Ho, 2025). In DMC, such
mediation is evident in how participants orchestrate GenAI alongside
other digital tools and semiotic resources to produce multimodal texts
(e.g., websites) for particular audiences. In this study, we therefore focus
on patterns of agentive decision-making about GenAI use (including
selective uptake and deliberate restraint), the rationales that shape these
decisions, and how participation in GenAI-assisted DMC contributes to
TESOL pre-service teachers’ development of AI –multimodal–digital lit-
eracies and pedagogical reasoning for future practice.
In addition, central to this framing is the dual identity of many TESOL
pre-service teachers, including the participants in our study (see Method-
ology section), as L2 users of English who are simultaneously preparing
to become English-language teachers. This positioning means that, in
addition to technical and compositional considerations, their decisions
regarding the use of GenAI could be inﬂuenced by language and iden-
tity. For example, they may evaluate AI outputs as language (e.g., register,
ﬂuency, accuracy, and intelligibility), negotiate questions of linguistic
authority and ownership of English as L2 users themselves (Warschauer
et al., 2023), and reason about the multimodal meaning-making practices
they may later ask their own language learners to undertake.

Participants
During the DMC workshop, the pre-service teachers were given
information about the research project and provided with a link to an
instant messaging group where they could indicate their interest in
joining the project. Of the 137 students in the course, 22 agreed to
participate, representing ten group projects (see Supplementary
Material S5 for the group project topics and list of group members
who participated in each interview).
The participants included 19 females and 3 males. They ranged in
age from 22 to 28. All participants originated from Mainland China.
Most had no or limited teaching experience before joining the pro-
gram. The vast majority planned to work as English-language teachers
in Mainland China, Hong Kong, or Macau upon graduating. All partic-
ipants were provided with an information sheet outlining the project
details and their rights as participants. They all provided informed
consent to participate.
Data Collection Tools
The study deployed two data collection methods: group / individual
interviews with stimulated recall of the website composing processes,
which were the primary method, and the collection of the ten groups’
websites, used as a secondary method. This combination allowed us to
anchor participants’ accounts in concrete artifacts (their websites)
while also eliciting rationales for tool-related decisions that are not
directly observable from the ﬁnal product alone.
To facilitate the interviews, a protocol was developed. The ﬁrst
author reviewed the relevant literature and developed a draft protocol,
which was then shared with and discussed by the two other researchers

the third author, who neither taught the course nor had prior rela-

accounts of the composing decisions that led to them.

Data Analysis
We then engaged in a form of reﬂexive thematic analysis (Braun &
Clarke, 2006) to identify salient themes related to our RQs. In keeping
with reﬂexive thematic analysis, our aim was not to quantify stable
“coder agreement,” but to develop analytically defensible themes
through iterative engagement with the data, team dialogue, and
attention to how our theoretical orientation shaped interpretation
(Consoli & Ganassin, 2023).
To address RQ1, we ﬁrst read each transcript and extracted data on
participants’ use of GenAI and other digital tools. This included the
tools they described using (e.g., the type/speciﬁc tool mentioned) and
their use of the tools (see Table 1 for an example). We did this for
each transcript. After this, we extracted the mentioned AI tools and
categorized their uses under “uses for creation” and “uses for partner-
ing as part of the process.” We then analyzed participants’ accounts
for recurring decision-making patterns (e.g., delegating a task to
GenAI, iterative prompting, switching between GenAI and non-AI
tools, and verifying/editing GenAI outputs) and the rationales offered
for these patterns (e.g., efﬁciency, perceived capability, authenticity,
and degree of responsibility).
We then combined the data from all 10 interviews to identify simi-
larities and differences in how the tools were used. At this stage, we
observed that different groups used GenAI tools differently in their
DMC process, and we revisited the data to identify the reasons for
these differences. To enhance analytic transparency, we documented
themes, codes, and illustrative examples (an excerpt is provided in
Supplementary Material S7).
To address RQ2, we read the transcripts multiple times and identi-
ﬁed data pertaining to participants’ perceived learning from the pro-
ject and their future intentions regarding AI and DMC use. We then
coded the extracted data and organized it into salient themes (e.g.,

FINDINGS
TESOL Pre-Service Teachers Utilize GenAI as Part of the
DMC Process
The interviews revealed that all groups used GenAI tools alongside
other digital tools. However, the teachers reported using these tools in
different ways and to varying degrees. Their responses during the
interviews suggested they were selective and strategic in their use of
GenAI tools and functionalities. Below, we present (a) patterns in how
participants orchestrated GenAI and other tools during the DMC web-
site project, and (b) the rationales they reported for selective uptake
and deliberate restraint.
Patterns of tool orchestration across the DMC website process. As
part of the data analysis process, we extracted data on GenAI and the
other tools mentioned. We then categorized these into AI tools, tools
with embedded AI features, and non-AI tools. (These tools do have AI
features, such as algorithms that help identify content based on user
preferences; however, the AI feature is not actively used by the user.
Rather, it works in the background.) As Table 2 shows, nearly all the
groups (n = 9) used LLMs (e.g., ChatGPT; DeepSeek) as part of their
DMC process. In addition, two groups used image generators and
video generators, respectively. Importantly, the groups reported using
LLMs for image generation —many LLMs can now process and gener-
ate multimodal texts, reducing the need to go to speciﬁc platforms
(Moorhouse & Wong, 2025).
Given that the pre-service teachers were tasked with composing a
website, it is no surprise that they all used multimodal creation tools
and website builders (e.g., Canva; Wix). Three groups explicitly

TESOL pre-service teachers made decisions about GenAI use as part

using or not using GenAI. Four reasons were identiﬁed: (1) Efﬁciency;
(2) Functionality and capabilities; (3) Authenticity and creativity; (4)
Degree of involvement.

cost less time to achieve their intended outcomes. This was especially
evident in language-focused tasks. For example, participants frequently
delegated the summarizing and polishing of English text to GenAI
because it enabled them to quickly convert dense academic content

DMC project as a valuable learning opportunity. The participants had
limited or no prior experience creating a multimodal website. Com-
pared with traditional essay writing, the participants felt the DMC pro-

This, they felt, pushed them to learn new skills, such as website design,
video editing, AI evaluation, and image creation, that were relevant to
their future careers as language teachers. Chang (group 6) stated,
“This experience was very different from the assessments we’ve done before. It

teacher, having the ability to create a website could be a signiﬁcant
advantage.”

valuable included the need to create a multimodal website, the group
nature of the task, and the opportunity to review their peers’ websites
and receive feedback. Participants believed that the opportunity to
work in groups meant they could collaboratively solve problems and
learn from one another. For example, Shi (group 1) mentioned how
she learned about MidJourney, an AI image generator, from her team-

opportunities. Dong (group 7) shared that, after reviewing other

“engage our audience” when composing his group’s website. He elabo-

professional learning was not only about acquiring technical skills but
also about developing sensitivity to multimodal genre expectations
(e.g., audience engagement, pacing, and interactivity) relevant to
teacher-facing digital texts.

DISCUSSION
Situated within the social practice view of literacy (e.g., New London
Group, 1996) that sees literacy as a set of socially situated practices,
the study set out to explore TESOL pre-service teachers’ utilization of
GenAI as they engaged in a DMC project when they were given the
choice to use these tools as part of their DMC process. The study was
limited to one teacher education program in a Hong Kong university
and relied primarily on participants’ self-reported data through inter-
views. Therefore, the ﬁndings should be interpreted accordingly. How-
ever, the study provides useful insights despite these limitations.

decision-making and professional learning, using cognitive

Agentive Decision-Making and Orchestration of GenAI in
DMC
The ﬁndings show that GenAI tools can be integrated into various
aspects of the DMC process (see Table 3 for a summary). GenAI tools
were used to create and generate various kinds of multimodal repre-
sentations that formed parts of the website (e.g., images, videos,
graphs, and summaries), and consulted as partners that the teachers
used to seek ideas, advice and recommendations, and feedback and
improvement suggestions on content they had created. Similar GenAI
uses have been found in other studies. However, in previous studies,
participants were required to use GenAI (e.g., Smith et al., 2025), or
researchers employed an experimental design (e.g., Jiang &
Lai, 2025). Our study shows that in an authentic, naturalistic setting
where TESOL pre-service teachers were given agency to use GenAI or
not, they chose to use GenAI tools for speciﬁc tasks. This extends
prior work by showing how the use of GenAI emerges as selective
orchestration within a teacher-facing DMC website genre, rather than
as a uniform or mandated practice.
By exploring individual groups’ reported uses of GenAI, we can bet-
ter understand the factors that shaped the decisions to use GenAI. We
can see that some groups integrated GenAI tools across various aspects
of the process and product, while others used them more narrowly.
Using GenAI tools as a partner was common across the groups. These
ﬁndings support previous studies that found that GenAI is integrated
into learners’ composing processes (e.g., Kang & Kim, 2023; Moor-
house et al., 2025). In terms of creation, GenAI tools were used for
image generation, text creation and text summarization. However,
video generation and coding were less common. The decision to use
these tools was inﬂuenced by their perceived efﬁciency and ability to
support the website’s composition. For example, the pre-service
teachers reported that these tools were more effective and easier to
use for text-based written tasks than for video generation. The ability
of these tools to generate, revise, and summarize written text was con-
sidered a more mature capability than video generation. Importantly,
these decisions were not just about evaluations of “better” or “worse”
tools. From a social practice perspective, participants’ rationales (efﬁ-
ciency, capability, authenticity, and degree of involvement) reﬂect how
they negotiated the division of composing responsibilities, audience

common to multimodal composers across ﬁelds and are not, in them-

pants’ status as L2 users of English and prospective language teachers
mediated these decisions. Given their role, TESOL professionals likely
evaluate GenAI outputs as language, attending to register, ﬂuency,
accuracy, and audience intelligibility. In this way, their veriﬁcation
practices functioned as a form of professional linguistic monitoring, in
addition to fact-checking. In addition, several participants in our study
deferred to GenAI’s English as more ﬂuent than their own while insist-

a negotiation of linguistic authority and ownership of English particu-

Central to their decision to use tools was the DMC project itself.
Rather than using these tools to automate every process, the pre-
service teachers prioritized meaning-making, actively assessing whether
using the tools would help them achieve their intended aims. For
example, participants understood that websites should avoid large
blocks of text and that information should be presented in a multi-
modal, reader-friendly way. This led them to use GenAI tools to create
graphs and charts and to assist them in summarizing the information
for a more accessible presentation on their sites. At the same time,
they recognized the need for the “human touch” and aimed to ensure
human presence on the site. For this reason, they deliberately chose
not to use AI for certain aspects of their sites (e.g., videos, voice-overs,
and letter to the reader). As with other studies that have explored
DMC, the emphasis on combining semiotic modes to create meaning-
ful multimodal texts for a speciﬁc audience (Jiang, 2017; Jiang &
Hafner, 2024) stimulated the participants enough for them to use
GenAI selectively and strategically. This highlights a TESOL-relevant
point: participants’ GenAI decisions were mediated by the communica-
tive purposes and genre demands of producing an accessible teacher-
facing resource.

learning (Lo et al., 2024), our ﬁndings suggest that autonomy to
orchestrate GenAI within a meaningful DMC task can create condi-
tions for sustained engagement. The task required them to explore
different tools, research how to use them, and engage in trial and
error before integrating the tool into their DMC process. They were
also continuously comparing the efﬁcacy and performance of the tools

the process. When such conditions are in place, GenAI tools become
another resource that learners can draw on to achieve their project

Providing choice does not seem to lead to an overreliance on
GenAI but rather to a more conscious and reﬂective use of GenAI,
making agentive decisions based on the affordances, constraints, and
social meanings of these technologies in their educational context
(Godwin-Jones, 2024). Furthermore, AI reduced some of the technical
challenges of DMC, allowing them to focus on meaning-making
(Jiang, 2024) and maintain high involvement within the DMC process.
The ﬁndings support calls for a more open stance toward the use of
GenAI (Tan, Wang, & Xu, 2025). However, this should be combined
with training in the use of these tools, as well as raising awareness of
the complex issues involved in using GenAI as part of text-composing
processes, e.g., audience perceptions, learning opportunities, and ethi-
cal issues (bias, copyright) (Moorhouse et al., 2025). For teacher edu-
cation, this implies that scaffolding should not only introduce tools
but also provide guidance on orchestration practices (e.g., when to
delegate, how to verify, and how to preserve voice and audience con-
nection) and on ethical decision-making.
Professional Learning and Future Practice
The pre-service TESOL teachers in the study had never engaged in
a DMC project before, nor had they composed a website. They initially
conceptualized website building as a complex task that language
teachers could not do. However, their experience of the project chan-
ged this concept, with the teachers reporting that they learned trans-
ferable skills that they could use in their future teaching practice. As
Li ( 2020) mentions, it is unlikely that TESOL teachers will integrate
multimodal pedagogies into their teaching unless they have experi-
enced it themselves. The opportunity to work in groups and review
other groups’ websites allowed them to learn collaboratively. Indeed,
collaborative experimentation with GenAI, including the sharing of
prompts and the negotiation of tool use in group settings,

We found that the participants took an experiential approach to
using GenAI tools. They also used external resources to learn more
about GenAI and DMC. Given the wide range of GenAI tools and their
distinct affordances and interfaces, there is a clear need for users to
trial different tools and become familiar with their functionalities
(Kohnke et al., 2025; Moorhouse & Wong, 2025). Similarly, engaging
external resources, particularly social media, can provide TESOL
teachers with useful advice to assist them in using GenAI tools.
Through their self-exploration, they became more aware of potential
accuracy and performance issues with GenAI (e.g., bias, inaccuracies,
and spelling errors). This led the participants to adopt a more cau-
tious approach, affecting the tools they used and the tasks they worked
on with GenAI. By engaging in critical evaluation and ethical use of
GenAI during their DMC process, the participants demonstrated
awareness of the issues inherent in AI tools, questioned the authority
of AI-generated content to ensure accuracy, and reﬂected on the
implications for authenticity and audience engagement (Darvin, 2025).
This suggests that GenAI-assisted DMC can function as a situated con-
text for developing critical AI literacy alongside multimodal composing
competence, particularly when the output is a teacher-facing resource
that requires credibility and accessibility.
We were encouraged to ﬁnd that the participants showed positive
intentions to integrate AI and DMC into their future teaching prac-
tices. They saw utility in both AI and DMC in providing greater oppor-
tunities for language learning and increasing their future students’ AI
and multimodal literacies —skills they identiﬁed as essential for mod-
ern society. Notably, their reasoning was oriented speciﬁcally toward
language-learning outcomes, such as developing learners’ oral expres-
sion and integrating language skills into meaningful multimodal tasks.
This reﬂects the language-pedagogical lens through which they
appraised both GenAI and DMC. While most felt that composing web-
sites might be too large a project for their students, digital storytelling,
poster design, and video creation were all suggested as possibilities.
These positive ﬁndings support calls for greater attention to AI and
DMC in TESOL teacher education, especially if we wish for GenAI-
assisted DMC and other multimodal pedagogies to be integrated into
language teaching (Jiang & Hafner, 2024; Jiang & Lai, 2025).

CONCLUSION
The study provided valuable insights into how pre-service TESOL
teachers integrate GenAI into the DMC processes in an authentic, nat-
uralistic environment. The study showed that the decision to use AI
was mediated by a range of factors, with a commitment to creating a
socially relevant website being key. In summary, the study showed that
GenAI does not replace the social dimensions of literacy; rather, it is a
site for the negotiation of meaning and agency. The pre-service
teachers in our study appropriated GenAI as a resource within a
broader ecology of social, technological, and pedagogical practices —
thereby showing that the social practice view of literacy also encom-
passes the complexities and opportunities of the GenAI era.
By focusing on decision-making patterns under conditions of auton-
omy, the study extends TESOL research beyond tool adoption to dem-
onstrate how pre-service teachers selectively orchestrate GenAI in
relation to audience design, perceived tool capabilities, authenticity,
and responsibility. We believe that by integrating GenAI-assisted DMC
into teaching and learning for pre-service TESOL teachers, teacher
education programs can enrich their curricula while providing their
students with awareness of multimodal pedagogies well-suited to the
GenAI age (Jiang & Lai, 2025). The project described in this article
can serve as a reference for other TESOL education programs. Future
work could examine how such orchestration practices develop over
time and how they translate into classroom enactment. However, the
present study demonstrates the value of embedding GenAI within sub-
stantive, audience-oriented DMC tasks to support the development of
AI–multimodal–digital literacies and pedagogical reasoning in TESOL
teacher education.
