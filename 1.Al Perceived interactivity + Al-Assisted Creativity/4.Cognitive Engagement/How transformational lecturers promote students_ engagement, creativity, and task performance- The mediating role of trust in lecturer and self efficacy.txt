ABSTRACT
In today's fast changing and increasingly complex environments the promotion of ongoing employees' task and
contextual performance is an important issue. Thus, this longitudinal study investigates the impact of trans-
formationalteachingwhichisderivedfromtransformationalleadershipconceptonstudents'motivationalstates
(course-relatedself-efficacyandtrustinteacher)andsubsequentlyonstudents'increasedengagement,creativity
and task performance in higher education. The study (N=165) hypothesized positive changes in university
students' motivational states and performance criteria over a period of six weeks. Mediation analyses showed
that trust in lecturer but not students' course-related self-efficacy served as a mediator in the relationship be-
tween transformational teaching and an increased degree of study engagement, an increased degree of student
creativity, and task performance. Our results offer theoretical and practical implications for the development of
career relevant concepts and teaching in higher education.
1. Introduction
Today'semployeesfacehighlevelsofuncertaintyconcerningcareer
management and employability (Ng & Feldman, 2014). Thus, devel-

ployees (Guzman de & Choi, 2013). Besides task performance, organi-

Peiró, 2005), and financial returns (Xanthopoulou, Bakker, Demerouti,
& Schaufeli, 2009b). Employees' creative ideas and high performance

Patterson, 2016;Oldham & Cummings, 1996). Recently, the call for
these career relevant concepts has reached the domain of vocational
developmentandeducationalpsychology(Qureshi,Wall,Humphries,&
BahramiBalani,2016;Upadyaya&Salmela-Aro,2015).Practitionersas
well as researchers claim that we should foster engaged, creative, and
performance-orientedbehaviorattheworkplacebutalsoineducational

class sizes, and a more distant teacher-student relationship (Lowe &

their learning processes independently (Hassel & Ridout, 2018;
Murtagh, 2010). Thus, the question of how university lecturers can
support learning-related attitudes, skills and behavior is of critical im-
portance. Therefore, in our study we investigate how transformational
teaching–a promising teaching style derived from transformational
leadership concept–and its motivational impact predict changes in
students' engagement, creativity, and task performance over the course
of one semester.

We expect transformational teaching to predict study motivation in
higher education by addressing two central motivational states which
Hannah, Schaubroeck, and Peng (2016)describe as “reason-to” and
“can-do” motivations. More specifically, we propose trust in lecturer to
be the “reason-to motivation” and course-related self-efficacy to be the
“can-do-motivation” (Raub & Liao, 2012). In a further step, we expect
both motivational states to mediate the association between transfor-
mational teaching and three central student outcomes - study engage-
ment, student creativity, and task performance (seeFig. 1).
1.1.1. Transformational teaching and trust in lecturer
In the past, organizational research has already acknowledged the
important role of interpersonal trust for the functioning of individuals
and organizations (McAllister, 1995).Mayer, Davis, and Schoorman
(1995)propose a well-established model of trust defining trust as “the
willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party”
(p. 712). This vulnerability is based on the assumption that the other
partyseemstobetrustworthy,that isable,benevolent,andofintegrity
(Mayer et al., 1995). Furthermore, trust is often viewed as multi-
dimensional construct consisting of cognitive- and affect-based trust
(McAllister, 1995). In our study, we focus on affect-based trust as this
facet of trust is characterized by a caring relationship (Madjar & Ortiz-
Walters, 2009) which is also inherent in transformational teaching
behavior. Following social exchange theory, trusting relationships
emerge when one party acts benevolent and caring towards another
party thereby creating unspecified obligations in the future
(Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005). Thus, with individual consideration
being a core facet of transformational teaching, students will likely
perceive a transformational lecturer as caring and benevolent. This, in
turn, will foster students' trust in this lecturer and, in a further step,
motivate them to reciprocate this caring teaching style. Thus, based on
social exchange theory (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005), a lecturer will
provide students a “reason-to motivation” (Raub & Liao, 2012) as stu-
dents will feel obliged to his or her lecturer and therefore exert addi-
tional effort (Schaubroeck, Peng, & Hannah, 2013). Numerous studies
in leadership research have already shown that a transformational
leader isable toestablish trustin followers(Braunet al.,2013;Dirks&
Ferrin, 2002). Thus, analogously, we propose a positive relationship
between transformational teaching and students' trust in their lecturer.
Hypothesis 1.Transformational teaching predicts an increase in trust
in lecturer.
1.1.2. Transformational teaching and course-related self-efficacy
Inordertoexaminestudents'“can-do”motivationwefocusontheir
course-related self-efficacy. Self-efficacy is defined as the “beliefs in
one's capabilities to mobilize the motivation, cognitive resources, and
courses of action needed to meet given situational demands” (Wood &
Bandura, 1989).
Following social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1986), self-efficacy
emerges through four different ways: mastery experience, verbal per-
suasion, vicarious learning, and physiological arousal. We propose that
transformationallecturersprovidetheirstudentswithopportunitiesfor
mastery experience as they motivate students by formulating high ex-
pectations (Nielsen & Munir, 2009). Additionally, transformational
lecturers offer verbal persuasion by persuading students of their cap-
abilities to meet these expectations. By providing intellectual stimula-
tion, transformational lecturers help students to find new ways to deal
with challenges they encounter duringtheir studies, finally resulting in
higher levels of self-efficacy (Nielsen & Munir, 2009). By motivating
and supporting students individually, transformational lecturers most
likely help students to reduce anxiety arousal during challenging si-
tuations. Having successfully mastered challenging situations and the
respective physiological arousal will further strengthen students' self-
efficacy beliefs (Bandura, 1993).
In spite of this reasoning, research on the link between transfor-
mational leadership and self-efficacy has been inconclusive (Gong,
Huang,&Farh,2009;Hannahetal.,2016;Kark,Shamir,&Chen,2003;
Nielsen & Munir, 2009). One reason for these inconsistent results may
be the distinction between rather general versus more specific dimen-
sionsofself-efficacy.Contrarytostudiesmeasuringgeneralself-efficacy
(Nielsen & Munir, 2009), studies measuring specific facets of self-effi-
cacy, such as creative or role self-efficacy, have shown a positive re-
lationship between transformational leadership and self-efficacy
(Hannah et al., 2016). We therefore focus on the specific facet of
course-related self-efficacy and propose that transformational teaching
will increase students' course-related self-efficacy over time.
Hypothesis 2.Transformational teaching predicts an increase in
students' course-related self-efficacy.

1.2.3. Motivational states and task performance
In organizational research, performance is a multidimensional
construct that roughly divides into task and contextual performance
(Binnewies, Sonnentag, & Mojza, 2009b;Motowidlo, Borman, &
Schmit, 1997). Thereby, task performance is each individual's direct
contribution to the achievement of his or her work tasks and formal
organizational goals (Gang et al., 2011;Motowidlo et al., 1997). In
educational studies, the state-of-the-art indicator of academic task
performance is students' course grades (Bakker et al., 2015;Komarraju
& Nadler, 2013). Thus, we examine academic task performance by fo-
cusing on students' achievements in the respective course exam.
As argued before, students who have a trustful relationship with
their lecturer will have more cognitive resources available (Madjar &
Ortiz-Walters, 2009). Moreover, according to social exchange theory,
they are likely to put more effort into their studies (Schaubroeck et al.,
2013). Following this reasoning, students should be able to achieve
better learning results (i.e. better course grades). In organizational re-
search, the link between trust and performance is relatively well es-
tablished (Dirks & Ferrin, 2002;Madjar & Ortiz-Walters, 2009;
Schaubroeck et al., 2013). We therefore hypothesize that students' af-
fective trust in their lecturer predicts task performance and that trust
will mediate the association between transformational teaching and
task performance.

1.3. Overview of the empirical contribution
In the current study, we investigate the effects of transformational
teaching on increased study engagement, creativity, and task perfor-
mance via students' self-efficacy and trust in lecturer. To our knowl-
edge, this study is the first to examine transformational teaching in the
context of higher education. Yet, considering the specific challenges
studentsfaceinhighereducationandtheneedtodevelopemployability
capacitytransformationalteachingseemstobeanespeciallypromising
approach.
Following the framework byRaub and Liao (2012), as well as
Hannah et al. (2016), we expand previous literature by investigating
underlying motivational states linking transformational behavior to
beneficial follower outcomes in the context of higher education. We
assume that trust in lecturer and students' self-efficacy should be two
crucial mechanisms covering students' “reason-to” and “can-do” moti-
vation thereby linking transformational teaching to students' engage-
ment, creativity, and task performance.
Finally, we contribute to existing research by using a longitudinal
design. Thus, we address claims of existing research to conduct more
longitudinal studies (Ployhart & Vandenberg, 2010) and illustrate a
more dynamic relationship between transformational behavior and
motivation as well as performance. By focusing on the change within
the respective outcome variables (except students' course grades), we
areevenabletoeliminatereversecausationoreffectsofpotentialthird
variables (Binnewies, Sonnentag, & Mojza, 2009a;Zapf, Dormann, &
Frese, 1996).
2. Material and methods
2.1. Participants and Procedure
Participants were students of a large German university. They were
contacted via the university's student mailing list, via a mailing list of
students interested in psychological studies, via their participation in
the university's studentmentoring program, or approachedface-to-face
in central places on the campus. A prerequisite for participation was to
have at least one lecture which took place during the whole semester
with a single lecturer. To thank participants, students were offered to
obtain a profile of their learning patterns (Vermunt, 1994) including
feedback how to improve their learning strategies. Additionally, they
could win one of ten 25€-amazon vouchers. Concerning ethical prin-
ciples, we followed the guidelines of the Declaration of the World
Medical Association of Helsinki and the advice of the German Asso-
ciation of Psychology throughout the study and the respective institu-
tionalreviewboardapprovedthestudy.Thus,participantswereneither
stressed physically or mentally nor deceived during the research pro-
cess.Furthermore,allparticipantswereofage,participatedvoluntarily,
and were able to cancel participation in the study any time. Data were
collected anonymously.
Currently there is little knowledge about the appropriate timing of
measurement occasions in longitudinal studies (Ployhart &
Vandenberg, 2010). Therefore, we chose our measurement occasions
accordingtothefollowingconsiderations:Thefirstquestionnaire(Time
1) was administered three to four weeks after the beginning of the se-
mester, so that students had had the opportunity to get to know their
lecturers and were able to judge their teaching style. Initially, in the
first questionnaire students were asked to report all the lectures with a
single lecturer they attained during the semester. By random selection
one of the lectures was chosen by the computer system and students
had to fill in all questionnaires referring to this lecture. The first
questionnaire also contained demographic variables, predictor, med-
iator, and outcome variables. The second measurement occasion (Time
2) which contained mediators and outcomes was assessed approxi-
mately six weeks after Time 1, at the end of the semester. We assumed
six weeks to be enough time for a transformational teacher to build
trust and increased course-related self-efficacy as well as increased
engagement and creativity. Finally, participants were asked to report
the grade they obtained in the exam of the lecture at Time 3, a few
weeksaftertheendofsemester.Intotal,thefinalsampleinTime1and
Time 2 consisted of 165 students with 26.1% being male and 73.9%
being female. We controlled for students' gender in the later analyses
because of this unproportioned gender distribution. Students' average
agewas22.58(SD=6.03)andtheyalreadyhadbeenstudyingfor3.49
(SD=2.44) semesters. Students of different faculties took part in the
study with 24% being from the field of humanities and cultural sci-
ences, 43% being from the department of social and economic sciences
or being a law student, and 33% being from the domain of natural
sciences or medicine. In the end, 78 students of this sample also par-
ticipated at Time 3. MANOVAS conducted for a dropout analysis re-
vealed no significant differences with respect to all study variables as-
sessedatTime1betweentheparticipantsatTime1andTime2andthe
students that took part at Time 3.
2.2. Measures
All questionnaires were administered online via Unipark. All mea-
sures have been previously validated and should show good psycho-
metric properties as they were previously used in much-cited articles.
All items were in German and retrieved by using either the back
translation method (Brislin, Lonner, & Thorndike, 1973) for the scales
developed originally in English or by assessing scales already used in
international publications with German samples. Participants rated all
measuresona5-pointLikertscalerangingfrom1(totallydisagree)to5
(totally agree).

Beauchamp et al. (2010)and showed good psychometric properties in
other studies (Beauchamp et al., 2011;Beauchamp et al., 2014). The
scale pertains the following four dimensions: individualized con-
sideration, inspirational motivation, idealized influence, and in-
tellectual stimulation with four items, respectively. A sample item for
intellectual stimulation is “My lecturer encourages me to look at issues

2.2.2. Trust in lecturer
We used four items (Time 1: α=.87, Time 2: α=.87) of
McAllister's (1995)scale of affect-based trust which we adapted to the
student context tomeasure students'trust in their lecturer.We selected
the items according to their factor loadings and content fit. The scale

2.2.3. Course-related self-efficacy
We assessed course-related self-efficacy with the New General Self-
Efficacy Scale (NGSE) byChen, Gully, and Eden (2001)consisting of
eight items (Time 1: α=.94, Time 2: α=.95), which we adapted to
the context of a single student course. Previous studies have already
applied the scale in specific contexts (Neff et al., 2013) and obtained
good reliability. A sample item is “When facing difficult tasks in this
course, I am certain that I will accomplish them”.
2.2.4. Study engagement
Study engagement was assessed with the 9-item (Time 1: α=.92,
Time 2: α=.93) Schoolwork Engagement Inventory (Salmela-Aro &
Upadaya, 2012). This instrument is based on the short Utrecht Work
Engagement Scale originally developed bySchaufeli (2006), validated
intheeducationalcontext.Thescalehassuccessfullybeenusedinprior
studies (Salmela-Aro & Upadyaya, 2014;Upadyaya & Salmela-Aro,
2015) and measures the three sub dimensions vigor (e.g., “Within this
lecture, I am bursting with energy.”), dedication (e.g., “I am en-
thusiastic about my studies within this lecture.”), and absorption (e.g.,
“Time flies when I am studying within this lecture.”) with three items
each.
2.2.5. Student creativity
We used four of the thirteen items (Time 1: α=.88, Time 2:
α=.90) of an established creativity measure (George & Zhou, 2001).
We chosetherespective fouritemsbased ontheir fittotheeducational
contextandfurtheradaptedthemtoassesscreativitywithinthelecture.
Previous research has often used the scale and adapted it to specific
contexts (Aleksić, Černe, Dysvik, & Škerlavaj, 2016;Amundsen &
Martinsen, 2015;Boon, Vangrieken, & Dochy, 2016). A sample item is
“I often have new and innovative ideas within this lecture”.
2.2.6. Task performance
Students' course grades are a commonly used as objective perfor-
mance criterion (Bakker et al., 2015). Following previous research, we
usedself-reportstoassessstudents'coursegradesatTime3(Komarraju,
Ramsey, & Rinella, 2013). Course grades differed from 1 (outstanding)
to 5 (not succeeded). As course grades are highly dependent of the
department (Achen & Courant, 2009;Beenstock & Feldman, 2018),
students' grades were weighted around the average grade of the re-
spective study domain in order to gain comparable results.
2.2.7. Control variables
We controlled for students' sex as prior research showed gender to
haveanimpactonacademicperformance(Buchmann&DiPrete,2006).
For the same reason we also included age and study domain (Achen &
Courant,2009;Beenstock&Feldman,2018)ascontrolvariablesforthe
analyses. With regard to study domain, we divided students in groups
from the field of humanities and cultural sciences, social and economic
sciencesorbeingalawstudent,andthedomainofnaturalsciences.For
theanalyseswhichcontainedtheoutcomeoftaskperformanceweonly
included students' age and gender as control variables as study domain
was used to weight students' course grades. Note however that results
also stayed the same without the inclusion of the respective controls.
2.3. Statistical analysis
To test changes in our mediator variables (trust in lecturer and
course-relatedself-efficacy)aswellaschangesinouroutcomevariables
(study engagement and student creativity) we used hierarchical re-
gression analyses controlling for the respective Time 1 measures. This
means that further predictors entered in the model explain variance in
the difference between Time 1 and Time 2. Specifically, for all of these
analyseswecomparedthefollowingtwomodels:Model1containedthe
descriptive controls and the respective Time 1 measures of mediators
and outcomes. In Model 2, we entered the respective predictors. We
relied on hierarchical linear modelling and avoided structuralequation
modellingbecauseoftherelativelysmallsamplesizeof165studentsin
ourstudywhichdoesnotmeetprevalentsamplesizerecommendations
for structural equation modelling in literature (Kline, 2011;Nunnaly,
1967).
Furthermore,weusedabias-correctedbootstrapprocedurewiththe
PROCESS macro for SPSS byPreacher and Hayes (2008)with 10000
resamplestotesttheindirecteffectsofHypotheses6a,b,cand7a,b,c.
We obtained similar results calculating all analyses for each mediator
variable separately.

4. Discussion
In the current study we examined transformational leadership style
in the context of higher education. We were particularly interested in
theimpactoftransformationalteachingonstudents'motivationalstates
and an increased degree of positive student outcomes, namely study
engagement, student creativity, and task performance over time.
Thereby, we shed more light on the motivational mediating mechan-
isms, increased trust in lecturer and increased course-related self-effi-
cacy.
Our results confirm the prediction that transformational teaching
relates to increased trust in lecturer. In a further step, as hypothesized,
an increased level of trust in lecturer in turn predicted study engage-
ment, student creativity, and objective task performance. As predicted,
increased trust in lecturer mediated the relationship between transfor-
mational teaching and study engagement, student creativity, and task
performance. Contradictory, our results did not support a positive re-
lationship between transformational teaching and increased course-re-
lated self-efficacy. Nevertheless, as hypothesized, increased course-re-
lated self-efficacy predicted an increased degree of study engagement
and increased student creativity, whereas contrary to our prediction,
increased course-related self-efficacy did not predict task performance.
Furthermore, our results did not support increased course-related self-
efficacy as a mediating mechanism in the association between trans-
formational teaching and study engagement, student creativity, and
task performance. Below, we discuss these results and their theoretical
and practical implications.

4.1. Theoretical implications
Our finding of the relationship between transformational teaching
and increased trust in lecturer is in line with existing research on
transformationalleadershipandfollowertrust(Braunetal.,2013;Dirks
& Ferrin, 2002). Likewise, our results which show that an increased
level of trust in lecturer in turn predicts changes in relevant student
performancecriteria,namelystudyengagement,studentcreativity,and
task performance are in line with existing organizational research
(Chughtaietal.,2015;Madjar&Ortiz-Walters,2009).Furthermore,we
could identify increased trust in lecturer as a potential “reason-to”
(Hannah et al., 2016;Raub & Liao, 2012) motivational mediator in the
relationship between transformational teaching and beneficial student
outcomes.Therefore,wecontributetoexistingresearchinseveralways:
First,byaddingtoexistingresearchontransformationalleadershipand
initial results of transformational teaching in the school context, to the
best of our knowledge, our study is the first to successfully apply
transformational teaching in the context of higher education. As stu-
dents in higher education not only face challenges as higher workload,
larger class sizes and more distant teacher-student relationships in
comparisontoschool(Lowe &Cook,2013)butalsoneedtolearnmore
independently (Hassel & Ridout, 2018;Murtagh, 2010), transforma-
tional teaching seems to be a promising teaching style especially in
higher education. Accordingly, we could show that transformational
behaviorinthecontextofhighereducationpredictspositivechangesin
motivationalandperformance-relatedstudentcriteria.Theseconstructs
in turn are crucial for them to succeed in education and later life time
(Craft & Jeffrey, 2008).
Next, byemploying students'course gradesas an objective measure
oftaskperformanceweaddressexistingclaimstoreducesubjectivebias
in the measurement of outcome variables (Dvir, Eden, Avolio, &
Shamir,2002).Furthermore,weunderpinandextendtheoretical(Raub
& Liao, 2012) and empirical propositions (Hannah et al., 2016) con-
cerning the role of motivation as a mediating mechanism in the re-
lationship between transformational teaching and beneficial outcomes.
We thereby contribute to the understanding of how transformational
teaching works, and to the comprehension of the role that trust in
lecturer plays as a motivational state. More specifically, we focused on
affective trust which corresponds to transformational behavior such as
individual consideration (Madjar & Ortiz-Walters, 2009). In addition,
especially this emotional bond between leader and follower via affec-
tive trust seems to be crucial for social exchange and a psychological
safe environment (Schaubroeck et al., 2011). Social exchange and a
psychological safe environment in turn provide the basis for high en-
gagement, performance, and creativity.
Finally, we contribute to previous research by applying a long-
itudinal design that allows us to illustrate a more dynamic relationship
between our study variables and to make predictions of change over
time.Researchoftenhastestedmediatingprocesseswithcross-sectional
designs (Maxwell & Cole, 2007). Yet, cross-sectional approaches fail to
representlongitudinalprocesses,suchasmediations,becausetheytend
to over- or underestimate longitudinal effects (Maxwell & Cole, 2007).
Thus, our study successfully integrates a temporal perspective as im-
pliedinmediation.Furthermore,byusingrepeatedmeasurementofour
mediator and outcome variables we are able to eliminate reverse cau-
sation or effects of potential third variables (Binnewies et al., 2009a;
Zapf et al., 1996).
Concerning our second mediating mechanism–course-related self-
efficacy as a “can-do” motivation–our data show mixed results. On the
one hand, we were able to establish a link between increased course-
related self-efficacy and increased study engagement and creativity.
Thesefindingsareinlinewithpreviousresearch(Neffetal.,2013;Rego
et al., 2012) and additionally extend existing findings by making pre-
dictions of change over time. However, our data did not support our
hypotheses regarding a significant relationship between increased
course-related self-efficacy and our third performance criterion,
students' course grades (i.e., the objective performance measure). One
reason for this might be that attaining good grades in an exam is in-
fluenced by a different kind of self-efficacy, such as performance self-
efficacy,ratherthancourse-relatedself-efficacy.Tosuccessfullypassan
exam,studentsarenotonlyrequiredtoshowself-efficacyregardingthe
coursecontents,buttheyalsohavetofeelconfidentthattheycanrecall
their knowledge at the exact moment and that they are fast enough to
accomplish the test in the predetermined time. A study on preparatory
self-efficacy (relates to the context in which a student prepares for the
later exam performance) and performance self-efficacy could demon-
strate that performance self-efficacy, but not preparatory self-efficacy,
predicted students' performance in an exam (Hanks & Beier, 2012).
Furthermore, transformational teaching was neither related to an
increased level of course-related self-efficacy, nor did course-related
self-efficacy reveal to be a “can-do” motivational state mediating the
relationship between transformational teaching and students' in-role
and extra-role performance. Previous research on transformational
leadershipandself-efficacyshowedinconclusiveresultsintermsofthis
relationship. Whereas researchers likeKark et al. (2003)could not es-
tablishalinkbetweentransformationalleadershipandself-efficacyina
cross-sectional design,Hannah et al. (2016)even confirmed the re-
lationship between transformational leadership and increased self-effi-
cacyovertime.Contrarytothelatterstudy,theperiodbetweenthefirst
and the second measurement occasion in our study (six weeks) might
havebeen tooshort foratransformationalteachertoenhancestudents'
course-related self-efficacy. Self-efficacy is a rather trait-like construct
and the time interval of fourteen weeks in the study ofHannah et al.
(2016)might have been more appropriate to foster change in self-ef-
ficacy.
Albeit, our results did not support course-related self-efficacy as a
mediating mechanism, trust in lecturer explained the relationship be-
tweentransformationalteachingandstudentoutcomesasa“reason-to”
motivation.Thefactthatincreasedtrustinlecturerservesasamediator
over and above course-related self-efficacy also demonstrates the ro-
bustness of this mediating mechanism.
4.2. Limitations and future research directions
Onelimitationofthecurrentstudyisthatweonlyusedself-reported
data which can increase the possibility of same source bias (Podsakoff,
MacKenzie,Lee,&Podsakoff,2003).However,wetriedtoreducesame
source bias by using a longitudinal design with two measurement oc-
casions and a third one for assessing students' grades (Podsakoff et al.,
2003). Therefore, we were able to temporally separate the measure-
mentoftransformationalteachingandmediatorandoutcomevariables.
In addition, we even made predictions of change over time by mea-
suringmediatorandoutcomevariablesatthebeginningofthesemester
and six weeks later. Furthermore, by integrating a more objective
performance measure including students' course grades at Time 3 we
were able to reduce shared variance of students' self-reported variables
and task performance. Nevertheless, future studies could integrate a
third measurement occasion that also separates the assessment of
mediator variables and self-reported outcomes, creativity and engage-
ment. Additionally, one could include important sources such as the
perspectiveoftherespectiveteachersortheorganizationalperspective.
For instance, it might be interesting to take self-other ratings with re-
spect to transformational teaching into account as both perspectives
mostlikelyprovideadditionalinformation(Vazire&Mehl,2008).Asto
the approach of data analysis, future studies could apply structural
equation modelling to test this study's model as a whole using larger
sample sizes.
Other limitations are the problem of dropouts from the first and
second to the third measurement occasion and the larger number of
femalethanmaleparticipantsinoursample.Regardingdropouts,other
studies with longitudinal designs report similar dropout rates of up to
50% or more (Ployhart & Vandenberg, 2010). Additionally, we

conducted a dropout analysis with respect to all study variables to as-

Inthecurrentstudy,welookedattheunderlyingmechanismsofthe

beneficial student outcomes. For example, students' goal orientation
(Gong et al., 2009) might work as a moderator. We assume that
transformationalteachingunfoldsitspotentialespeciallywhenstudents
have a high learning goal instead of a high performance goal orienta-

Considering the many ways trust has been conceptualised in prior
research (Dirks & Ferrin, 2002) future studies could integrate a more
comprehensive picture of trust as a mediating mechanism in the re-
lationship between transformational teaching and student outcomes.
Thereby, it would be interesting to investigate the additive effects of
cognition-based and affect-based trust (Schaubroeck et al., 2011) as
well as different effects on outcomes. One might speculate that cogni-
tive trust could have a stronger impact on performance (Yang,
Mossholder, & Peng, 2009) while affect based trust may be more
strongly relatedtoaffectiveoutcomessuchas engagement.Inaddition,
itwouldbeinterestingtoinvestigatethelinkbetweentransformational
teaching not only on the individual but also on the team level by in-
cluding trust between students and its impact on student outcomes
(Braun et al., 2013).
Finally, it could be interesting to investigate effects of transforma-
tional teaching on self-efficacy not only in lectures across all depart-
ments but specifically in courses with high drop-out rates such as en-
gineering and natural science (Heublein, 2014). Prior research found
low initial self-efficacy to be one major reason for drop-out (Georg,
2009). One could assume that future studies might find a link between
transformational teaching and self-efficacy especially for courses in
these respective departments as transformational behavior might be
especially apt to address students with low self-efficacy.
4.3. Practical implications
Resultsofourstudycanalsoprovidepractitionerswithfirstinsights
regardinghowtofosterstudents'motivationandsubsequentlystudents'
study engagement, creativity, and task performance in higher educa-
tion.Thismighthelptopreparestudentsfortheirlaterprofessionallife.
First, lecturers in higher education should show their individualized
consideration for their course participants, for example by answering
students'questionsorbyprovidingconstructivefeedback.Furthermore,
lecturers should stimulate and challenge their students by encouraging
them to take different perspectives on a subject or by setting specific,
challenging, but manageable goals. Finally, lecturers should be aware
oftheirfunctionasarolemodelandreflectandthusregulatetheirown
behavior.
In addition, organizational research showed that training can foster
transformational leadership effectively by applying self-reflection
methods, role play, and group discussions with a focus on transforma-
tional behavior (e.g. goal setting) (Barling, Weber, & Kelloway, 1996;
Dvir et al., 2002). Thus, transformational teaching training could be a
valuable component of further education of university lecturers, high
school teachers, and other lecturers who offer courses (e.g., teachers of
language courses).
5. Conclusion
To sum up, our results indicate that transformational teaching can
enhance students' in-role as well as extra-role performance criter-
ia–namely study engagement, student creativity, and task
performance–over time in the context of higher education through in-
creasedtrustinlecturer.Thus,increasedtrustinlecturerseemstoserve
asa“reason-to”motivationinthisrelationship.Wethereforecontribute
to existing research by pointing out that positive transformational be-
havior can be applied to the university context and importantly con-
tributes to beneficial student outcomes over time. Our findings suggest
transformationalteachingtobeapromisingavenueforstudents'in-role
and extra-role performance in higher education.
