   ABSTRACT
Students’ perceived competencies may reflect dispositions to trigger and 
enhance engagement acts and promote engagement benefits. Although 
perceived competencies and engagement acts are significant resources in 
STEM learning, their relationships remain unclear. Accordingly, we applied 
the transactional model of engagement (TME) to examine these associations, 
focusing on how emotional and cognitive engagement affect the mediating 
effect of perceived digital inquiry on perceived creativity. The participants 
were 305 secondary school STEM learners in Hong Kong. Mediation analyses 
showed that emotional engagement mediated the positive relationship 
between perceived digital inquiry and creativity, whereas cognitive engage -
ment had no such mediating effect.
  Introduction
 Individuals’ perceptions of their creativity are considered to have significant effects on improve -
ments in their creative performance, problem-solving, and academic achievement (Anderson & 
Haney,  2021 ; Zhou et al.,  2014 ). As creativity is a critical learning outcome of STEM education 
(Weng, Chiu, & Jong,  2022 ; Honey et al.,  2014 ), we regard STEM learning as a potential envi -
ronment that cultivates the factors associated with student creativity. For instance, in the context 
of STEM learning, students’ self-related factors including perceived digital inquiry and engagement 
with learning tasks are essential agents of creativity development (de Jong et al.,  2021 ;  Yang 
et al.,  2020 ). Fostering this development requires the process of intentionally gaining new insights 
and different ideas through existing information (Pande & Bharathi,  2020 ). In the process, stu -
dents feel empowered to develop more ideas when embedding digital technologies into STEM 
education (Weng, Cui, et al.,  2022 ; Weng, Chiu, & Tsang,  2022 ).
 The digital technologies create more authentic learning and assessment and can better engage 
students in inquiry-based learning (Lindgren, & Johnson-Glenberg,  2013 ). For example, the 
students who perceive stronger self-efficacy in using digital tools for inquiry-based learning 
(referred to as perceived digital inquiry) are likely to have more new ideas and feel they can 
create something new. Moreover, student engagement is the key to creativity development in 
STEM education (Weng, Chiu, & Tsang, 2022). Student engagement is multifaceted with major 
dimensions including behavioral, emotional, and cognitive. Emotional and cognitive engagement 
are relatively more influential on student creativity development (Orkibi & Ram-Vlasov,  2019 ). 
Students engaging in fun and interesting learning environments (i.e., emotional engagement) are 
more curious, and always want to learn and see STEM problems from multiple perspectives 
(Gallagher & Grimm,  2018 ). Students who are willing and able to take on challenges (cognitive 
engagement) keep improving their ideas when solving STEM problems (Guan et al.,  2021 ).

Accordingly, how students perceive their digital inquiry competence, and emotional and 
cognitive engagement in learning could affect their creative thinking in STEM education. It is 
necessary to understand how these factors relate to creativity. This gap is supported by recent 
studies advocating that student perceptions of digital inquiry, emotional engagement, and cog -
nitive engagement have received growing attention in STEM education research (Händel et al., 
 2022 ; Howard et al.,  2016 ). However, most of the engagement-related previous studies in STEM 
education used the different dimensions of student engagement as the final outcomes (Chiu, 
 2022 ; Chiu et al.,  2022 ; Hall & Miro,  2016 ; Struyf et al.,  2019 ). Very few studies used emotional 
and cognitive engagement as mediators. Furthermore, most related studies that have investigated 
perceived competencies and engagement were conducted in higher education or non-interdisciplinary 
contexts (Y ang et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2014). Student engagement is an important mediator in 
adolescent learning (Chiu, 2022 ; Chiu et al., 2022 ). Therefore, the generalizability of the findings 
of these studies to STEM learning in K-12 settings merits exploration (Lawson & Lawson,  2013 ).
The transactional model of engagement (TME) offers a framework to explain the relationships 
between various perceived competencies that lead to and benefit from engagement acts (Lawson 
& Lawson, 2013). Accordingly, this study investigates creativity in digital learning environments 
through STEM school education from a different perspective—student engagement as mediators. 
It uses the TME as the theoretical framework to (i) investigate the direct associations between 
perceived digital inquiry, emotional engagement, cognitive engagement, and perceived creativity 
and (ii) examine the mediating effects of emotional and cognitive engagement on the association 
between perceived digital inquiry and creativity.
  The proposed research model
This section explains what the TME is and proposes a research model with hypotheses.
  The TME framework and engagement-related studies
The TME framework provides a transactional view of student engagement. In this view, the 
engagement process consists of four main components. They are acts of engagement, benefits 
of engagement, dispositions for engagement, and conditions/contexts of engagement. Each of 
these components is linked by a temporal dimension. First, act of engagement refers to the 
various states of experience of individuals. It states how students’ experiences lead to their states 
such as joy, fun, boredom, and disinterest (Bempechat & Shernoff,  2012  ; Pekrun & 
Linnenbrink-Garcia, 2012). This component includes behavioral, cognitive and emotional engage -
ment. Second, the idea of benefits of engagement refers to the outcomes of engagement-related 
acts. The quality of experiences directly determines whether students receive the benefits from 
each of the acts. The benefits are seen as student proximal outcomes; they are either obscure 
or solid, such as feelings, belongings, competencies, academic achievements, and participation. 
Third, the notion of dispositions for engagement refers to students’ perceptions of the “will” 
and “skill” they bring to an activity, including motivation, interests, self-directed learning, and 
language proficiency. Students’ prior learning experiences are influential factors important to 
these perceptions (Taines,  2012 ). Finally, an engagement disposition is developmental, which is 
highly influenced by engagement’s surrounding conditions, cultures, and contexts. These ideas 
are conditions/contexts of engagement. Lawson and Lawson ( 2013) state that the TME is a new 
and contemporary conceptual framework for student engagement research and practice.
K-12 students are adolescents, and their engagement is the key to their STEM learning as its 
multifaceted nature affects their outcomes in different ways. However, most of the engagement-related 
studies use the different dimensions of engagement as final outcomes, particularly in STEM edu -
cation (Chiu, 2022; Chiu et al., 2022; Hall & Miro, 2016; Struyf et al., 2019). Very few used different 
dimensions of engagement as mediators explaining relations between perceived competencies and 
learning outcomes. This gap can be addressed by the TME. As in the current study, participants

Based on the TME, we propose that positively perceived digital inquiry competencies can trigger

defines each of the constructs and discusses the proposed research model for this study.
  Perceived digital inquiry
Perceived digital inquiry is defined as students’ perceptions of their competencies in using digital 
tools to conduct inquiry activities in various learning contexts (de Jong et al., 2021). Some STEM 
education studies have suggested that students tend to perceive stronger digital inquiry when they 
have opportunities to explore or interact with technological tools (Bruce & Casey,  2012 ; de Jong 
et al., 2021). Perceiving stronger digital inquiry usually implies successful interactions or experiences 
with digital learning environments, resulting in more positive dispositions toward learning institutions 
such as schools (Porat et al.,  2018 ). According to the TME, perceived digital inquiry can be regarded 
as an engagement disposition that fosters students’ acts of engagement (Lawson & Lawson, 2013).
  Emotional and cognitive engagement
In the TME, acts of engagement indicate the various states that students can experience, from 
flow-like experiences to feelings of boredom or disinterest, as they participate in learning activities 
(Lawson & Lawson, 2013 ). This study focuses on acts of emotional engagement and cognitive 
engagement, which are crucial psychological resources for student learning (Pietarinen et al.,  2014 ). 
Emotional engagement concerns individuals’ affection during learning activities, which involves 
both positive and negative emotions (Ninaus et al.,  2019 ). Cognitive engagement reflects students’ 
willingness to make efforts to understand learning content and master skills (Li et al.,  2021 ). These 
two types of engagement interact with each other (Chiu,  2021 , 2022 ; Chiu et al., 2022 ;  Dubovi & 
Tabak, 2021 ; Hong et al.,  2020 ). Moreover, they are significant resources that facilitate the creative 
process of student learning (Álvarez-Huerta et al.,  2021 ; Sun,  2020 ). As creativity is a major out -
come of STEM activities (Honey et al., 2014 ), emotional engagement and cognitive engagement 
have received increasing research attention in STEM-related areas (Fredricks et al.,  2018 ).  
  Perceived creativity
 Studies have categorized creativity in terms of two constructs: self-perceptions of creativity (i.e., 
perceived creativity; Silvia et al.,  2012 ) and domain-specific creativity (i.e., creativity performance; 
Barbot,  2020 ). Perceived creativity refers to the creative skills that students believe they possess. 
This perception indicates how learners think of their abilities to generate ideas that are inno -
vative and useful (Kaufman et al.,  2016 ; Laguía et al.,  2019 ). Kaufman ( 2019 ), Laguía et al. ( 2019 ), 
and Silvia et al. ( 2012) suggested that perceived creativity can foster creative performance.

Two types of self-rated factors that influence individuals’ judgments about their efforts and 
accomplishments relating to creativity have been identified (Kaufman et al., 2016). The first 
concerns being aware of one’s own strengths and weaknesses related to creativity, both within 
a specific area (e.g., perceived digital inquiry) and as a general attribute. The other concerns 
knowing when, where, how, and why to be creative by considering the social contextual interplay 
(i.e., engagement interactions with the learning tasks). Both self-rated factors interact with each 
other in the development of creativity (Fischer et al.,  2019 ; Richter et al.,  2012 ). Therefore, 
research on perceived creativity should simultaneously consider these two factors. In this study, 
we emphasize the interactions between the two self-rated factors by examining how perceived 
digital inquiry and emotional and cognitive engagement with learning tasks explain perceived 
creativity in STEM learning.
  Relationships between perceived digital inquiry, emotional engagement, 
cognitive engagement, and perceived creativity
Studies have reported associations between perceived digital inquiry, emotional engagement, 
cognitive engagement, and perceived creativity. Perceived digital inquiry has been shown to have 
large positive direct effects on students’ emotional and cognitive engagement (Marty et al.,  2013 ; 
Wu & Wu,  2020 ). For instance, a survey-based study of 605 secondary school students in Taiwan 
revealed that the students’ perceptions of their inquiry-related characteristics (e.g., curiosity) 
were associated with different dimensions of engagement in a technology-facilitated science 
laboratory context (Wu & Wu, 2020).
 Many studies have demonstrated that emotional engagement and cognitive engagement are 
related bidirectionally. For instance, Chiu et al. ( 2012 ) analyzed the Program for International 
Student Assessment (PISA) data of immigrant children in 41 countries and reported that the 
cognitive and emotional components of the students’ engagement were interconnected. Dubovi 
and Tabak (2021 ) analyzed 14,700 trending Y ouTube videos about science topics and found that 
the public’s emotional engagement and cognitive engagement in learning science via social media 
were interrelated. Additionally, Hong et al. ( 2020) conducted a study of 789 Chinese primary 
school students in which they distributed a mathematics engagement scale three times over a 
1.5-year intervention. Their findings suggest that students’ cognitive and emotional engagement 
influences each other over time.
Moreover, perceived digital inquiry, emotional engagement, and cognitive engagement have 
direct impacts on students’ self-perceived creativity. Some scholars have provided evidence that 
students’ perceived digital inquiry and creativity are positively associated. For example, Hwang 
et al. ( 2018 ) collected the perceptions of 658 high school students from Taiwan and found a 
positive relationship between the students’ inquiry-related use of mobile phones and learning 
behaviors and their tendency to engage in creativity. Furthermore, many researchers have reported 
a positive relationship between students’ engagement and perceived creativity. Wang et al. ( 2022 ) 
surveyed 542 postgraduate students in China and found that learning engagement involving 
both emotional and cognitive components had a positive effect on the respondents’ self-reported 
creativity. Similarly, in a study of 775 Spanish university students, Álvarez-Huerta et al. ( 2021) 
investigated the association between the participants’ engagement, which had a cognitive focus, 
and their creative confidence beliefs. They found that students with higher engagement were 
more likely to have stronger creativity perceptions.
 In addition, several studies have reported the mediating effects of engagement on the asso -
ciation between students’ digital/media experience and their creativity development. For instance, 
Malik et al. ( 2020 ) collected survey data from 334 undergraduate students in Chinese universities 
and found that their intrinsic motivation, representing their emotional engagement, mediated

the association between digital inquiry-related behavior and creativity. Likewise, Sun ( 2020) 
conducted a survey of 652 undergraduate students in Chinese public universities and found that 
the students’ cognitive engagement (i.e., time- and energy-related efforts) partially mediated the 
relationship between digital inquiry-related behavior and self-reported creativity. These studies 
suggest that both emotional engagement and cognitive engagement play mediating roles in the 
relationship between perceived digital inquiry and creativity.
 Although researchers have reported that perceived digital inquiry has strong associations with 
emotional and cognitive engagement (Chiu & Churchill,  2015 ; Chiu et al.,  2020 ; Marty et al., 
2013; Wu & Wu, 2020) and these associations can foster perceived creativity (Álvarez-Huerta 
et al., 2021; Hwang et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2022), few studies have examined the relationships 
between the engagement components of the TME in authentic educational practice. It is import -
ant to investigate the associations between these variables (i.e., engagement dispositions, acts, 
and benefits) from the perspectives of theoretical application and development. The aim of this 
study was to investigate these associations.
 Based on the studies described above, the following hypotheses are proposed (see  Figure 2   
for a visual representation):
•   H1: There is a positive association between perceived digital inquiry and emotional 
engagement.
•   H2: There is a positive association between perceived digital inquiry and cognitive 
engagement.
•   H3: There is a positive association between perceived digital inquiry and perceived 
creativity.
•   H4: There is a bidirectional association between emotional engagement and cognitive 
engagement.
•   H5: There is a positive association between emotional engagement and perceived 
creativity.
•   H6: There is a positive association between cognitive engagement and perceived 
creativity.
•   H7: Perceived digital inquiry is positively associated with perceived creativity via emotional 
engagement.
•   H8: Perceived digital inquiry is positively associated with perceived creativity via cognitive 
engagement.

  Measures in the questionnaire
The first part of the questionnaire collected demographic information such as the students’ 
gender and school name. The second part consisted of items investigating perceived digital 
inquiry, emotional engagement, cognitive engagement, and perceived creativity.
The questionnaire included four measures, all of which were measured using 5-point Likert 
scale items. The reliability and validity of the items are specified below. All the items were 
translated into Chinese and checked by three teachers from the participating schools to ensure 
that they were readable and understandable.
 The measure of perceived digital inquiry was evaluated using items adapted from a study by 
Ng ( 2012 ), who used self-perception measures to explore students’ digital literacy, with original 
Cronbach’s alpha values α = .88, including acceptable reliability. These items were tested in the 
study of the Chiu ( 2022) and Chiu et al. ( 2022) with similar background participants. They were 
modified to fit the context of STEM education. The items for this dimension were “I am able 
to use technology to test scientific explanations in my STEM project, ” “I am able to search and 
combine various Internet resources to conduct scientific investigations, ” and “I am able to use 
different technologies to research scientific topics” .
 The measures of emotional engagement were evaluated using items adapted from the study 
of Skinner et al. ( 2009 ), with acceptable reliability (α = . 82). The items were tested in the study 
of the Chiu ( 2022) and Chiu et al. ( 2022) with similar background participants who had taken 
part in online learning from different aspects. They were modified to fit our STEM education 
context. The items for emotional engagement were “When doing STEM projects, I feel like I’m 
doing something important to me, ” “I love having more time to work on my STEM projects, ” 
and “I like to use my free time to think about my STEM projects” . Moreover, the items for 
cognitive engagement were adapted from Wang et al. ( 2022) that validated and verified the items 
to measure high school students’ cognitive engagement in science and mathematics, with accept -
able reliability (α > .75). The items fitted our participants’ education level and subject domains 
and were tested in the Hong Kong context (Chiu, 2022; Chiu et al., 2022). They were “I am 
able to participate in different types of learning activities to make a good STEM project, ” “I am 
able to complete the tasks planned for the STEM project promptly, ” and “I am able to enrich 
my knowledge by talking to other people about STEM projects” .
 The measure of perceived creativity was evaluated using items adapted from a study by Tan 
et al. ( 2008 ), who investigated students’ creativity self-efficacy, with α =.80 (i.e., acceptable 
reliability). We adapted the items for our STEM education context. The items for this dimen -
sion were “I am able to come up with some innovative approaches or questions, ” “I can apply

  Data collection procedure
We obtained the participants’ consent before distributing the two-part questionnaire. The ques -
tionnaire was distributed to the participants via an online platform with the help of the STEM 
subject coordinators of the schools. The students were asked to submit a completed questionnaire 
to the researcher within one week after it was distributed. The coordinators reminded the par -
ticipants to respond to the items with close attention and to answer all the questions.
  Results 
  Construct validity and reliability
This study is the first to adapt a scale involving the different perceived competencies and 
engagement acts of STEM digital learners to the Hong Kong context. We tested whether the 
factor loadings were consistent to validate their use in this context.
Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using maximum likelihood estimation in Mplus 8.3 was 
used to verify the scale structure and check whether the data were a satisfactory fit for the 
measurement model. Various indices were used to evaluate the overall model fit (Hair,  2010 ). 
The goodness-of-fit statistics for the scale suggested that the four-factor model fitted the data 
well (χ2 = 118.82, df = 48, χ2/df = 2.48, CFI = .98, TLI = .97, SRMR = .02, RMSEA = .07). The 
items’ factor loadings ranged from .57 to .80. As shown in  Table 1  , the Cronbach’s alpha values 
of the four factors were .89 (perceived digital inquiry), .90 (emotional engagement), .88 (cognitive 
engagement), and .91 (perceived creativity), indicating good internal consistency.
  Descriptive and correlation analyses
The descriptive statistics for the four factors examined are presented in Table 1. Perceived creativity 
(M = 3.75, SD = .75) scored the highest among all the factors. Emotional engagement (M = 3.67, SD 
= .75) had the lowest score. The mean score and standard deviation for cognitive engagement were 
3.73 and .71, respectively, and those for perceived digital inquiry were 3.72 and .73.
The correlation matrix of the four factors is shown in Table 1. There were significant positive 
correlations between perceived digital inquiry and emotional engagement (r = .78, p < .01), 
cognitive engagement (r = .85, p < .01), and perceived creativity (r = .80, p < .01); emotional 
engagement and cognitive engagement (r = .81, p < .01) and perceived creativity (r = .80, p < 
.01); and cognitive engagement and perceived creativity (r = .77, p < .01).

and creativity.
  Discussion
The two primary goals of this study were (i) to examine the direct associations between perceived 
digital inquiry, emotional engagement, cognitive engagement, and perceived creativity and (ii) to 
apply the TME to investigate whether emotional engagement and cognitive engagement had 
indirect effects on the relationship between perceived digital inquiry and creativity. We used SEM 
to examine the mediating effects of emotional and cognitive engagement in our proposed model.
The results have a major theoretical contribution. They provided more empirical evidence to 
support Lawson and Lawson’s ( 2013) relatively new conceptual framework of student engage -
ment—TME—by presenting the significant relationships that exist among acts of engagement, 
benefits from engagement, and dispositions for engagement in STEM education. This study 
supported the application of TME in student STEM learning. We encourage more applications 
of TME in other learning environments to reveal or explain the interactions between students’ 
digital inquiry, emotional engagement, cognitive engagement, and creativity.

The results also have the following empirical implications. They showed that perceived digital 
inquiry had direct positive associations with both emotional engagement and cognitive engage -
ment (H1 and H2). These findings align with previous results showing that perceived inquiry-related 
features can indicate a disposition that triggers different engagement acts (Wu & Wu, 2020). 
Students who perceive stronger digital inquiry tend to try out their skills by actively engaging 
in learning activities emotionally and cognitively (Porat et al., 2018). Moreover, our results indi -
cate that the direct association between perceived digital inquiry and creativity is not statistically 
significant (H3). This finding is inconsistent with that of Hwang et al. ( 2018), who reported a 
significant positive correlation between students’ perceptions of inquiry-related mobile adoption 
and creativity tendency. This suggests that students’ development of creativity depends on their 
engagement with a task. Compared with students who engage in STEM activities, disengaged 
students are less likely to feel that they can create something new.
A significant positive bidirectional association between emotional engagement and cognitive 
engagement was identified (H4). This finding aligns with reports of interactions between the two 
types of engagement (Chiu, 2021, 2022; Chiu et al., 2012, 2022; Dubovi & Tabak, 2021 ; Hong 
et al., 2020). Emotional engagement encourages cognitive engagement development, and vice versa. 
Correspondingly, we included the interaction between different engagement acts as a factor influ -
encing engagement development, supplementary to the suggestion of Fredricks et al. ( 2004 ) that 
the interaction between the individual and the context affects student engagement.
Furthermore, emotional engagement had a significant positive effect on perceived creativity 
(H5). This result supports reports that emotional engagement makes a notable contribution to 
creativity development (Wang et al., 2022). Being emotionally engaged in a learning activity 
means that students are devoting their affection resources to the tasks, which will foster their 
perceptions of competencies such as creativity. However, we did not find a statistically significant 
association between cognitive engagement and perceived creativity (H6). This finding is incon -
sistent with studies that reported a significant positive correlation between these two variables 
(Álvarez-Huerta et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022). The discrepancy may be because students with 
strong cognitive engagement in STEM digital making activities are more likely to feel challenged 
or frustrated. A challenging experience with a heavy cognitive load could lead students to per -
ceive their competencies negatively as a result of cognitive overload and frustration (Chiu & 
Churchill, 2015 ; Reynolds & Caperton,  2011 ). This finding indicates the need for educators to 
carefully design learning tasks with appropriate difficulty levels for students. It can avoid the 
possible negative effects that may be caused by cognitive overload.
We next examined the indirect relationships between the variables. We found an indirect 
effect of emotional engagement on the relationship between perceived digital inquiry and cre -
ativity, supporting H7. This result aligns with the finding of Malik et al. ( 2020) that emotional 
engagement mediates the association between students’ digital inquiry related to behavior and 
creativity. As a direct association between perceived digital inquiry and perceived creativity was 
not found in the present study, this mediation effect suggests an alternative approach to pro -
moting perceived creativity by developing students’ emotional engagement. Nevertheless, it is 
notable that there was no indirect effect of cognitive engagement on the relationship between 
perceived digital inquiry and creativity (H8). The result is inconsistent with Sun’s ( 2020) con -
clusion that cognitive engagement can partially mediate the relationship between digital 
inquiry-related behavior and students’ creativity. A possible reason for this inconsistency is that 
the contradictory relationship between cognitive engagement and perceived creativity caused 
by cognitive load (Reynolds & Caperton, 2011) may eliminate the mediating effect of cognitive 
engagement on the association between perceived digital inquiry and creativity. Another potential 
explanation is that in STEM education, making creative outcomes requires long-term investment. 
Interest and joy would encourage students to invest in learning for a longer period. The 
user-friendly digital making platforms adopted, including Scratch, Arduino Kits, and Microbits, 
were designed to foster learners’ interest and joy in digital making. They can be promising 
tools to promote student creativity in STEM learning.

Overall, our results show that in secondary school STEM education, perceived digital inquiry 
can be an important personal resource that directly improves students’ emotional and cognitive 
engagement. Moreover, emotional engagement can mediate the positive relationship between 
perceived digital inquiry and perceived creativity. Therefore, interventions aimed at fostering 
perceived digital inquiry may be useful for developing self-perceived creativity of secondary 
school STEM learners. We recommend that teachers should make efforts to emotionally engage 
students in digital inquiry activities when the learning objective is creativity (Chiu et al., 2020; 
Weng, Chiu, & Jong, 2022; Weng, Cui, et al., 2022). The more emotionally engaged students 
feel with their learning tasks, the more likely it is that they will feel that they can create some -
thing new.
Our findings provide two practical suggestions for fostering creativity in digital-supported 
STEM learning. The first suggestion is that STEM teachers can apply different strategies to 
make students feel more engaged in utilizing digital tools for inquiry-based learning. For 
example, they could use various digital making platforms and tools to encourage students 
to complete small projects. The second suggestion is that instead of emphasizing STEM 
disciplinary knowledge development, STEM teachers are encouraged to enhance students’ 
STEM interest and identity first before STEM learning. This enhanced affection would lead 
to greater emotional engagement. The students would sustain their STEM digital inquiry 
learning for more creative work. To achieve the desired outcomes, the teachers could share 
some alumni’s success stories of STEM learning, and show the interesting and inspiring 
STEM products.
  Conclusion, limitations, and future directions
This study investigated the direct relationships between perceived digital inquiry, emotional 
engagement, cognitive engagement, and perceived creativity, and the indirect effects of emotional 
engagement and cognitive engagement on the association between perceived digital inquiry and 
creativity. The results imply that STEM digital making activities show promise for promoting 
students’ perceived competencies and learning engagement.
However, this study has some limitations. First, we cannot infer causal relationships between 
the variables because of the cross-sectional research design. Besides, because the number of 
participants was relatively small, the research findings should be confirmed by studies with a 
larger sample size. Moreover, other types of data sources such as qualitative interviews or class -
room observations should be collected to enhance the explanatory power of the study. Additionally, 
each of the four variables was evaluated using only three items. As the dimensions involved are 
complex, it is unclear whether the measurements satisfactorily reflect these four variables. 
Furthermore, the measurements were self-reported, so the relationships between perceived cre -
ativity and the other variables may have been inflated or deflated. Nevertheless, the CFA indicated 
that four separate factors were derived from the constructs. Despite these limitations, by following 
a TME framework, this study provides the first evidence of the interactions between secondary 
STEM learners’ perceived competencies and their engagement.
 This programme was designed to enhance perceived digital inquiry holistically in line with 
self-perception theory (Teng,  2018  ). The adoption of digital tools, scientific inquiry, and 
hands-on experience obtained through digital making activities foster students’ competencies 
and learning engagement (Stork,  2020 ). Accordingly, future studies could evaluate the out -
comes and report students’ feedback on the digital making programme in terms of develop -
ments in their self-perceived competencies and learning engagement. Furthermore, future 
research could use other frameworks to investigate the associations between student perceived 
digital inquiry, emotional engagement, cognitive engagement, and perceived creativity in STEM 
digital making environments. Future studies can also be conducted in other learning contexts, 
such as an Artificial Intelligence-facilitated learning environment instead of a STEM-based 
environment.
