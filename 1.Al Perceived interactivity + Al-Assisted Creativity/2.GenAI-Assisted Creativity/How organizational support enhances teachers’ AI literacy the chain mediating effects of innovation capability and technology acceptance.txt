ABSTRACT  
Teachers’ artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) literacy is a critical competency for building eﬀective 
interactive learning environments, yet a substantial gap persists between organizational 
support and teachers’ individual capacities. Drawing on the Technology–Organization– 
Environment (TOE) framework, this study employs structural equation modeling to 
analyze survey data from 10,683 primary and secondary school teachers in Shanghai, 
China, examining the mechanism through which organizational support enhances 
teachers’ AI literacy via innovation capability and technology acceptance. The ﬁndings 
reveal that organizational support exerts both a signiﬁcant direct eﬀect on AI literacy 
(18.2% of total eﬀect) and substantial indirect eﬀects through innovation capability 
(22.4%) and technology acceptance (22.8%). Most importantly, innovation capability 
and technology acceptance jointly form a chain mediation pathway accounting for 
36.6% of the total eﬀect, revealing a complete mechanism: organizational support /uni2192 
innovation capability /uni2192 technology acceptance /uni2192 AI literacy. This study is the ﬁrst to 
clarify this chain transmission mechanism, oﬀering a new theoretical perspective for 
understanding how organizational factors systematically promote teachers’ AI literacy. 
Based on these ﬁndings, a four-level collaborative framework—institutional support, 
resource coordination, mechanism innovation, and agent empowerment—is proposed 
to provide actionable strategies for enhancing teachers’ AI literacy and optimizing 
interactive learning environments.

1. Introduction
The digital transformation of education has become a major driving force in global educational reform, pro -
foundly reshaping learning environments and teachers’ professional practice. In this process, teachers’ artiﬁ -
cial intelligence (AI) literacy is not only a core vehicle for technology use but also a key prerequisite for the 
sustainable development of digital education (Le et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). The Artiﬁcial Intelligence Com -
petency Framework for Teachers, released by the United Nations Educational, Scientiﬁc and Cultural Organ -
ization (UNESCO) in 2024, explicitly identiﬁes AI literacy as a core competency for teachers in the era of 
intelligent education, encompassing technical proﬁciency, ethical re/f_lection, and pedagogical innovation 
(UNESCO, 2024). In China, the Opinions on Accelerating the Advancement of Educational Digitalization 
and the Guidelines for General Artiﬁcial Intelligence Education in Primary and Secondary Schools, both 
issued in 2025, emphasize the importance of deep integration between AI and education for improving edu -
cational quality. However, a substantial gap remains between policy aspirations and implementation in prac-
tice. Relevant studies have shown that Chinese teachers still demonstrate limitations in both the breadth and 
depth of AI application in educational settings (Yang et al., 2023). Many teachers struggle to use AI tools 
proﬁciently to design inquiry-based learning activities, and most applications remain at the level of basic 
operation, lacking systematic pedagogical redesign and in-depth re/f_lection on AI ethics (Chiu et al., 
2024a). Although the National Smart Education Public Service Platform has registered more than 178 
million users and aggregated over 130,000 high-quality resources for primary and secondary education,

the development of teachers’ AI literacy has not kept pace with the rapid transformation of educational con -

This tension is closely associated with a “dual disconnect” in organizational support. First, at the resource 
level, there is a structural mismatch between supply and educational needs. Although schools have generally 
increased investment in intelligent hardware, the development of AI curricular resources, interdisciplinary 
case repositories, and technical support systems has lagged behind substantially (Zeng, 2022). This conﬁgur-
ation pattern, characterized by an emphasis on hardware over content, places teachers in the dilemma of 
“having access to technology but being unable to use it eﬀectively,” thereby hindering the deep integration 
of AI into teaching. Second, at the mechanism level, school support tends to focus on short-term skills train -
ing while neglecting the synergistic relationship between the cultivation of teachers’ innovation capability 
and their psychological readiness to accept technology. This fragmented support model fails to stimulate 
teachers’ intrinsic motivation and obstructs their transition from “technology users” to “educational innova -
tors” (Xu & Zhu, 2023).
To address this dual disconnect, the present study draws on the Technology–Organization–Environment 
(TOE) framework (Tornatzky et al., 1990) and proposes a four-level collaborative framework consisting of insti-
tutional support, resource coordination, mechanism innovation, and agent empowerment as a systematic sol-
ution for narrowing the gap between policy aspirations and practical implementation. By progressively 
addressing the directional disconnect between macro-level policy and school-level implementation, the struc-
tural mismatch inherent in the hardware-centered model, the fragmented support problem of short-term skills 
training, and teachers’ transformation from “technology users” to “educational innovators,” this framework 
oﬀers an integrated and multilevel solution that connects organizational resource provision with individual 
capability development, providing a clear theoretical anchor for the subsequent empirical investigation.
In the international literature, teachers’ AI literacy has rapidly emerged as a growing area of research. 
Recent systematic reviews have shown that, although AI literacy is receiving increasing attention in K–12 
education, research speciﬁcally focused on teachers’ AI literacy remains at an early stage (Biagini, 2025; 
Casal-Otero et al., 2023). Studies of preservice teachers further suggest that the development of AI literacy 
depends not only on external support such as training and curricular resources but also substantially on lear-
ners’ motivation and attitudes (Lee & Song, 2024; Qian & Yan, 2026). By contrast, related research in the 
Chinese academic context has largely remained at the levels of information literacy and digital literacy, 
with relatively limited attention given to teachers’ AI literacy per se, especially to the role of organizational 
support mechanisms. Accordingly, this study raises a central question: How can school organizational 
support move beyond superﬁcial technological enablement and promote the deeper development of tea -
chers’ AI literacy through the mediating roles of innovation capability and technology acceptance? Addres -
sing this question can not only help bridge the gap between macro-level policy and micro-level practice but 
also provide actionable pathways for mechanism optimization and a systematic practical framework for 
advancing the digital transformation of education.
2. Literature review and research hypotheses
2.1. Theoretical framework: The Technology–Organization–Environment Model
The Technology–Organization–Environment (TOE) framework, proposed by Tornatzky et al. ( 1990), provides 
a systematic analytical perspective for understanding the process of technological innovation adoption at 
the organizational level. According to this framework, an organization’s decisions regarding technological 
innovation are jointly shaped by three key dimensions: the technological context, the organizational 
context, and the environmental context. The framework has been extensively validated in studies of infor -
mation technology adoption and digital transformation (Baker, 2011; Gangwar et al., 2015) and, in recent 
years, has been widely applied to emerging technological domains such as blockchain adoption (Chittipaka 
et al., 2023) and artiﬁcial intelligence applications (Gupta et al., 2026). The TOE framework not only oﬀers a 
theoretical explanation for the mechanism through which organizational support operates, but also provides 
the theoretical foundation for understanding the chain transmission pathway of “organizational support /uni2192 
innovation capability /uni2192 technology acceptance /uni2192 AI literacy,” as shown in Figure 1.

The technological context encompasses the characteristics of technologies available both within and 
outside the organization, including attributes such as relative advantage, complexity, and compatibility. In 
the context of teachers’ AI literacy development, the technological context is re/f_lected in technology accep-
tance, particularly in terms of the functional features of AI tools, their operational complexity, and their com -
patibility with existing instructional systems. These factors directly shape teachers’ perceptions of the 
usefulness and ease of use of AI technologies (Gangwar et al., 2015).
The organizational context re/f_lects the internal characteristics and resource allocation of the organization, 
including management support, technological infrastructure, human resources, and organizational culture. 
In the present study, the organizational context is primarily re/f_lected in school organizational support, 
encompassing dimensions such as work support, value recognition, beneﬁt-related care, interpersonal 
coordination, and professional development (Zhu et al., 2006). As the starting point of the chain mediation 
process, organizational support initiates the entire transmission mechanism by stimulating teachers’ tech -
nology acceptance and innovation capability.
The environmental context encompasses the organization’s external institutional and competitive 
environment, including factors such as policies and regulations, industry standards, and competitive 
pressure, all of which are closely related to teachers’ innovation capability. In the development of teachers’ 
AI literacy, the environmental context is mainly re/f_lected in policy orientations toward educational digitali-
zation, development trends in the AI education industry, and society’s acceptance of AI in education. Among 
these factors, an innovative climate is particularly critical to teachers’ innovation capability (Ng et al., 2023).
The TOE framework aligns closely with the chain mediation logic of the present study and also provides 
theoretical support for the proposed four-level collaborative framework. Speciﬁcally, the three dimensions 
correspond to diﬀerent stages of the chain transmission process: the organizational dimension initiates 
the chain, the technological dimension shapes the transformation process, and the environmental dimen -
sion reinforces the transmission mechanism. Together, these dimensions constitute a comprehensive mech -
anism underlying the development of teachers’ AI literacy. This multidimensional interactive mechanism 
provides a theoretical basis for understanding the pathways through which organizational support in/f_lu-
ences teachers’ AI literacy.

The widespread application of artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) across education and other sectors provides an 
important backdrop for understanding the signiﬁcance of teachers’ AI literacy. In education, AI technologies 
are demonstrating substantial transformative potential across multiple domains: personalized learning 
systems tailor content to individual students’ needs (Peng et al., 2019), intelligent tutoring systems

improve outcomes through real-time performance tracking (Ally & Perris, 2022), and adaptive assessment 
technologies oﬀer continuous and dynamic feedback beyond the limitations of standardized testing 
(Chen et al., 2020; Chiu, 2024; Du, 2025; Ng et al., 2023; Strielkowski et al., 2025).
AI is also reshaping curriculum development and content generation, with AI-driven tools enabling more

thermore, AI-integrated virtual learning environments are creating immersive and interactive spaces that 
extend the boundaries of conventional classroom instruction (Wang & Huang, 2025).
From a cross-sector perspective, successful AI applications in other ﬁelds also oﬀer important insights for 
the development of AI in education and underscore the broader signiﬁcance of AI literacy. In healthcare, AI- 
driven diagnostic systems and clinical decision-support tools demonstrate how human expertise can be 
enhanced through human–AI collaboration, requiring professionals to develop the kind of AI literacy 
needed to work eﬀectively with intelligent systems rather than merely operate tools (Liaw et al., 2022; 
Topol, 2019; Zöller et al., 2025). In ﬁnance, algorithmic trading and risk assessment models illustrate that 
AI integration requires not only technical competence but also ethical awareness regarding algorithmic 
bias and decision transparency (Vuković et al., 2025; Yeo et al., 2025). In manufacturing, Industry 4.0 initiat -
ives suggest that successful AI adoption depends on a comprehensive organizational support system, includ-
ing systematic training, adaptive management structures, and an innovation-supportive culture 
(Ghobakhloo et al., 2022; Nayernia et al., 2022). Across these sectors, the evidence consistently indicates 
that AI literacy has become a foundational competency in contemporary society that transcends sectoral 
boundaries, and that its development depends critically on the organizational ecosystem rather than on indi-
vidual eﬀort alone (Casal-Otero et al., 2023).
These cross-sector insights suggest that the development of teachers’ AI literacy must be understood 
within the broader context of professional transformation, in which learning technologies are reshaping 
the fundamental nature of teaching practice (Strielkowski et al., 2025). Eﬀective AI literacy development 
requires simultaneous attention to technological understanding, evaluative skills, practical application, 
and ethical considerations (Ng et al., 2023; Pinski & Benlian, 2024), while the rapid iteration of AI technologies 
demands continuous professional learning (Su et al., 2023).
This context highlights the critical role of organizational support in the development of teachers’ AI lit -
eracy. In response to the growing diﬀusion of AI technologies and the urgent demands of educational 
digital transformation, school organizations must provide systematic support to ensure that teachers are 
able to respond eﬀectively to the educational challenges of the intelligent age.
2.3. Research hypotheses
2.3.1. The eﬀect of organizational support on teachers’ AI literacy
Perceived organizational support (POS), deﬁned as individuals’ overall perception that the organization 
values their contributions and cares about their well-being, is re/f_lected in educational settings through 
the resource support, value recognition, beneﬁt-related care, and professional empowerment that schools 
provide to teachers (Rhoades & Eisenberger, 2002). Prior research has shown that high levels of organiz -
ational support can foster a positive instructional ecology by providing explicit resources, such as technical 
training and equipment provision, as well as implicit support, such as cultivating a culture that encourages 
innovation, thereby strengthening teachers’ conﬁdence in using intelligent technologies to improve instruc -
tion (Shen et al., 2024). Particularly in the context of rapid AI development, organizational support provides 
teachers with the necessary conditions and motivational backing to access, learn, and apply AI tools. Accord-
ing to the TOE framework, when teachers strongly perceive organizational support, they are more likely to 
engage in activities that enhance their competence, strive to meet organizational expectations, and realize 
their self-worth (Chiu et al., 2024b). Empirical studies further indicate that perceived organizational support 
serves as a key mediating mechanism through which leadership behaviors are translated into positive pro -
fessional outcomes (Sluss et al., 2008). Teachers with stronger perceptions of organizational support are

more likely to proactively acquire new knowledge and to experiment with integrating technology into class -
room practice, thereby gradually enhancing their AI literacy through practice (Futris et al., 2015). Accord -
ingly, this study proposes the following hypothesis: 
H1: Organizational support has a signiﬁcant positive eﬀect on teachers’ AI literacy.
2.3.2. The mediating role of innovation capability
Innovation capability refers to teachers’ core ability to identify problems, generate creative ideas, and 
implement novel solutions in instructional practice, encompassing innovative thinking, practical transform -
ation, and adaptability (Lin & Liu, 2025; Qin et al., 2025). Organizational support can eﬀectively stimulate tea-
chers’ creative exploratory behavior by providing professional development resources, such as 
interdisciplinary collaboration platforms and technological experimentation spaces, as well as innovation- 
oriented incentive mechanisms, such as incorporating AI application into performance evaluation (Zargar 
et al., 2025). Research has shown that, in educational contexts shaped by the rapid development of AI, tea -
chers with stronger innovation capability are more likely to move beyond traditional instructional frame -
works and proactively explore innovative applications of AI in education (Chen et al., 2020). For example, 
they may use intelligent learning analytics tools to design personalized learning pathways or employ gen -
erative AI to create interactive instructional content. This innovation-oriented process of practice not only 
deepens teachers’ understanding of the functions and potential of AI technologies but also promotes the 
comprehensive development of their AI literacy through an iterative cycle of technological exploration, ped -
agogical innovation, and re/f_lective improvement (Tang et al., 2023).
Furthermore, teachers with stronger innovation capability demonstrate greater adaptability in response 
to evolving AI technologies, enabling them to integrate tools creatively into instructional design (Dilek et al., 
2025; Mazman Akar, 2019; Scherer et al., 2019). Innovation capability thus serves as a critical bridge linking 
organizational support and AI literacy by transforming external resources into teachers’ intrinsic motivation 
to learn and apply AI technologies. Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypothesis: 
H2: Innovation capability mediates the relationship between organizational support and teachers’ AI literacy.
2.3.3. The mediating role of technology acceptance
Technology acceptance refers to teachers’ psychological level of acceptance of AI technologies and is driven 
by perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). Recent research has shown 
that teachers’ digital literacy can signiﬁcantly in/f_luence their willingness to use AI-based instructional 
tools through pathways such as perceived usefulness, behavioral attitudes, and subjective norms (Du, 
2025). Organizational support enhances teachers’ recognition of the educational value of new technologies, 
such as precise learning analytics and personalized feedback, through systematic training, while also lower -
ing barriers to use by simplifying technological procedures. Research further indicates that the widespread 
application of AI has brought transformative changes to education, from personalized learning to intelligent 
assessment, all of which require teachers to possess an adequate level of technology acceptance (Chen et al., 
2020).
Teachers with higher levels of technology acceptance are more likely to embed AI tools into instructional 
design and assessment, thereby accumulating practical experience and developing a more sophisticated 
understanding of AI technologies (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Dilek et al., 2025).
Moreover, teachers’ technology acceptance directly aﬀects the eﬀectiveness of their AI-supported instruc-
tional practices and their capacity for continuous improvement (Long & Magerko, 2020; Tang et al., 2023). 
Therefore, technology acceptance may serve as a key psychological pathway through which organizational 
support is translated into AI literacy. Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypothesis: 
H3: Technology acceptance mediates the relationship between organizational support and teachers’ AI literacy.
2.3.4. The chain mediating eﬀects of innovation capability and technology acceptance
The mechanism through which organizational support in/f_luences AI literacy is not a simple linear relation-
ship; rather, it involves a spiral process driven by the synergistic eﬀects of innovation capability and

technology acceptance. Organizational support empowers teachers’ innovation capability by providing 
resources and incentive mechanisms (Hammond et al., 2009), while enhanced innovation capability 
further promotes deeper understanding and acceptance of AI technologies, facilitating teachers’ transition 
from “passive learning” to “active innovation” (Chen et al., 2020), as shown in Figure 2. This chain pathway of

As a multidimensional competency framework, AI literacy requires teachers not only to master technical 
operational skills but also to develop critical thinking and innovative application capabilities (Long &

support systematically promotes the development of teachers’ AI literacy. Accordingly, this study proposes 
the following hypothesis: 
H4: Innovation capability and technology acceptance exert a chain mediating eﬀect on the relationship between 
organizational support and teachers’ AI literacy.
3. Method
3.1. Data source
The research team conducted a large-scale survey of primary and secondary school teachers across all 16 
districts of Shanghai to systematically examine the current status of teachers’ organizational support, AI lit -
eracy, and related factors. A total of 11,581 questionnaires were collected through the Wenjuanxing plat -
form. After a rigorous quality-control procedure, including the removal of questionnaires containing 
logical inconsistencies, excessive missing data, and highly repetitive response patterns, 10,683 valid 
responses were retained, yielding an eﬀective response rate of 92.25%.
The survey employed a stratiﬁed random sampling method. First, the 16 districts of Shanghai were 
divided into three tiers according to their level of economic development: central urban districts 
(Huangpu, Jing’an, Xuhui, and Changning; four districts), inner suburban districts (including Minhang, 
Baoshan, Jiading, and Pudong; eight districts), and outer suburban districts (Jinshan, Chongming, Feng -
xian, and Qingpu; four districts), thereby ensuring that the sample covered areas with diﬀerent levels of 
development. Within each district, a second stage of stratiﬁcation was conducted by school type (public 
vs. private) and educational level (primary school, junior secondary school, and senior secondary 
school), after which teachers were randomly selected from each school to participate in the survey. 
To ensure data quality, the research team established strict quality-control criteria, including a 
minimum completion time of 8 min, logical consistency checks across related items, and outlier screen -
ing, with questionnaires excluded when the proportion of consecutively selecting the same option was 
excessively high.

3.2. Variable selection
3.2.1. Organizational support
This study adopted the classic Perceived Organizational Support (POS) scale developed by Eisenberger et al. 
(1986), drawing on its revised version for the Chinese organizational context and adapting the wording of 
the items to re/f_lect the professional characteristics of teachers. Speciﬁcally, the adaptation involved four 
steps. First, linguistic localization was conducted through a translation–back-translation procedure, in 
which two doctoral students in education independently translated the items and cross-checked the 
results to ensure semantic accuracy. Second, contextualization was implemented by replacing generic 
organizational expressions in the original scale with education-speciﬁc terminology; for example, “organiz -
ation” was uniformly changed to “school,” and “job performance” was revised to “teaching performance.” 
Third, item screening was carried out to remove statements irrelevant to the teaching profession while 
retaining 15 core items in light of the work characteristics of Chinese teachers. Fourth, some items were cul -
turally reﬁned; for instance, “management” was replaced with “school leadership” to better align with the 
cultural context of Chinese education. These adaptations were necessary because the original scale devel -
oped by Eisenberger et al. ( 1986) was designed for general organizational settings and did not fully 
capture the distinctive features of organizational support for teachers in schools, including instructional lea -
dership, the provision of teaching resources, and support from professional learning communities. A pilot 
test involving 120 teachers from two schools in Shanghai conﬁrmed that the adapted items demonstrated 
better face validity and comprehensibility than a direct translation of the original version.
The scale consisted of 15 items (A1–A15) covering ﬁve dimensions: work support, value recognition, 
beneﬁt-related care, interpersonal coordination, and professional development. All items were measured 
on a ﬁve-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), with higher total 
scores indicating stronger perceived organizational support. All items were positively keyed. Reliability 
and validity tests showed that the Cronbach’s α coeﬃcients for the ﬁve dimensions were 0.949, 0.962, 
0.965, 0.970, and 0.957, respectively. The conﬁrmatory factor analysis showed a good model ﬁt (GFI =  
0.932, RMSEA = 0.079, CFI = 0.977, NFI = 0.976), indicating strong internal consistency and structural validity.
3.2.2. Teachers’ AI literacy
Given the dynamic evolution of the concept of literacy in the digital era – from information literacy to digital 
literacy and, more recently, AI literacy – this study integrated the AI literacy scale developed by Nong et al. 
(2024) with the Artiﬁcial Intelligence Competency Framework for Teachers proposed by the United Nations 
Educational, Scientiﬁc and Cultural Organization (UNESCO, 2024) to construct a localized AI literacy scale. The 
adaptation process involved four steps. First, the theoretical framework was localized by adjusting the com -
petency structure in light of the policy requirements set forth in China’s Teachers’ Digital Literacy Standards 
and the AI Plus Teacher Workforce Development Action Plan. Second, item content was revised by convert -
ing technology-development-oriented statements in the original scale into teaching-application-oriented 
items; for example, “being able to program AI algorithms” was revised to “being able to select appropriate 
AI tools to support instruction.” Third, cultural contextualization was undertaken by simplifying certain tech -
nical terms and adding pedagogically situated descriptions in consideration of Chinese teachers’ levels of 
understanding of AI technologies. Fourth, expert review was conducted by inviting ﬁve experts in AI edu -
cation and 10 frontline teachers to evaluate the content validity of the scale, after which ﬁnal revisions 
were made based on their feedback. These adaptations were necessary because the original scale developed 
by Nong et al. (2024) was intended primarily to assess general AI literacy across diﬀerent groups and did not 
speciﬁcally address teachers’ instructional application contexts or the policy requirements of Chinese

3.2.3. Innovation capability
The innovation capability scale was adapted from the teacher innovation capability scale proposed by 
Sharma and Sharma ( 2018) to re/f_lect the characteristics of teachers’ instructional practice. The revision 
process involved four steps. First, translation and cultural adaptation were conducted through a standard 
procedure of forward translation, back translation, and expert review to ensure the accuracy and compre -
hensibility of the items in the Chinese context. Second, educational contextualization was implemented 
by revising generic innovation-related statements in the original scale into pedagogically oriented 
expressions; for example, “I often look for new ways of working” was changed to “I often explore new teach -
ing methods.” Third, item reduction was undertaken to balance questionnaire length and teacher response 
burden, resulting in the selection of 9 representative items from the original 12-item scale. Fourth, linguistic 
localization was applied to make the wording more natural and /f_luent in accordance with Chinese teachers’ 
expression habits. These revisions were necessary because the original scale was designed for general organ-
izational settings and did not fully capture the distinctive features of instructional innovation in K–12 edu -
cation. The reduction from 12 to 9 items was guided by item – total correlations and factor loadings from an 
exploratory factor analysis based on a pilot sample of 150 participants, as well as substantive judgments 
regarding relevance to the Chinese teaching context.
The scale comprised 9 items (C1–C9) across three dimensions: innovative thinking (e.g. “I often think 
about how to make teaching more creative”), innovative practice (e.g. “I try to use new technologies to 
support teaching”), and innovative disposition (e.g. “I value continuous learning and improvement”). All 
items were measured on a ﬁve-point Likert scale. Reliability testing showed that the Cronbach’s α coeﬃcients 
for the three dimensions were 0.975, 0.947, and 0.975, respectively. The conﬁrmatory factor analysis yielded 
excellent ﬁt indices (GFI = 0.946, RMR = 0.018, CFI = 0.983, NFI = 0.983), providing strong support for the 
reliability and validity of the scale.
3.2.4. Technology acceptance
This study used the technology acceptance scale developed by Davis ( 1989) as the basis for the question -
naire and adapted it for Chinese teachers. The modiﬁcation process involved four steps. First, the target tech-
nology was speciﬁed by replacing all generic references to “information technology” in the original scale 
with “AI technology” to ensure consistency in the measurement focus. Second, educational contextualization 
was introduced by embedding teaching scenarios into the items; for example, “this technology is useful” was 
revised to “AI technology can improve teaching eﬃciency.” Third, item optimization and selection were con -
ducted by choosing 6 core items from the original 12-item scale in order to streamline the questionnaire and 
sharpen its focus. Fourth, linguistic localization was applied by adopting wording consistent with the 
expression habits of Chinese teachers to ensure naturalness and comprehensibility. These modiﬁcations 
were necessary because the original scale targeted general information technology systems in workplace 
settings, whereas AI technology acceptance in educational contexts involves distinct considerations such 
as pedagogical appropriateness and alignment with curricular goals. A pilot study with 80 teachers 
conﬁrmed that the shortened scale retained a stable two-factor structure with strong factor loadings (all 
> 0.70).
The scale consisted of two dimensions: perceived usefulness (e.g. “AI technology can improve teaching 
eﬃciency”) and perceived ease of use (e.g. “AI tools are simple and convenient to operate”). It included 6 
items (D1–D6), all measured on a ﬁve-point Likert scale, with higher total scores indicating a stronger

3.3. Data analysis
This study used SPSS 26.0 and Amos 26.0 for data analysis. SPSS 26.0 was used for data preprocessing, includ-
ing reliability analysis (Cronbach’s α), descriptive statistics, and Pearson correlation analysis. Amos 26.0 was 
used for conﬁrmatory factor analysis (CFA) and structural equation modeling (SEM) with maximum likelihood

4. Results
4.1. Common method bias
To control for the potential in/f_luence of common method bias on the study ﬁndings, Harman’s single-factor 
test was applied to the measurement items for organizational support, innovation capability, technology 
acceptance, and AI literacy. The results showed that ﬁve latent factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 
were extracted, and the ﬁrst factor accounted for 37.3% of the total variance, which is below the critical 
threshold of 40%. This result indicates that the data were not substantially aﬀected by common method 
bias and that the ﬁndings are therefore credible.
4.2. Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis
The overall mean score for teachers’ AI literacy was 3.656 (SD = 0.827). Signiﬁcant diﬀerences were observed 
across the subdimensions. AI ethics (M = 4.091) and AI thinking (M = 3.881) showed the highest levels of per -
formance. Teachers’ professional learning (M = 3.543) and AI tools (M = 3.513) were at a moderate level. By 
contrast, AI pedagogy (M = 3.497) and AI foundations (M = 3.409) were relatively weak, suggesting that tech-
nological application capability and the integration of pedagogy remain the most salient shortcomings, as 
shown in Table 1.
These ﬁndings reveal a structural imbalance in the development of teachers’ AI literacy and carry impor -
tant theoretical implications. The relatively weak performance in AI foundations (M = 3.409) suggests that 
teachers have an insuﬃcient understanding of the principles and functions of AI technologies, which directly 
constrains their ability to use AI tools /f_lexibly in instructional practice and may result in a pattern of appli-
cation characterized by procedural use without deeper conceptual understanding. This ﬁnding is consistent 
with the technological context dimension of the Technology–Organization–Environment (TOE) framework, 
which posits that technological complexity signiﬁcantly aﬀects adoption outcomes (Tornatzky et al., 1990). 
Without suﬃcient foundational knowledge, teachers cannot eﬀectively evaluate the suitability and limit -
ations of diﬀerent AI tools in speciﬁc instructional contexts, causing their technology acceptance to 
remain at the level of superﬁcial compliance rather than deep integration.

5. Discussion and conclusion
Based on the structural equation modeling analysis, this study elucidates the pathways and underlying 
mechanisms through which organizational support enhances teachers’ AI literacy. Drawing on empirical 
data from 10,683 teachers in Shanghai, the study conﬁrms four key ﬁndings and further clariﬁes their 
deeper implications for the digital transformation of education.
5.1. Core ﬁndings: the multifaceted mechanism of organizational support
The ﬁndings show that organizational support has a signiﬁcant direct eﬀect on teachers’ AI literacy (0.110, p  
< 0.001), accounting for 18.2% of the total eﬀect. This indicates that the provision of resources, value

recognition, and professional development support by schools can directly enhance teachers’ ability and 
willingness to apply AI technologies (Moreira et al., 2019). Through both tangible support, such as technical 
training and hardware provision, and intangible support, such as fostering an innovative climate, organiz -
ational support eﬀectively shortens the transition from “technological awareness” to “instructional appli -
cation” (Xie et al., 2023), consistent with the core proposition of the TOE framework.
Innovation capability also plays a signiﬁcant mediating role in the relationship between organizational 
support and AI literacy, with an indirect eﬀect of 0.136, accounting for 22.4% of the total eﬀect. By providing 
professional platforms, incentive mechanisms, and spaces for innovation, organizational support eﬀectively 
cultivates teachers’ innovative thinking and practical capability, thereby promoting their integration of AI 
technologies into instructional design (Lin, 2021). This pathway underscores the central importance of 
capacity building in technology use, suggesting that merely having access to technological tools is not 
suﬃcient; teachers must also develop the capability to use these tools for pedagogical innovation (Hulpia 
et al., 2011).
Technology acceptance likewise serves a signiﬁcant mediating function (indirect eﬀect: 0.138, 22.8% of 
total eﬀect), as organizational support enhances teachers’ perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use 
(Scherer et al., 2021). Most importantly, innovation capability and technology acceptance jointly constitute 
the strongest chain mediation pathway (eﬀect: 0.222, 36.6% of total eﬀect), forming a progressive pathway 
of “innovation-driven development /uni2192 technological internalization” (Chen et al., 2020; Dilek et al., 2025; 
Hammond et al., 2009; Tang et al., 2023). This ﬁnding highlights the critical value of the synergy between 
external support and teachers’ endogenous factors in technology adoption.
5.2. The four-level collaborative framework and the mechanism for advancing the digital 
transformation of education
Based on the empirical ﬁndings, this study proposes a four-level collaborative framework – comprising insti -
tutional support, resource coordination, mechanism innovation, and agent empowerment – as a practice- 
oriented support system for advancing the comprehensive digital transformation of education (see Figure 4).
This framework moves beyond a narrow focus on individual teachers’ competency development and pos-
itions organizational support as a systemic lever for institutional change. The level of institutional support 
provides the policy environment and normative foundation for the intelligent development of the education 
system, including the establishment of AI education standards, the formulation of technology integration 
policies, and the creation of accountability mechanisms that align institutional incentives with the goals 
of digital transformation (Chiu, 2024; Dilek et al., 2025; Ng et al., 2023).
The level of resource coordination provides material and soft-resource support for the intelligent trans -
formation of the education system by prioritizing the development of curricular resources, technical 
support infrastructure, and professional learning communities, thereby addressing the documented imbal -
ance of emphasizing hardware while neglecting content (Abbasi et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2024). The level of 
mechanism innovation stimulates stakeholders’ intrinsic motivation to participate in digital transformation 
by establishing incentive and evaluation systems that reward pedagogical innovation and by creating col -
laborative governance mechanisms that promote knowledge sharing across schools and disciplines (Lin & 
Liu, 2025; Qin et al., 2025). The level of agent empowerment systematically enhances teachers’ AI literacy 
through the chain mechanism of “innovation capability /uni2192 technology acceptance,” while also fostering stu -
dents’ digital competence and improving the digital adaptability and innovative capacity of all participants 
in the educational system (Scherer et al., 2019; Zhang & Zhang, 2024).
Importantly, this framework aligns with emerging global discourse on the digital transformation of edu -
cation, which emphasizes that sustainable technology integration requires simultaneous change at the 
policy, institutional, pedagogical, and individual levels rather than fragmented interventions (Strielkowski 
et al., 2025). The four-level collaborative framework advances educational digital transformation through 
three core mechanisms. First, the vertical penetration mechanism ensures the eﬀective transmission and 
implementation of policy intentions (Shen et al., 2024). Second, the horizontal coordination mechanism 
creates a uniﬁed force for digital transformation (Qin et al., 2025). Third, the spiral enhancement mechanism 
enables cyclical feedback and iterative optimization, thereby forming a spiral trajectory for the digital trans -
formation of education (Strielkowski et al., 2025).

This framework drives the digital transformation of education as a multilayered process that extends 
beyond the improvement of teachers’ AI literacy alone. At the micro level, the chain mediation pathway 
enables a shift from traditional knowledge transmission to intelligent and personalized teaching (Scherer 
et al., 2019; Zhang & Zhang, 2024). At the meso level, the framework promotes data-driven educational man-
agement, as organizations that support innovation capability simultaneously build institutional capacity for 
evidence-based governance (Eryilmaz et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2024). At the macro level, the framework facili -
tates the formation of a digital education ecosystem in which government, schools, the technology industry, 
and teachers converge toward shared transformation goals (Abbasi et al., 2025; Saeedikiya et al., 2025; 
Strielkowski et al., 2025). By embedding technological change within comprehensive reforms involving insti -

titative support for the argument that digital transformation requires coordinated organizational, cognitive, 
and psychological mechanisms rather than isolated technological provision.
In conclusion, this study reveals the full mechanism through which organizational support enhances tea -
chers’ AI literacy via direct eﬀects (18.2%) and indirect eﬀects (81.8%), and constructs a four-level collabora -
tive framework that enables an organic linkage between the enhancement of teachers’ literacy at the micro 
level and the transformation of the education system at the macro level through vertical penetration, hori -
zontal coordination, and spiral enhancement mechanisms.
6. Theoretical and practical implications
6.1. Theoretical implications
This study contributes to the theoretical literature on the development of teachers’ AI literacy in three major 
ways. First, it extends research on the antecedents of teachers’ AI literacy. Existing studies have focused pri -
marily on teachers’ individual characteristics while paying insuﬃcient attention to organizational environ -
mental factors. By incorporating organizational support into the analytical framework, this study veriﬁes

its role as a key external driving force, ﬁlls a theoretical gap concerning the in/f_luence of macro-level organ-
izational factors on teachers’ AI literacy, and enriches the application of the Technology–Organization– 
Environment framework in the ﬁeld of education.
Second, this study reveals the mediating mechanisms through which organizational support in/f_luences 
teachers’ AI literacy. By introducing innovation capability and technology acceptance as mediating variables, 
the study not only conﬁrms their respective independent mediating eﬀects but also identiﬁes, for the ﬁrst 
time, the chain mediating eﬀect of “innovation capability /uni2192 technology acceptance.” This ﬁnding establishes 
a theoretical model in which organizational factors promote teachers’ technological literacy through a 
pathway of “capacity building /uni2192 psychological acceptance,” thereby oﬀering a new perspective for under -
standing the interactive mechanism between organizational environments and teachers’ individual 
characteristics.
Third, this study develops an integrated theoretical framework for the development of teachers’ AI lit -
eracy. Speciﬁcally, it advances a multilayered developmental model of “organizational resources /uni2192 
teacher capability /uni2192 psychological acceptance /uni2192 literacy performance,” providing teacher education 
theory with a new perspective on the coordinated development of external support and internal motivation. 
This theoretical framework helps move beyond the binary divide between “individual-centered” and “organ -
ization-centered” approaches in teacher development research and contributes to a more integrated theory 
of teachers’ professional development.
6.2. Practical implications
At the practical level, this study oﬀers three speciﬁc recommendations for education administrators, school 
leaders, and policymakers. First, the ﬁndings underscore the critical role of organizational support in foster -
ing teachers’ AI literacy. Accordingly, education administrators are advised to move beyond a narrow focus 
on hardware provision and instead build a comprehensive support system that encompasses technological 
resources, professional training, incentive mechanisms, and an innovation-supportive culture.
Second, the mediating eﬀects of innovation capability and technology acceptance account for 22.4% and 
22.8% of the total eﬀect, respectively, indicating the critical role of teachers’ endogenous factors in translat -
ing organizational support into literacy outcomes. Schools should therefore establish a dual-track teacher 
development system. On the one hand, they should cultivate teachers’ innovation capability through 
project-based learning and interdisciplinary collaboration. On the other hand, they should enhance teachers’ 
technology acceptance through demonstration lessons and the sharing of successful cases. Given that the 
chain mediating eﬀect of “innovation capability /uni2192 technology acceptance” accounts for 36.6% of the total 
eﬀect, schools should give priority to fostering innovation capability.
Third, this study provides a practical framework for building a multi-stakeholder system to support the 
development of teachers’ AI literacy. Based on the four-level structure of institutional support, resource 
coordination, mechanism innovation, and agent empowerment, government agencies need to strengthen 
policy guidance and resource investment; schools need to optimize their internal incentive and support 
mechanisms; and teachers need to enhance their innovative awareness and self-directed learning capacity. 
Such a multilayered support system can generate synergistic eﬀects and provide a systematic solution for 
the sustainable development of teachers’ AI literacy.
7. Limitations and future research
This study has four main limitations, each of which points to directions for future research. First, the study 
adopted a cross-sectional design and therefore can capture only correlational relationships among variables 
rather than establish causality. Future studies could employ experimental or longitudinal designs to track 
changes in teachers’ AI literacy by intervening in levels of organizational support, thereby providing a 
more rigorous test of causal relationships.
Second, this study relied exclusively on questionnaire data, which may raise concerns regarding common 
method bias. Although this risk was addressed through Harman’s single-factor test, with the ﬁrst factor 
accounting for 37.3% of the variance, below the 40% threshold, future research should nevertheless consider

mixed-methods approaches. Integrating classroom observation, in-depth interviews, and case analysis could 
yield richer multidimensional data and enhance both the reliability and explanatory power of the ﬁndings.
Third, although the sample size was relatively large (N = 10,683), the participants were limited to teachers in 
Shanghai, which may restrict regional representativeness. Given regional disparities across China in edu -
cational resources, levels of informatization, and support for teachers’ professional development, future 
research should expand the sampling frame to include teachers from central and western regions as well 
as rural areas. Comparative analyses across regions would help clarify how diﬀerent organizational support 
mechanisms in/f_luence teachers’ AI literacy and would strengthen the external validity of the ﬁndings.
Finally, this study did not take into account potential moderating variables such as teachers’ individual 
levels of technological competence and disciplinary backgrounds. Future research could examine the mod -
erating roles of individual diﬀerences, such as technological proﬁciency and subject characteristics, as well as 
structural factors, such as policy environments and resource investment, in the pathways through which 
organizational support in/f_luences teachers’ AI literacy. This would enable the development of a more com-
prehensive theoretical model. In addition, given the rapid evolution of AI technologies, future studies could 
incorporate related variables such as digital literacy and computational thinking into the analytical frame -
work in order to explore the broader mechanisms underlying the development of teachers’ digital 
competence.
In sum, future research should further advance the theoretical and practical study of teachers’ AI literacy 
development by expanding regional coverage, enriching research methods, reﬁning variable systems, and 
deepening comparative analysis, thereby providing more scientiﬁc and systematic support for the digital 
transformation of education.
