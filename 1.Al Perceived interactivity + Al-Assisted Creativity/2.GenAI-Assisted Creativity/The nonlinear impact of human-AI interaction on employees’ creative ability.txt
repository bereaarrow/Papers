The nonlinear impact of human-AI interaction on employees’ creative ability
Minling Chen, Yue Zhang and Jing Xue
School of Economics and Management, Xi’an Shiyou University, Xi’an, People’s Republic of China
ABSTRACT
Is AI always beneﬁcial? Despite the growing body of research on the positive outcomes of AI, some 
evidence still suggests the negative impact of its introduction into the workplace on employees. 
Taking into account the characteristics of human-AI interaction, this study seeks to answer the 
question of the non-linear impact of human-AI interaction on employee creativity. Utilising 284 
data from China’s smart manufacturing industry, the results show that the impact of human-AI 
interaction on employee creativity is nonlinear, showing an increasing and then decreasing 
trend. Second, cognitive outsourcing plays a curve mediating role between the two, which is 
manifested in the fact that human-AI interaction aﬀects creativity by inducing an inverted U- 
shaped change in cognitive outsourcing and then aﬀecting creativity. Finally, team mastery 
motivational climate moderated the inverted U-shaped relationship between human-AI 
interaction and cognitive outsourcing. This study practically helps companies to develop 
appropriate human-AI interaction strategies to enhance the core competitiveness of 
organisations in the smart era.

1. Introduction
As enterprises increasingly adopt artiﬁcial intelligence 
technologies (Von Krogh 2018), human-AI interaction 
(HAI) has become a prevalent new mode of work within 
organisations (De Cremer and McGuire 2022). Voice 
assistants, intelligent decision-making systems, genera -
tive AI, and other common artiﬁcial intelligence tech -
nologies are transforming how employees work while 
placing higher demands on their creativity in areas 
such as machine learning and big data processing. 
Given that AI can increase employee labour pro -
ductivity (Brynjolfsson, Mitchell, and Rock 2018), 
industries including healthcare, hospitality, and energy 
are striving to explore the positive eﬀects of HAI and 
thus achieve better development (Choi et al. 2020; 
Guo, Gu, and Huo 2025; Masudin et al. 2024).
Human-AI interaction refers to the interaction and 
integration process in which human beings and techno-
logical subjects (referring to AI, industrial intelligent 
systems, etc.) directly or indirectly share operational 
intentions, make joint decisions, and collaborate to 
accomplish tasks in an organisational environment 
(Gorecky et al. 2014; Romero, Stahre, and Taisch 
2020). Human-AI interaction can, to a certain extent, 
lead to an increase in the eﬃciency of human resource 
management (Vrontis et al. 2022). For example, doctors 
utilise AI to diagnose and improve diagnostic eﬃciency 
and accuracy (Guo, Gu, and Huo 2025); employee- 
GenAI collaboration in/f_luences helping behaviour and 
family engagement by reducing instrumentality in 
work relationships (Li et al. 2025). But when AI acts 
as a leadership role to collaborate with employees to 
accomplish tasks, this human-AI interaction is con -
sidered harmful by some scholars . This leads to pro -
blems such as employee income inequality, turnover, 
and information leakage (Acemoglu and Restrepo 
2020; Aizenberg and van den Hoven 2020), posing a 
threat to business management (Vrontis et al. 2022). 
For creativity and innovation, human-AI interaction, 
while having a positive impact, does not completely 
replace the role of humans (Wirtz et al. 2018).
Scholars have accumulated a wealth of results on the 
study of employee creativity. Studies have found that 
diﬀerent leadership styles (e.g. servant leadership, 
ambivalent leadership), team performance monitoring, 
knowledge sharing, and generative artiﬁcial intelligence 
aﬀect creativity (Benbya, Strich, and Tamm 2024; Diao, 
Jin, and Yu 2025; Hamid, Dar, and Kundi 2025; Mistry, 
Nanu, and Daley 2025; Nian et al. 2025 ). Existing 
research is divided on the eﬀect of HAI on employee 
creativity. Most scholars believe that AI has a positive 
eﬀect on employee creativity (Jia et al. 2024; Verma 
and Singh 2022). However, some scholars believe that

interacting with AI is not beneﬁcial to the productivity 
of knowledge workers (Man Tang et al. 2022; Skaria, 
Satam, and Khalpey 2020), and creates knowledge iner -
tia and path dependency (Laursen and Salter 2006; Liao 
2002), instead of being detrimental to the development 
of employee creativity. The reasons for the contradic -
tory results may be: ﬁrst, HAI has both positive and 
negative eﬀects, and is not linearly related to employee 
creativity; second, the diﬀerences in the conditions 
under which AI works and the contexts in which 
employees ﬁnd themselves lead to diﬀerent results. 
Therefore, unlike previous studies that adopted either 
positive or negative perspectives, this research explores 
the relationship between human-AI interaction and 
employee creativity from a nonlinear perspective.
First, one of the research objectives is to clarify the 
mechanism of human-AI interaction on employee crea-
tivity. According to the Conservation of Resources The-
ory (COR), employees will change their behaviours and 
attitudes by comparing the size of resource loss and 
gain. In moderate HAI, employees delegate low-value 
cognitive tasks to AI, enhancing information retrieval 
capabilities and problem-solving skills (Dwivedi et al. 
2023; Teodoridis, Bikard, and Vakili 2019), thereby 
boosting creativity. Conversely, in high HAI scenarios, 
rapid AI advancement enables automated task 
execution (Raisch and Krakowski 2021), potentially 
inducing negative emotions among employees and dis -
couraging learning and innovation. Therefore, the COR 
theory oﬀers a uniﬁed resource perspective, explaining 
how employees dynamically manage their resources 
based on the level of HAI to maximise gains and mini -
mise losses. In previous studies, work engagement, 
innovation eﬃcacy, and so on are often utilised as 
their characterisation indicators (Yin, Jiang, and Niu 
2024). As AI becomes increasingly integrated into 
human innovation tasks, its role has evolved from pas -
sive assistance to active collaboration, necessitating a 
deeper exploration of the intrinsic pathways through 
which AI in/f_luences employee creativity. Cognitive out-
sourcing is grounded in distributed cognition (Liu, Ner-
sessian, and Stasko 2008). When external information 
entropy exceeds a certain threshold, humans achieve 
synergy between internal and external cognitive net -
works by ‘outsourcing’ speciﬁc cognitive tasks – such 
as information retrieval and memory substitution – to 
external intelligent devices. In the context of AI appli -
cations, cognitive oﬄoading is often confused with cog -
nitive outsourcing. Cognitive oﬄoading constitutes an 
instinctive response driven by cognitive load, whose 
core mechanism lies in leveraging external resources 
to optimise the brain’s ﬁnite processing capacity while 
retaining cognitive control within the individual. 
Cognitive outsourcing, however, represents a strategic 
behaviour in which individuals delegate core cognitive 
processes to external agents, thereby relinquishing 
direct control over the task. This process represents a 
resource investment strategy (Tao, Zhang, and Liu 
2024; Yu and Wang 2023). Therefore, this study 
suggests that cognitive outsourcing mediates the 
relationship between HAI and employee creativity.
Second, the way employees cope with technological 
shocks depends not only on the individual’s factors, 
but also on the context in which the individual ﬁnds 
himself or herself. Both technology adoption and crea -
tivity enhancement are aﬀected by the environment 
(Yin, Jiang, and Niu 2024). Although scholars have 
identiﬁed the important impact of organisational cli -
mate on AI implementation and integration, the process 
of employee interaction with AI has rarely been 
addressed. To cope with external shocks, teams tend 
to emphasise continuous learning (Mu et al. 2021), 
resulting in a unique mastery motivational climate 
(Cerne et al. 2014). The mastery motivational climate 
can in/f_luence team members’ perceptions of resource 
acquisition or depletion (Hobfoll 1989) and can explain 
changes in employees’ processes of altering resource 
input and creativity (Cerne et al. 2014; Hu et al. 2015). 
However, existing research has paid less attention to 
the mastery motivational climate (Solberg et al. 2022). 
Therefore, the present study included team mastery 
motivational climate as a contextual variable and pro -
posed that team mastery motivational climate can 
modulate the relationship between HAI and cognitive 
outsourcing.

its mechanism of action are empirically examined using

made in the following aspects: ﬁrst, compared with 
the positive eﬀects of augmented HAI or the negative 
eﬀects of automated AI, this paper uniquely proposes 
the nonlinear eﬀects of HAI on creativity. It conﬁrms 
that HAI does not have a simple linear eﬀect on individ-
ual creativity, providing a new theoretical perspective 
for studying the relationship between HAI and

2. Theoretical analysis and research 
hypotheses
2.1. The inverted U-shape of human-AI 
interaction on employee creativity
The COR theory is an important motivational theory 
that centres on explaining how individuals protect and 
acquire valuable resources (Halbesleben et al. 2014). 
HAI empowers the sustainable development of employ-
ees’ creativity in the following ways: ﬁrst, employees 
enhance their knowledge integration ability with the 
help of HAI, which is a process of eﬀective use and 
reuse of knowledge resources (Zahra, Neubaum, and 
Hayton 2020). When employees achieve eﬀective 
accumulation of knowledge resources due to AI assist -
ance, it then lays the resource foundation for the stimu -
lation of creativity and innovative behaviour. 
Knowledge integration promotes organisational inno -
vation (Chen et al. 2021; Tsou 2012), and the ability 
to use existing knowledge and information to generate 
diﬀerent combinations and reconﬁgurations is a source 
of innovation. Second, moderate HAI takes employees 
away from tedious tasks (Vrontis et al. 2022) and 
focuses their attention on creative tasks. And due to 
the change in interaction objects, employees are motiv -
ated to move from traditional learning methods to more 
technology-driven learning approaches (Markauskaite 
et al. 2022), which is more conducive to employee crea -
tivity. Finally, AI enhances the accessibility and compre-
hensibility of information with powerful data 
processing capabilities (Gibbard et al. 2025), helping 
employees to apply otherwise incomprehensible infor -
mation to their work and improve their ability to 
innovate.
However, if HAI exceeds certain limits, it may lead to 
negative eﬀects such as information overload and tech -
nical failures, which can cause stress for individuals. On 
the one hand, from a cognitive allocation perspective, 
employees need time to additionally understand the 
internal logic behind AI technology (Skaria, Satam, 
and Khalpey 2020), taking up mental resources that 
could have been used for innovation. Employees 
become accustomed to the solutions provided by AI 
and thus lose the motivation to invest cognitive eﬀort 
in thinking on their own (Lindebaum, Vesa, and den 
Hond 2020), and without a deeper understanding and 
internalisation of these solutions, the brain processes 
the information superﬁcially, making it diﬃcult to gen -
erate innovative ideas. Second, Over-reliance on out -
sourcing tasks to AI fosters mental rigidity and locks 
innovation pathways (Laursen and Salter 2006; Liao 
2002), thereby hindering the stimulation of innovative 
ideas among team members. Finally, excessive AI invol-
vement fosters a sense of technological displacement 
among employees (Wu and Hu 2018), potentially 
prompting them to adopt passive-aggressive strategies 
(such as resisting AI) and suppressing creative explora -
tory behaviours. This leads to hypothesis H1: 
H1: There is an inverted U-shaped relationship between 
HAI and employee creativity.
2.2. The mediating role of cognitive outsourcing
Given the scarcity of cognitive resources and the com -
plexity of processing, employees tend to engage in cog -
nitive outsourcing behaviours when AI can solve 
problems faster. The human cognitive system is charac -
terised by an extended mindset, which oﬄoads some 
internal activities to the environment to aid cognitive 
processing (Weis and Wiese 2022). ‘Knowing where 
information is’ rather than ‘remembering the infor -
mation itself (Sparrow, Liu, and Wegner 2011).’ At 
low levels of HAI, employees do not yet have an in- 
depth understanding of the AI’s capabilities, resulting 
in a low level of cognitive outsourcing. However, as 
the interaction deepens, deeper trust in the AI prompts 
increased cognitive outsourcing. Because most AIs have 
powerful and versatile problem diagnosis and solving 
capabilities (Guha et al. 2023), employees perceive that 
they can accurately perform a variety of tasks with con -
sistent and reliable results. Secondly, AIs provide 
employees with suﬃcient information resources (Dwi -
vedi et al. 2023) to help them process information and 
analyze data (Raisch and Krakowski 2021), and improve 
the eﬃciency of storing and transferring explicit knowl-
edge (Pai et al. 2022). Addressing the problem of hand -
ling large amounts of data, which are considered 
cumbersome and diﬃcult to manage (Wu and Hu 
2018).
However, when the degree of HAI is too high, AI 
may make employees resistant (Sverke et al. 2019), 
and cognitive outsourcing is reduced. A high degree 
of HAI may bring about a large amount of redundant 
information that exceeds the processing capacity of 
employees, creating information overload (Shao et al.

2024). Meanwhile, from a psychological perspective, 
excessive HAI may cause employees to have a ‘self- 
eﬃcacy crisis’ (Yan and Teng 2025). Therefore, exces -
sive HAI subjects employees to resource threats and 
losses, leading them to reduce trust in cognitive out -
sourcing and proactively decrease outsourcing beha -

H2: HAI and cognitive outsourcing have an inverted U-

Employees outsource routine tasks to AI and will 
instead reserve more energy for solving high-level pro -
blems (Jia et al. 2024), utilising AI to reconﬁgure work/f_l-
ows and innovation processes (Brougham and Haar 
2018). At the same time, external storage reduces the 
burden on memory (Barr et al. 2015) and allows for 
easy mobilisation of materials needed for innovation 
when needed. In addition, the diverse information 
interactions brought about by cognitive outsourcing 
can help stimulate employee creativity. Through cogni -
tive outsourcing to AI, employees are exposed to and 
assimilate new information, combine and utilise it to 
generate new ideas to solve work problems. By integrat -
ing diﬀerent pieces of information, employees can be 
motivated to ‘think outside the box’ to stimulate the 
generation of solutions to problems (Teodoridis, Bikard, 
and Vakili 2019). This leads to hypothesis H3: 
H3: Cognitive outsourcing mediates the inverted U- 
shaped relationship between HAI and employee 
creativity.
2.3. Moderating eﬀects of team mastery 
motivational climate
Team mastery motivational climate is a climate that 
emphasises supportive, collaborative, learning, and 
skill acquisition (Cerne et al. 2014), and signiﬁcantly 
acts on proactive innovation behaviours by in/f_luencing 
the way employees manage their resources. Technol -
ogies such as AI have brought about dramatic changes 
to employees’ work tasks, work processes, and division 
of labour (Einola and Khoreva 2023), and can organis -
ational support employees in accessing resources? This 
may explain, in part, the alienation of innovation out -
comes. Therefore, we introduce the variable of team 
mastery motivational climate to explore the moderating 
eﬀect on the relationship between HAI and cognitive 
outsourcing.
Speciﬁcally, in teams with a low team mastery moti -
vational climate, employees are more concerned with 
task completion and individual performance, and mem-
bers choose to maintain the status quo more for 
resource protection and acquisition. When HAI is mod-
erate, members tend to rely on external technical sup -
port to complete cognitive tasks, accelerating cognitive 
outsourcing; while in the over-interaction state, despite 
the risk of resource depletion such as technological 
dependence and cognitive overload, due to the lack of 
motivation to proactively optimise cognitive strategies, 
members intensify cognitive outsourcing behaviours 
due to the pressure of resource maintenance. However, 
in teams with a highly team mastery motivational cli -
mate, members are more inclined to view HAI as a 
tool for capability expansion rather than a substitute 
for cognitive eﬀort, and this motivational orientation 
undermines cognitive outsourcing triggered by exces -
sive interaction. First, teams require employees to 
initiate cognitive validation in AI use, such as question -
ing algorithmic logic and supplementing contextualised 
knowledge, rather than directly adopting AI sugges -
tions. This deep-processing behaviour forces employees 
to continuously activate their metacognitive abilities, 
respond more rationally to challenges during inter -
actions, and avoid cognitive oﬄoading due to techno -
logical convenience (Skulmowski 2023). Secondly, by 
recognising employees’ value, the team mastery motiva-
tional climate enhances employees’ innovative self- 
conﬁdence and sense of eﬃcacy (Yin, Jiang, and Niu 
2024), which makes them believe more in their pro -
blem-solving ability rather than relying on AI, and 
more able to experience autonomy and a sense of con -
trol in their work tasks (Zhang et al. 2022). This leads 
to hypothesis ( Figure 1) H4: 
H4: The team mastery motivational climate moderates 
the inverted U-shaped eﬀect of HAI on cognitive out -
sourcing, i.e. the inverted U-shaped curve between 
HAI and cognitive outsourcing is shifted to the left 
and tends to be /f_latter for employees in a high team 
mastery motivational climate.

According to the report ‘Mapping the Emerging Geo -
graphic Layout of Artiﬁcial Intelligence’ published by 
Harvard Business Review in 2023, China has become 
one of the markets with the most extensive application 
of AI technology and the richest scenarios in the 
world, with a signiﬁcant demonstration eﬀect in the 
ﬁeld of human-AI interaction. This development status 
makes China an important strategic region for AI data 
collection, and its data samples are not only representa -
tive of the industry but also re/f_lect the cutting-edge

trends of global AI applications. Given that we specialise 
in the ﬁeld of human-AI interaction, we mainly target 
frontline employees in China’s smart manufacturing 
industry as respondents. In China’s intelligent manufac-
turing practices, artiﬁcial intelligence is primarily 
applied in typical scenarios such as intelligent quality 
inspection, intelligent optimisation of production pro -

Taking a new energy vehicle manufacturing workshop 
as an example, a factory has deployed 256 data collec -

of the entire production process status through a 
uniﬁed interface. Relying on the intelligent control plat-
form, a team of just seven personnel can eﬃciently oper-
ate 150 or more production equipment, signiﬁcantly 
enhancing human-machine collaboration eﬃciency.
This study adopted a questionnaire approach. Rel -
evant authoritative literature in the ﬁeld was reviewed, 
and English scales were collected and back-translated 
into Chinese. Subsequently, three senior experts in 
related ﬁelds were invited to double-check the content 
of the survey items. The data survey was divided into 
two steps: ﬁrst, a pre-test was conducted within the 
research team, based on which the questionnaire was 
modiﬁed. Second, the questionnaire was formally dis -
tributed mainly online and oﬄine. The data of employ -
ees in diﬀerent regions of China were collected online 
through the questionnaire star platform, and relying 
on the MBA Federation of the university to contact 
the HR department of the enterprises, and using them 
to contact the employees for oﬄine research. Finally, 
to mitigate homogeneity bias, a multi-time-point ques -
tionnaire collection approach was adopted. Speciﬁcally, 
data collection was divided into two phases, with a 
three-week interval between data collection sessions. 
Participants were asked to provide the last four digits 
of their mobile phone number for matching purposes. 
In this study, 50 paper questionnaires and 300 electronic 
questionnaires were distributed, and a total of 305 ques-
tionnaires were returned in the ﬁrst stage, with a recov -
ery rate of 87.14%. Incomplete and invalid 
questionnaires were eliminated in the second stage, 
and 284 valid questionnaires were obtained, and the 
respondents were distributed in Shaanxi, Guangdong, 
Jiangsu, and Beijing. As shown in Table 1 , the main 
characteristics of the respondents are as follows: in 
terms of gender, 41.2% are male and 58.8% are female; 
in terms of age, most of them are younger than 40 
years old (68.1%); in terms of educational background, 
half of the respondents have more than a bachelor’s 
degree (52.4%); in terms of length of service, most of 
them have more than 3 years of work experience 
(86.3%).

4. Empirical analyses
4.1. Common method bias test
A number of approaches were taken to minimise the 
impact of common methodological biases. First, in the 
design of the questionnaire, well-established and vali -
dated scales were selected, and the questions were 
made as concise as possible and could be eﬀectively 
understood. Second, the questionnaire followed the 
code of ethics for social science research and was anon -
ymous to minimise ethical issues and the creation of 
bias. Finally, all items were subjected to principal com -
ponent analysis using Harman’s one-way test. Harman’s 
one-way test showed that after unrotated principal com-
ponent analysis of all items for the four variables: 
human-AI interaction, cognitive outsourcing, employee 
creativity, and team mastery motivational climate, the 
ﬁrst factor obtained explained 36.45% of the variance, 
which is much lower than the baseline of 50%. In con -
clusion, based on the above test results it indicates 
that there are not many CMB issues.

ables is greater than 0.8, which proves that the reliability

interaction terms, the variables were centred.

0.01), and HAI squared term has a signiﬁcant negative

5. Conclusion and discussion
Based on the COR theory, this study examined the non-
linear impact mechanism of human-AI interaction on 
employee creativity. The main ﬁndings of the study 
are as follows:
First, this study conﬁrms the double-edged sword 
eﬀect of human-AI interaction: AI is a double-edged 
sword, and the principle of ‘moderation’ should be fol -
lowed in order to maximise the potential of AI. Moder -
ate interaction empowers employee creativity, while 
excessive interaction is a hindrance to creativity. There -
fore, the results of human-AI interaction are not posi -
tive, in part echoing the controversy (Karhade and 
Dong 2021; Yin, Jiang, and Niu 2024) that over-reliance 
on AI hinders the development of employee creativity 
and corporate innovation. Moreover, this double- 
edged sword eﬀect may be more pronounced in knowl -
edge-intensive industries closely tied to cognitive 
resources, whereas its intensity may diminish for stra -
tegic decision-makers or labour-intensive positions.
Second, cognitive outsourcing plays an important 
mediating role between human-AI interaction and 
employee creativity. According to the COR theory, in 
human-AI interaction scenarios, moderate interaction 
is seen as a way for individuals to gain access to infor -
mation processing eﬃciency, technical support, and

other resources, which in turn facilitates cognitive out -
sourcing. However, excessive interaction may trigger 
the risk of resource depletion, such as technology 
dependence and cognitive overload, leading to a weak -
ening of the cognitive outsourcing eﬀect. High-quality 
cognitive outsourcing can assist employees to better uti-
lise AI capabilities and enhance creativity, and conver -
sely, excessive cognitive outsourcing will lead to a 
decline in employees’ ability to think and innovate. 
Therefore, it is corroborated that cognitive outsourcing 
is an important mediator between human-AI inter -
action and employee creativity.
Third, the moderating role of team mastery motiva -
tional climate in HAI on cognitive outsourcing was ver-
iﬁed. When the HAI is in the moderate range, the team 
mastery motivational climate drives employees to 
actively monitor the interaction process, ﬁlter eﬀective 
information, and avoid falling into technology depen -
dence; when the interaction intensity exceeds the 
threshold, employees will spontaneously adjust the out -
sourcing strategy based on the preciousness of cognitive 
resources and the vigilance against the depreciation of 
competence, and reduce the irrational cognitive cession. 
The self-development orientation advocated by the 
team mastery motivational climate pushes employees 
to actively master AI-related skills, which in turn 
prompts them to invest more resources in tasks that 
support competency development, forming a virtuous 
cycle of return on investment (Nerstad, Roberts, and 
Richardsen 2013).
5.1. Theoretical contributions
First, it reveals the inverted U-shaped eﬀect of HAI on 
employee creativity. There is a theoretical debate 
among established studies about the consequences of 
AI technology application in the workplace: a part of 
scholars hold a single view that AI positively or nega -
tively aﬀects employee creativity in a linear relationship 
(Skaria, Satam, and Khalpey 2020; Verma and Singh 
2022). While another group of scholars believes that 
AI is a double-edged sword, for example, AI is 
employed to promote innovative behaviours by enhan -
cing knowledge workers’ challenging stress assessments, 
while inhibiting innovative behaviours by promoting 
their obstructive stress assessments (Dong et al. 2025). 
Focusing on the dynamic process of human-AI inter -
action, we explored the impact of HAI on employee 
creativity from a nonlinear perspective, breaking 
through the limitations of existing studies that think lin-
early while enriching the study of antecedent variables 
of creativity.
Second, cognitive outsourcing is introduced as a 
mediator, thus making an important contribution to 
the cognitive outsourcing literature. Academic research 
on cognitive outsourcing has remained at the level of 
theoretical analysis, and empirical research has been 
less involved. We innovatively construct the framework 
of ‘human-AI interaction – cognitive outsourcing – 
creativity,’ which reveals the mediating mechanism of 
cognitive outsourcing between HAI and employee crea-
tivity. According to COR theory, cognitive outsourcing

5.2. Management enlightenment
This study presents the following practical insights. 
First, optimise the HAI process to stimulate employees’ 
creativity. Enterprises should guide employees to 
develop a rational perspective on human-AI interaction, 
establish a negative list for AI applications to deﬁne 
usage boundaries, and emphasise data privacy, security, 
and compliance. Additionally, by organising AI skills 
training and establishing AI innovation platforms, cus -
tomised AI training programmes and certiﬁcation path-
ways are designed for employees across diﬀerent 
functions to enhance their human-AI literacy. For 
instance, Accenture launched the AI Navigator for 
Enterprise platform, which aims to systematically elev -
ate employees’ ability to harness AI and maximise its 
value.
Second, correctly recognise the irreversibility of cog -
nitive outsourcing and balance knowledge internalis -
ation and cognitive outsourcing in practice. While 
enjoying the dividends of technological eﬃciency, 
organisations also need to ensure the continuous devel -
opment of employees’ core cognitive abilities. For 
instance, establish task lists to clearly deﬁne the creative 
work employees must complete independently, prevent-
ing them from losing their ability to think autono -
mously and integrate knowledge due to overreliance 
on AI. Through process design, mandate that human

5.3. Limitations and future research direction
Despite the theoretical and practical contributions of 
this paper, there are still limitations, which provide 
opportunities for future research. First, the measure -
ment of HAI is yet to be further explored, and future 
research can be expanded in terms of frequency and 
depth. Secondly, human-AI interaction is a dynamic 
process, and questionnaire surveys may introduce 
bias. Future research could adopt longitudinal designs 
or scenario studies to more eﬀectively evaluate temporal 
dynamics. Finally, the empirical data for this study pri -
marily originates from Chinese employees, limiting the 
generalizability of its ﬁndings. Future research should 
collect cross-cultural data spanning multiple industries 
in regions such as Europe and the United States.
