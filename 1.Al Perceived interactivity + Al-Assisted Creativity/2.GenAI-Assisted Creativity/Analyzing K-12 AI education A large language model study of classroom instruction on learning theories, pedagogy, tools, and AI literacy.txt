There is growing recognition among researchers and stakeholders about the signiﬁcant impact of artiﬁcial 
intelligence (AI) technology on classroom instruction. As a crucial element in developing AI literacy, AI education 
in K-12 schools is increasingly gaining attention. However, most existing research on K-12 AI education relies on 
experiential methodologies and suﬀers from a lack of quantitative analysis based on extensive classroom data, 
hindering a comprehensive depiction of AI education’s current state at these educational levels. To address this 
gap, this article employs the advanced semantic understanding capabilities of large language models (LLMs) to 
create an intelligent analysis framework that identiﬁes learning theories, pedagogical approaches, learning tools, 
and levels of AI literacy in AI classroom instruction. Compared with the results of manual analysis, analysis 
based on LLMs can achieve more than 90% consistency. Our ﬁndings, based on the analysis of 98 classroom 
instruction videos in central Chinese cities, reveal that current AI classroom instruction insuﬃciently foster AI 
literacy, with only 35.71% addressing higher-level skills such as evaluating and creating AI. AI ethics are even 
less commonly addressed, featured in just 5.1% of classroom instruction. We classiﬁed AI classroom instruction 
into three categories: conceptual (50%), heuristic (18.37%), and experimental (31.63%). Correlation analysis 
suggests a signiﬁcant relationship between the adoption of pedagogical approaches and the development of 
advanced AI literacy. Speciﬁcally, integrating Project-based/Problem-based learning (PBL) with Collaborative 
learning appears eﬀective in cultivating the capacity to evaluate and create AI.
1. Introduction
Leveraging big data, large models, and potent computing power, ar-
tiﬁcial intelligence (AI) technology has been rapidly advancing. This 
progress has been evidenced by the development of large language mod-
els (LLMs) like ChatGPT, the evolution of multi-modal models such as 
GPT-4, and the progression of the long video generation model, Sora. 
AI technology has shifted from merely understanding language to com-
prehending and simulating the world, a transition that has drastically 
reshaped various industries. The profound impact of AI technology, par-
ticularly generative AI, on future labor demands is widely recognized. 
As a result, AI literacy has emerged as a critical technical skill in the 
21st century.
Several global organizations and nations are increasingly advocating 
for the inclusion of AI classroom instruction in K-12 education. For ex-
ample, UNESCO (2021)has  undertaken a comprehensive analysis of AI 
course content and AI literacy in primary and secondary schools world-

In recent years, there has been a surge in research on K-12 AI edu-
cation. Empirical studies have explored various aspects of AI education 
implementation, from curriculum design to student outcomes. For in-
stance, Chai et al. (2021)used  the Positive Technological Development 
Framework to assess student learning on AI issues. Kim et al. (2021b)
investigated the eﬀectiveness of AI education programs in developing 
computational thinking skills among K-12 students. Recent systematic 
reviews have shed light on the current state of AI in education. Sanusi et 
al. (2023)conducted  a review of teaching and learning machine learning 
in educational settings, highlighting the growing interest in integrat-
ing machine learning concepts into curricula. Their ﬁndings underscore 
the importance of developing appropriate pedagogical approaches for 
teaching machine learning across diﬀerent educational levels. Lee and 
Kwon (2024) revealed that various topics are covered in K-12 AI ed-
ucation, including fundamental AI concepts, diﬀerent types of AI, AI 
applications, and ethical considerations related to AI. Su et al. (2022)
examined 14 learning experiences in the Asian-Paciﬁc region, mapping 
AI tools, activities, educational models, and research outcomes. Li et al. 
((2024) analyzed learning task design in 47 empirical studies on K-12 
AI education, identifying four dimensions including theoretical foun-
dations, learning contents, pedagogical approach, and assessment and 
outcomes.
Despite the growing body of research on AI education, signiﬁcant 
gaps remain. Most existing studies rely on qualitative methodologies or 
small-scale quantitative analyses, limiting our understanding of AI ed-
ucation’s broader implementation and eﬀectiveness. There is a notable 
lack of data-driven analyses of actual classroom practices in AI educa-
tion. Moreover, while individual studies have explored various aspects 
of AI education, few have attempted to integrate analyses of learning 
theories, pedagogical approaches, learning tools, and AI literacy out-
comes within a single study. This integration is crucial for developing 
a comprehensive understanding of eﬀective AI education practices. The 
advent of LLMs presents a unique opportunity to address these research 
gaps. LLMs’ advanced natural language processing capabilities can be 
leveraged to analyze vast amounts of educational data, including class-
room dialogues and instructional materials. This approach can provide 
insights into AI education practices at a scale and depth previously 
unattainable.
To address the limitations, this study employs LLMs to conduct a 
comprehensive, quantitative analysis of AI education in K-12 settings. 
By analyzing 98 AI classroom instructional videos, we seek to provide 
a data-driven understanding of current AI education practices and their 
eﬀectiveness in fostering AI literacy, aiming at contributing to the grow-
ing body of knowledge on AI education, provide practical insights for 
educators and policymakers, and demonstrate the potential of LLMs as 
a tool for educational research and assessment.
2. Literature review
2.1. LLMs in education
LLMs hold signiﬁcant potential for enhancing teaching and learning 
(Dao et al., 2023). They are applied in various educational areas, in-
cluding automated assessment (Naismith et al., 2023; Khayi et al., 2021; 
Moore et al., 2022), personalized learning (Xiao et al., 2023; Liang et 
al., 2023), and intelligent tutoring (Chen et al., 2023; Hicke et al., 2023; 
Tack & Piech, 2022). For automated assessment, LLMs have been ﬁne-
tuned to evaluate student responses and discourse coherence (Khayi et 
al., 2021; Moore et al., 2022; Naismith et al., 2023). In content cre-
ation, LLMs generate interactive materials like quizzes and multimedia 
resources to boost engagement (Chen & Wu, 2024). For personalized 
learning, LLMs help develop eﬃcient student models and instructional 
exercises (Liang et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2023). In intelligent tutoring, 
LLMs are used to create conversational agents, question-answer assis-
tants, and comprehensive tutoring systems with features like automated 
course planning (Tack & Piech, 2022; Hicke et al., 2023; Chen et al., 
2023). Although current research on LLMs in education does not ex-
plicitly address instructional video analysis, LLMs’ ability to process 
multimodal data, including video, suggests their potential for this ap-
plication (Summaira et al., 2022).
Some studies suggest that LLMs can replicate human-like reasoning 
and decision-making in classroom instruction (Whitehill & LoCasale-
Crouch, 2024; Wang & Demszky, 2023). Whitehill and LoCasale-Crouch 
(2024)f o u n d that LLMs’ analysis of classroom speech closely aligns 
with scores provided by human experts. Similarly, Wang and Demszky 
(2023)noted  that while ChatGPT generates relevant instructional feed-
back, it often lacks originality and depth. Whitehill and LoCasale-Crouch 
(2024)a l s o demonstrated that automating classroom instruction eval-
uation using LLMs and Bag-of-Words models can oﬀer teachers more 
speciﬁc, frequent, and actionable feedback. Wang and Demszky (2023)
further explored the use of generative AI as a tool to coach teachers 
in classroom instruction. Although these studies highlight the potential 
of LLMs in providing instructional feedback, they oﬀer limited insights 
into how teachers can design and strategically reﬁne their pedagogical 
approaches.
2.2. AI education in K-12 settings
AI education has gained prominence in 21st-century learning due 
to the pervasive inﬂuence of AI technologies in various aspects of life. 
Recognizing the importance of AI literacy across educational levels, 
Governments worldwide, including the United States, China, and Ger-
many, have implemented national AI strategic plans (Laupichler et al., 
2022; Ng et al., 2021a). Several nations are actively working to ad-
vance AI education in primary and secondary schools (Casal-Otero et 
al., 2023). In the United States, the AI4K12 program is a notable initia-
tive to integrate AI into schools (Heintz, 2021). In China, the Ministry 
of Education has incorporated AI into the mandatory secondary school 
curriculum (Ottenbreit-Leftwich et al., 2021; Xiao & Song, 2021). Singa-
pore launched the “AI Singapore” project in 2018 to develop students’ 
AI capabilities (Su et al., 2022). South Korea planned to introduce AI 
education for all high school students in 2021 and expand it to younger 
grades by 2025(Kim  et al., 2021a).
AI literacy encompasses the competencies that enable individuals to 
critically evaluate AI technologies and leverage them for eﬀective com-
munication and collaboration (Long & Magerko, 2020). The term “AI 
literacy” emerged from researchers’ focus on preparing students for an 
AI-driven future (Wang & Cheng, 2021). This concept entails the design 
and implementation of AI learning activities, tools, applications, and 
pedagogical models (Yim & Su, 2024). Ng et al. (2021b)proposed  four 
categories of AI literacy: (1) know and understand AI, (2) use and apply 
AI, (3) evaluate and create AI, and (4) AI ethics. Touretzky et al. (2019)
identiﬁed ﬁve key components for K-12 students to learn about AI: per-
ception, representation and reasoning, learning, natural interaction, and 
societal impact. Evaluating AI literacy in K-12 education is crucial. Gov-
ernments and universities worldwide are collaborating with researchers, 
policymakers, software developers, and educators to develop curriculum 
guides, tools, pedagogies, content knowledge, and assessment methods. 
The goal is to equip K-12 learners with AI literacy skills (Ng et al., 2021b; 
Payne & Journell, 2019). However, developing, implementing, and scal-
ing AI curricula presents signiﬁcant challenges. To address these, it is 
essential to establish a robust, evidence-based research foundation in 
AI education that can guide and inform the development of AI curric-
ula(Wang  & Lester, 2023).
2.3. Characteristics of AI classroom instruction in K-12 settings
Three widely accepted learning theories—constructivism,  cogni-
tivism, and behaviorism—serve  as foundational frameworks for teach-

ing and the development of AI curricula (GSE, 2023). Constructivism 
emphasizes active engagement and the construction of knowledge by 
learners (McLeod, 2024). Cognitivism focuses on the mental processes 
involved in learning, such as how information is perceived, processed, 
and stored (Main, 2022). Behaviorism, on the other hand, centers on 
observable stimulus-response behaviors and largely disregards inter-
nal mental processes like cognition and emotion (Krapﬂ, 2016). These 
theories oﬀer diverse perspectives on curriculum design and teaching 
strategies, and their integration into classroom instruction can cater to 
varying learning needs.
Pedagogical approaches in AI education encompass a range of meth-
ods, including direct instruction, interactive learning, project-based 
and problem-based learning, participatory learning, collaborative learn-
ing, hands-on activities, game-based learning, inquiry-based learning, 
and design-oriented learning (Ng et al., 2023; Lee & Kwon, 2024). 
Game-based learning, for instance, is an accessible way to introduce 
AI concepts to beginners with limited computer science backgrounds, 
helping to sustain their motivation (Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et 
al., 2021a, 2021b). Some researchers advocate for hands-on activities 
through case studies to enhance understanding of AI concepts (Shamir 
& Levin, 2022; Vartiainen et al., 2021). In project-based learning, the 
emphasis is on contextualized, real-world problem-solving (Vartiainen 
et al., 2021). While there are pedagogical guidelines for fostering AI lit-
eracy in students (ISTE, 2022; UNESCO, 2021; AI4K12, 2019), current 
research often relies on case studies to identify teaching methods used 
in classrooms, oﬀering a limited perspective that does not account for 
the potential impact of integrating multiple pedagogical approaches.
Educators can select from a variety of AI learning tools based on 
learning objectives and tool functionalities. Lee and Kwon (2024) cat-
egorizes these tools into three groups: programming languages and 
environments, machine learning and AI platforms, and educational plat-
forms and provisioning, which include tools such as Scratch, Scikit-
Learn, and School-Book. When choosing or developing AI tools, edu-
cators must consider factors such as age-appropriateness (Lee & Kwon, 
2024) and alignment with the content being taught. It is also crucial for 
educators to regularly assess the eﬀectiveness of these tools in achieving 
instructional goals and make necessary adjustments based on feedback. 
Thoughtful integration of these tools can enhance teaching and learning 
outcomes.
Regarding AI learning outcomes, research has primarily focused on 
areas such as AI knowledge acquisition (Chiu et al., 2022; Aung et al., 
2022), skills and performance related to AI (Park & Kwon, 2024; Shamir 
& Levin, 2021), motivation and attitudes (Aung et al., 2022; Chiu et al., 
2022), and social impact and career exploration (Lin et al., 2022; Park 
& Kwon, 2024). Notably, AI knowledge is often assessed alongside other 
critical skills, such as problem-solving (Park & Kwon, 2024) and com-
putational thinking (Shamir & Levin, 2021). Although Lee and Kwon 
(2024)acknowledges  the signiﬁcant contributions of these studies in en-
hancing students’ AI literacy, no research has systematically evaluated 
classroom eﬀectiveness based on the four dimensions of AI literacy: un-
derstanding AI, applying AI, evaluating and creating AI, and AI ethics 
(Ng et al., 2021b).
The integration of established learning theories, pedagogical ap-
proaches, and learning tools is essential for developing students’ AI 
literacy through classroom implementation. According to Lee and Kwon 
(2024)’s systematic review, AI classroom instruction topics include (a) 
awareness of AI, (b) AI applications, and (c) future work in AI. However, 
existing research lacks a comprehensive, quantitative analysis of class-
room teaching practices that encompass learning theories, pedagogical 
approaches, learning tools, and AI literacy. This gap hinders a thorough 
understanding of the current state of AI education.
3. Research aims and questions
This study proposes a novel quantitative method for analyzing AI 
classroom instruction videos. The method involves converting class-
room instruction videos into teacher-student dialogues and using LLMs 
to analyze them from four perspectives: learning theories, pedagogical 
approaches, learning tools, and AI literacy. To our knowledge, this is the 
ﬁrst study that employs LLMs for the quantitative analysis of AI educa-
tion in primary and secondary schools. This approach provides teachers, 
educational researchers, and administrators with an eﬃcient and intel-
ligent means to explore a vast amount of AI classroom teaching data, 
facilitating the improvement of pedagogical approaches and the culti-
vation of students’ higher-order AI literacy. The key research questions 
guiding this study are as follows:
RQ1: To what degree are the results of LLM’s analysis of AI classroom 
instruction videos reliable?
RQ2: Which learning theories, pedagogies approaches, and learning 
tools have been commonly used, and what are the outcomes of AI liter-
acy in K-12 AI education?
RQ3: What types of AI classroom instruction can be classiﬁed by 
integrating learning theories, pedagogical approaches, learning tools, 
and AI literacy?
4. Methods
To address the research questions posed, we devised two paths for 
analyzing the AI classroom instruction videos at both primary and sec-
ondary levels: LLMs-based analysis and manual analysis. Drawing from 
the ﬁndings yielded by LLMs-based analyses, we extract and examine 
the salient characteristics inherent in the learning theories, pedagogical 
approaches, learning tools, and AI literacy associated with AI classroom 
instruction in K-12 settings, as shown in Fig.1.
Our study focuses on four independent variables: learning theories, 
pedagogical approaches, learning tools, and AI literacy. To analyze these 
variables, we developed a comprehensive Python-based program as our 
primary research tool. This program utilizes ﬀmpeg for Video-to-Speech 
conversion, OpenAI’s Whisper model for Speech-to-Text transcription, 
and NVIDIA’s Nemo (Nvidia, 2024) for Text-to-Dialogue processing. The 
core analysis is performed using GPT-4 API provided by Microsoft Azure 
cloud services, which interprets the dialogue to extract information on 
our independent variables. We crafted specialized prompts to guide the 
LLM in analyzing these four independent variables. Our tool chain al-
lows for systematic processing of multimodal classroom data, providing 
a robust framework for quantitative analysis of AI education practices.
4.1. Data source
Starting in 2020, China initiated a pilot program in select primary 
and secondary schools to introduce AI courses. A portion of these classes 
were comprehensively video recorded and subsequently shared as open 
educational resources. The dataset for this study is exclusively derived 
from AI classroom instruction videos from a central city in China, en-
compassing a total of 98 videos, each conducted by various teachers. The 
duration of these videos varies, ranging from a minimum of 30 minutes 
to a maximum of 46 minutes.
The collection of 98 instructional videos is divided between primary 
and secondary schools, with 90 videos for primary schools and 8 for 
secondary schools. Speciﬁcally, the breakdown for primary schools in-
cludes 6 videos for 3rd grade, 37 videos for 4th grade, and 46 videos for 
5th grade. In contrast, secondary school features 1 video for 6th grade, 
3 videos for 7th grade, and 1 video for 8th grade. The program is pre-
dominantly focused on primary school, and the majority of the videos 
are concentrated in 4th and 5th grades. There are a total of 62 teach-
ers, divided between primary and secondary schools, with 55 teachers 
for primary schools and 7 for secondary schools. Speciﬁcally, 3 teach-
ers are for 3rd grade, 26 teachers for 4th grade, and 29 teachers for 5th 
grade. There is 1 teacher for 6th grade. Additionally, 3 teachers teach 
across both 4th and 5th grades, and 1 teacher teaches across both 5th 
and 6th grades.

4.2. LLMs-based analysis
This study develops an intelligent AI classroom instruction video 
analysis framework, incorporating a suite of technologies including 
speech recognition, speaker diarization, and long text analysis, as il-
lustrated in Fig.1.
4.2.1. Extraction of class dialogue
Classroom dialogue provides valuable insights into the learning theo-
ries and pedagogical approaches employed by educators, encapsulating 
details about both the educational tools utilized and the resulting stu-
dents’ AI literacy. However, collecting extensive datasets of classroom 
dialogue poses signiﬁcant challenges. In contrast, videos of classroom in-
struction are more accessible and abundant. This study harnesses such 
video resources to extract classroom dialogues, employing a sequen-
tial process that includes Video-to-Speech, Speech-to-Text, and Text-to-
Dialogue conversion.
• Video-to-Speech conversion. The ﬀmpeg library is employed to iso-
late the audio tracks from classroom instruction videos. Nonethe-
less, these original tracks may include background music and noise 
that potentially impede the accuracy of subsequent speech recog-
nition processes. To mitigate this issue, the demucs library is uti-
lized to segregate spoken dialogue from extraneous sounds. Subse-
quently, ﬀmpeg is used once again to adjust the output audio to the 
optimal frequency of 16,000 Hz for speech processing.
• Speech-to-Text conversion. Given the superior capabilities of Ope-
nAI’s Whisper model in speech recognition, this study adopts 
Whisper-large-v3 for the conversion of speech to text. Throughout 
the process, audio segments are partitioned into 30-second chunks, 
and Flash-Attention 2 is employed to expedite the computation. 
The transcription output not only encompasses the textual content 
but also annotates each segment with its corresponding start and 
end times, thereby streamlining the alignment of speaker turns.
• Text-to-Dialogue conversion. To discern the distinct speaker iden-
tities within the text content, the study employs NVIDIA’s NeMo 
(Nvidia, 2024) for speaker diarization. Given the variable number 
of student participants in class discussions, speaker labels are pro-
visionally assigned as “teacher,” “student 1,” “student 2,” and so 
forth. The diarization process involves speech activity detection, 
executed by the “𝑣𝑎𝑑_𝑚𝑢𝑙𝑡𝑖𝑙𝑖𝑛𝑔𝑢𝑎𝑙_𝑚𝑎𝑟𝑏𝑙𝑒𝑛𝑒𝑡” model, and speaker 
identiﬁcation, conducted using the 𝑡𝑖𝑡𝑎𝑛𝑒𝑡_𝑙𝑎𝑟𝑔𝑒model. A matching 
algorithm, predicated on the principles of intersection and cover-
age, is developed to quantify the correspondence between the tran-
scribed text and the identiﬁed speakers. The outcome is a structured 
classroom dialogue, establishing a one-to-one relationship between 
the speakers and their respective textual contributions.

4.2.2. Analysis of class dialogue from four perspectives
Considering the advanced text comprehension abilities and extensive 
knowledge across disciplines inherent in large language models, GPT-
4 is deployed to analyze classroom dialogue through a fourfold lens: 
learning theories, pedagogical approaches, learning tools, and students’ 
AI literacy. This analytical endeavor unfolds in three sequential stages: 
long text chunking, prompt construction, and instruction analysis.
•L o n g text chunking. The upper token input threshold for GPT-4 
stands at 8,192 tokens, yet the classroom dialogues in question 
extend between 10,000 and 19,000 tokens, surpassing the single 
input capacity of the model. To address this, a sentence-level text 
chunking algorithm has been designed. With the aid of the tiktoken 
package, this algorithm tallies tokens for individual sentences and 
segments the text into portions, each not exceeding 6,000 tokens. 
Consequently, the comprehensive classroom dialogue is systemati-
cally segmented into 2 to 4 such chunks for processing.
•P r o m p t construction. The eﬀectiveness of large language model 
outputs hinges on well-crafted input prompts. For optimized AI 
classroom instruction analysis, prompt design is stratiﬁed across 
three critical dimensions. Firstly, the model’s role is articulated, 
casting it as an expert in AI education. Secondly, the model’s tasks 
are delineated, analyzing classroom dialogue from the viewpoints 
of learning theories, pedagogical approaches, learning tools, and 
AI literacy. Learning theories encompass Behaviorism, Cognitivism, 
and Constructivism. Pedagogical approaches span a variety that 
includes Direct instruction, Interactive learning, Experiential learn-
ing, Project-based / Problem-based learning (PBL), Participatory 
learning, Collaborative learning, Hands-on only, Situated learning, 
Game-based learning, Inquiry-based learning, and Design-oriented 
learning. Learning tools are extended to both hardware and soft-
ware resources. AI literacy is deﬁned as knowledge and understand 
AI, use and apply AI, evaluate and create AI and AI ethics. Finally, 
the desired format for the output is stipulated as JSON, to stream-
line the integration with subsequent statistical analyses. Detailed 
prompt exemplars are systematically outlined in AppendixA.
• Classroom instruction analysis. The analysis is conducted using the 
GPT-4 model, which operates on carefully constructed prompts in 
step 2. At the beginning of the analysis process, the overarching 
prompts are introduced to the model. It is then explicitly commu-
nicated that the classroom dialogue will be presented in segmented 
parts due to its length. Upon the sequential input of these parts, the 
model proceeds to the summarization and comprehensive analysis 
phase.
4.3. Manual analysis
The manual evaluation process is centered around the design of anal-
ysis questionnaires and the manual quantitative analysis of the AI class-
room instruction videos. Expertise in education was a prerequisite for 
the graduate students who were enlisted to scrutinize the AI classroom. 
Questionnaire Star served as the chosen platform for the aggregation of 
the responses.
• Analysis questionnaire design. The questionnaire is comprehen-
sively constructed to explore four fundamental dimensions: learn-
ing theory, pedagogical approaches, learning tools, and AI literacy. 
Formatted as multiple-choice questions, it comprises a set of ﬁve 
items, including one that speciﬁcally requests the name of the AI 
classroom. Every question enumerates all potential answers, ensur-
ing alignment with the prompts of the large language model. For 
instance, within the learning theory category, the choices span Be-
haviorism, Cognitivism, and Constructivism. Please see AppendixB
for the detailed questionnaire.
•M a n u a l quantitative analysis. Each AI instructional Video spans 
40 minutes, making the manual analysis of each video quite time-
consuming. To address this, we employed a random sampling strat-
egy, selecting 10 videos from AI classroom instruction videos. We 
enlisted the help of ﬁve volunteers, with each volunteer tasked with 
analyzing two videos. The process involved the volunteers viewing 
the instructional video for their assigned AI teaching before pro-
ceeding to complete the corresponding evaluation questionnaire.
• Test-Retest reliability. It measures an instrument’s reliability by 
evaluating the consistency of participant responses to survey ques-
tions with repeated testing (Smithies et al., 2024; Wisner et al., 
2024; Bergman et al., 2024). To ensure the reliability of our re-
search design, we conducted a second evaluation of the classroom 
videos using a group of ﬁve volunteers. The overall correlation 
between the initial and subsequent measurements was 0.86, in-
dicating high consistency. Speciﬁcally, we found correlations of 
0.87 for learning theory, 0.88 for teaching methods, a perfect 1.00 
for learning tool selection, and 0.73 for AI literacy. These results 
demonstrate strong reliability across most categories, though the 
slightly lower correlation for AI literacy suggests potential areas 
for improvement in assessment criteria or evaluator training.

4.5. Further exploration of analysis results
To delve deeper into the characteristics of AI classroom instruction 
in K-12 settings from four key perspectives—learning  theories, peda-
gogical approaches, learning tools, and AI literacy —  we expanded our 
research to the classiﬁcation of AI classroom instruction and the inter-
relationships among the four aspects. This comprehensive analysis was 
based on analysis results generated through LLMs. We treated the se-
lection within each aspect as a separate dimension, resulting in a total 
of 19 distinct dimensions. Speciﬁcally, learning theories comprise 3 di-
mensions, pedagogical approaches encompass 10 dimensions, learning 
tools are divided into 2 dimensions, and AI literacy is broken down into 
4 dimensions. The analytical outcomes for 98 AI classroom instruction 
videos were organized into a 98 by 19 matrix. Entries in the matrix are

4.5.2. Classroom instruction characteristics analysis from four perspectives
To analyze the correlations among various dimensions, we utilized 
a 19-dimensional matrix and implemented the AGENS algorithm for hi-
erarchical clustering. This process consists of three main steps:
• Initialization of Cluster Set. Each dimension is initially treated as 
an individual cluster. In our study, each cluster is represented as a 
row vector containing 98 elements, corresponding to the number 
of classroom instruction videos analyzed. Consequently, we begin 
with a total of 19 clusters.
• Circular Hierarchical Clustering. We employ the Euclidean distance 
metric to assess the similarity between clusters. The two clusters 
demonstrating the closest proximity are merged to form a new, 
combined cluster. This process is indicative of the hierarchical clus-
tering principle, where clusters at lower levels of the tree exhibit 
higher similarity. After each merging step, we recalculate the sim-
ilarities among all remaining clusters and continue this iterative 
clustering until only one cluster remains or a predeﬁned condition 
is met.
•D r a w i n g Hierarchical Relationship Tree. To visualize the hierarchi-
cal relationships among the dimensions, we utilize the dendrogram 
package to create a dendrogram based on the 19x98 matrix. To 
enhance the clarity and relevance of the dendrogram, we prepro-
cess the data by excluding dimensions where over 80% of the data 
points are either 1 or 0. This step helps in focusing on the dimen-
sions that provide more variability and potentially more insightful 
correlations.

6. Discussion
The integration of AI into K-12 education has gained signiﬁcant 
attention, with various countries and international organizations estab-
lishing frameworks to guide AI course development. However, existing 
AI education research lacks data-driven analyses of classroom practices 
and comprehensive studies integrating learning theories, pedagogical 
approaches, tools, and AI literacy outcomes. This study addresses this 
gap by employing LLMs to construct an intelligent analysis framework, 
enabling the classiﬁcation of AI classroom instruction based on four key 
perspectives: learning theory, pedagogical approaches, learning tools, 
and AI literacy.
6.1. Comparison of LLMs-based analysis and manual analysis on AI 
classroom instruction
To validate the eﬀectiveness of our LLM-based analysis framework, 
we conducted a comparative study between LLM-based analysis and tra-
ditional manual analysis of AI classroom instruction. Our ﬁndings reveal 
a high degree of consistency between LLM-based and manual analyses 
across various dimensions of AI education. This aligns with recent re-
search (Parker et al., 2024), which demonstrated that GPT-4 achieves 
human or near-human level performance across a range of tasks in an-
alyzing education feedback surveys.
In terms of learning tools and learning theory analysis, the results of 
LLM-based analysis show a high degree of consistency with manual anal-
ysis. The perfect 100% match in identifying learning tools demonstrates 
the LLM’s proﬁciency in recognizing and categorizing speciﬁc educa-
tional resources mentioned in the course transcripts. This suggests that 
LLMs can be particularly eﬀective in tasks requiring the identiﬁcation 
of concrete, well-deﬁned elements within educational contexts.
While there was strong concordance in identifying most pedagogical 
approaches, minor discrepancies were observed in 1-2 courses for Col-
laborative Learning, PBL, and Experiential Learning. These diﬀerences 
likely stem from the LLM’s reliance on textual data alone, whereas man-
ual analysis beneﬁted from visual cues in the video recordings. This 
limitation highlights the potential for enhancing LLM performance by 
incorporating multimodal data in future analyses.
The assessment of learning outcomes presented the greatest chal-
lenge, with the LLM showing high accuracy in identifying ‘Knowledge 
and Understanding AI’ and ‘Using and Applying AI’, but occasionally 
missing ‘Evaluate and Create AI’ and ‘AI Ethics’ in 1-2 lessons. This dis-
crepancy can be attributed to the complexity of inferring higher-order

6.2. Current status of AI classroom instruction in k-12 settings based on 
LLMs analysis
Our LLM-based analysis of AI classroom instruction in K-12 schools 
reveals a complex landscape that both aligns with recent research ﬁnd-
ings and highlights signiﬁcant gaps in current AI education practices. 
This comprehensive examination provides crucial insights into the the-
oretical underpinnings, pedagogical approaches, and content focus of 
existing AI curricula, addressing several key areas in the literature.
The analysis categorized AI classroom instruction into three
main types: conceptual (50%), heuristic (18.37%), and experimental 
(31.63%). This distribution basically aligns with recent studies on AI 
education content (Lee & Kwon, 2024), reﬂecting a balanced approach 
between theoretical understanding and practical application. However, 
this balance may not be optimal for developing the full spectrum of AI 
literacy skills required in the rapidly evolving ﬁeld of artiﬁcial intelli-
gence.
Conceptual instruction, focusing on broad AI concepts through direct 
instruction and participatory learning, provides a crucial foundation for 
AI understanding (Alonso, 2020). However, our ﬁndings suggest that 
this approach alone may be insuﬃcient for developing higher-order AI 
skills, highlighting a potential area for curriculum enhancement. Exper-
imental instruction, emphasizing PBL and group collaboration, aligns 
with research demonstrating the positive impact of hands-on AI tools 
on students’ computational thinking and self-eﬃcacy (Shamir & Levin, 
2022). This approach shows promise in fostering higher-level AI lit-
eracy but is currently underrepresented in the curriculum. Heuristic 
instruction, while employing inquiry-based and PBL approaches, lacks 
suﬃcient practical and collaborative elements. This gap presents an op-
portunity for improvement, as collaborative learning has been shown to 
enhance AI literacy and motivation.
The analysis reveals a preference for traditional teaching methods 
over innovative approaches like situated learning and game-based learn-
ing. There is the signiﬁcant deﬁciency in teaching high-level AI skills 
(evaluate and create AI), with only 35.71% of classroom instruction en-
gaging in these areas. Our ﬁndings suggest that current approaches may 
not adequately support the development of higher-order AI skills, indi-
cating a need for instructional redesign to address this shortfall.
Perhaps most concerning is the signiﬁcant underrepresentation of 
AI ethics in classroom instruction, with only 5 out of 98 instances ad-
dressing this critical area. This ﬁnding is particularly alarming given 
the growing emphasis on ethical AI. Researchers have emphasized the 
importance of developing an understanding of the risks, biases, and ben-
eﬁts associated with AI as a crucial component of AI literacy (Jang et 
al., 2022; Monteith et al., 2022). The lack of focus on AI ethics in cur-
rent instruction represents a critical gap between educational practices 
and the ethical considerations necessary for responsible AI development 
and use.
6.3. Directions for improving AI classroom instruction in k-12 settings
Our analysis of AI courses indicates signiﬁcant potential for enhance-
ment in AI literacy, pedagogical strategies, and content design for AI 
education in primary and secondary schools.
The statistics analysis has highlighted two critical areas for enhanc-
ing AI literacy in K-12 education: enhancing AI Ethics education and 
promoting advanced AI literacy. It is essential to integrate AI ethics more 
thoroughly across all AI courses, covering topics such as privacy, secu-
rity risks, discrimination, and potential misuse of AI technologies. This 
integration will enable students to comprehend the broader implications 
of AI and encourage responsible usage. Additionally, there is a need to 
develop curriculum segments speciﬁcally designed to enhance students’ 
abilities to evaluate and creatively engage with AI. Such segments could 
involve advanced projects where students are tasked with designing AI 
solutions or critically analyzing existing AI systems.
Our correlation analysis indicates that combining PBL with Collabo-
rative learning can eﬀectively develop higher-level AI skills. However, 
currently, less than 40% of teachers utilize these proven methods. Addi-
tionally, innovative educational approaches like Situated Learning and 
Game-based Learning, despite their potential to boost engagement and 
contextual understanding, are rarely implemented. Future AI course in-
struction should expand the use of eﬀective instructional strategies and 
incorporate both Situated and Game-Based Learning to enhance the ed-
ucational experience.
The AI teaching in k-12 schools is often characterized by coarse-
grained content and lacks a systematic structure, which impedes the 
development of advanced AI literacy. To rectify this, future AI education 
should focus on creating a more structured curriculum that progressively 
builds upon students’ knowledge and skills. This could entail a series of 
interconnected courses that gradually increase in complexity and depth, 
thereby fostering a robust and comprehensive understanding of AI.
We recommend that future research initiatives focus on integrating 
multi-modal data sources to provide a more comprehensive understand-
ing of AI classroom dynamics. Additionally, we propose that researchers 
design and implement extended research projects that track students’ 
AI literacy development from elementary through secondary education. 
These studies should aim to evaluate how diﬀerent instructional ap-
proaches, particularly those combining collaborative learning and PBL, 
inﬂuence students’ AI knowledge acquisition, skill development, and ca-
reer aspirations over time.
For curriculum writers, we recommend developing dedicated mod-
ules that explore ethical implications of AI, including privacy concerns, 
potential biases, and societal impacts. These modules should be woven 
throughout the curriculum, progressively building students’ understand-
ing of AI ethics as they advance in their studies. Furthermore, curriculum 
writers should focus on designing project-based units that challenge stu-
dents to critically assess existing AI systems and create their own AI 
solutions.
We strongly recommend that teachers explore and implement in-
structional strategies that integrate collaborative learning with PBL ap-
proaches. This could involve designing group projects where students 
collaboratively tackle real-world AI challenges, fostering both teamwork 
skills and practical AI knowledge.
For policymakers, we recommend developing comprehensive AI edu-
cation standards that explicitly require the integration of ethics through-
out the curriculum. These standards should also emphasize the impor-
tance of hands-on, project-based learning experiences that engage stu-
dents in evaluating and creating AI solutions.
6.4. Limitations
While our study employs LLMs to conduct a quantitative analysis of 
AI classroom instruction, the approach presents certain limitations.
Our current analysis framework primarily relies on dialogue data 
from teacher-student interactions, lacking multi-modal data integration. 
This limitation can reduce the visibility of less dialogue-intensive peda-
gogical approaches like Collaborative learning. Future research should 
explore ways to incorporate visual and other non-verbal data to provide 
a more comprehensive analysis of classroom dynamics.

A signiﬁcant limitation of our study is the analysis of pedagogical 
approaches based on single-lesson observations. This approach is par-
ticularly problematic for comprehensive methodologies like PBL, which 
typically unfold over multiple lessons or entire course units. Our ﬁnding 
is that 42.86% of courses implemented in PBL may not accurately rep-
resent the true prevalence of this methodology, as a single lesson may 
not capture the full implementation of PBL in a course. This limitation 
extends to other long-term teaching strategies that require sustained im-
plementation across multiple sessions. Future research should consider 
longitudinal observations or analysis of full course syllabi to more accu-
rately assess the use of complex teaching methodologies in AI education.
The assessment of AI literacy development is based on observed 
teaching content and methods, which may not accurately reﬂect actual 
student outcomes. Future studies should incorporate direct measures of 
student learning and skill development to validate the eﬀectiveness of 
diﬀerent instructional approaches. Our study focused on publicly avail-
able AI classroom instruction videos, which may not represent the full 
spectrum of AI education approaches globally. This limitation could 
introduce selection bias, potentially overrepresenting certain types of 
instruction or educational contexts.
The use of LLMs as the primary analytical tool may introduce bi-
ases based on the training data and algorithms used. Future research 
should consider triangulating LLM analysis with other methodologies to 
enhance the robustness of ﬁndings. The statistical analyses conducted 
in this study are based on the LLM’s interpretation of classroom instruc-
tion, which may not capture all nuances of human-led analysis. Further 
validation of these statistical outcomes through human expert review 
would strengthen the reliability of our ﬁndings.
Our analysis does not account for potential variations in student de-
mographics, prior knowledge, or learning styles. This limitation could 
mask important diﬀerences in the eﬀectiveness of various instructional 
approaches for diﬀerent student populations. Additionally, all experi-
mental data in this study are sourced from publicly available AI class-
room instruction videos. This reliance on public data may introduce a 
bias towards instruction that is more likely to be recorded and shared, 
potentially excluding valuable insights from less visible educational con-
texts.
7. Conclusion
This study addresses a critical gap in AI education research by de-
veloping an innovative framework for analyzing classroom instruction 
using LLMs. Our approach provides a quantitative analysis of actual 
classroom data, moving beyond the limitations of previous research that 
primarily relied on literature reviews. Our ﬁndings reveal key insights 
into the current state of AI education in K-12 settings, identifying three 
distinct types of AI courses: conceptual, heuristic, and experimental, 
with conceptual courses being the most prevalent.
Our analysis indicates a signiﬁcant gap in training for advanced AI 
literacy skills, particularly in evaluating and creating AI systems. We 
also found a concerning underrepresentation of AI ethics in current cur-
ricula. These ﬁndings underscore the need for more balanced AI curric-
ula that incorporate ethics, advanced AI skills, and innovative teaching 
methods. Our study reveals that combining collaborative learning with 
PBL can eﬀectively develop higher-level AI skills, though PBL was im-
plemented in less than half of the observed courses.
These insights have signiﬁcant implications for curriculum devel-
opers, educators, and policymakers, emphasizing the need for more 
comprehensive and ethically grounded AI education. While our study 
provides valuable insights, we acknowledge its limitations, particularly 
in analyzing only processed teacher-student dialogue data and basing 
observations on single lessons.
Future research should address these limitations by integrating 
multi-modal large models for more comprehensive analysis and con-
ducting longitudinal studies to better understand the long-term impact 
of AI education approaches. As AI continues to shape our world, ensur-
ing eﬀective AI literacy education for young learners remains crucial 
for preparing them for the challenges and opportunities of an AI-driven 
future. This study represents a signiﬁcant step forward in understand-
ing and improving AI education, setting the stage for evidence-based 
enhancements to AI classroom instruction in K-12 schools.
