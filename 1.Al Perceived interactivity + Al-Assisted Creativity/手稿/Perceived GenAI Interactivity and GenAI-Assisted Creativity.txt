The Impact of Perceived GenAI Interactivity on GenAI-Assisted Creativity: The Mediating Role of Flow Experience and Cognitive Engagement

Abstract
Academic motivation and self-regulated learning have each been linked to academic misconduct, and separately to AI literacy, but no study has examined whether these two mechanisms jointly carry AI literacy's influence on misconduct in the GenAI context. This study addresses this gap by testing a serial mediation model in which AI literacy shapes academic misconduct through academic motivation and self-regulated learning, using a three-wave longitudinal survey and structural equation modeling among Chinese university students. Grounded in Ng et al.'s (2021b) AI Literacy Model and Molenaar's (2022a, 2022b) Hybrid Human-AI Regulation Model, this study is expected to clarify whether AI literacy reduces misconduct primarily by sustaining students' motivation to learn or by preserving their regulatory control over GenAI-assisted work, informing targeted, low-cost classroom interventions—such as brief AI-literacy modules embedded in orientation courses.

1.1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence has profoundly impacted education (Celik, 2023; Montebello, 2018). Recently, there has been growing interest in exploring the interactions between people and AI and AI education systems. However, much of this interest has concentrated on AI's instructional and technical affordances, with comparatively little attention paid to the integrity-related consequences of students' growing access to generative AI (GenAI) tools such as ChatGPT (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Perkins et al., 2024). The rapid diffusion of GenAI into higher education has unsettled the traditional assumptions underlying assessment design, evaluation, and academic honesty (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024). Indeed, more than a third of university students surveyed in Australia reported having used a chatbot for assistance with an assessment, and many of them did not regard this as a breach of academic integrity (Gruenhagen et al., 2024). This finding suggests that students' understanding of what counts as legitimate versus dishonest AI use—that is, their AI literacy—may be a critical yet underexamined antecedent of academic misconduct in the GenAI era.

The AI shift has generated challenges and opportunities for teaching and learning (Ding et al., 2024; Pan & Wang, 2025; Wijaya et al., 2024). The expansion of higher education and advanced technologies has intensified concerns about academic dishonesty (Shomotova et al., 2025). Although AI has great potential to facilitate learning, its integration into higher education settings generally requires students to navigate multilayered ethical decisions about legitimate and illegitimate AI use (Al-Abdullatif & Alsubaie, 2024; An et al., 2023). Academic misconduct—broadly defined as behavior that violates the standards of honesty and fairness expected in academic work—has long been recognized as a pervasive problem in higher education, with the majority of students in most countries admitting to having cheated at least once during their studies (Stephens, 2019; Stephens et al., 2024). A nationwide survey of 4,493 undergraduates in New Zealand found that approximately two-thirds reported engaging in at least one form of academic misconduct in the preceding year (Stephens et al., 2024). 

First, although the potential for AI-enhanced learning has grown rapidly, there is a notable lack of both theoretical and empirical research on AI Literacy in the context of academic integrity in higher education (Lu et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024). Although AI literacy has been conceptualized as a multidimensional competency spanning the understanding, application, and evaluation of AI (Ng et al., 2021b), empirical research has rarely tested whether students' overall AI literacy translates into stronger academic motivation and self-regulated learning when they use AI tools for their academic work (Lund et al., 2025; Perkins et al., 2024). Few studies have systematically examined how AI literacy shapes students' academic motivation and self-regulated learning (Nabhan & Habók, 2026).

Second, although interest in AI Literacy is increasing, the precise relationship between AI literacy and academic misconduct remains largely unexplored. While academic motivation and self-regulated learning have each been studied as predictors of academic misconduct (Murdock & Anderman, 2006; He & Wang, 2026), few studies have integrated these two well-established but largely separate literatures with the emerging literature on AI literacy into a single research framework capable of explaining the motivational and self-regulatory pathways through which AI literacy shapes academic misconduct (Wu et al., 2024).

Although China has actively integrated AI tools into its higher education landscape, systematic empirical research on how Chinese university students' AI literacy relates to their academic motivation and self-regulated learning remains scarce (A. Zhao et al., 2020), leaving open how institutional and cultural factors shape the dynamics of academic misconduct in this population (Brown & Wang, 2016; Zhao et al., 2020).

Artiﬁcial intelligence literacy has become increasingly critical as AI integrates into high education, yet understanding of the factors inﬂuencing students’ AI literacy and its relationship with self-regulated learning remains limited (D. Wang et al., 2026). Derived from autonomous learning or learner autonomy, self-regulated learning (SRL) has garnered extensive attention among educational scholars, enabling people to decide what, when, and how to conduct learning, resulting in lifelong learning (Huang & Derakhshan, 2025). Relatively few studies have examined how learners self-regulate their learning with AI (Huang & Derakhshan, 2025), and to date, the interrelationships among AI literacy, academic misconduct, academic motivation, and self-regulated learning have not been systematically examined (Shi et al., 2025). Existing research on AI and SRL has focused primarily on how AI tools support and enhance SRL—for example, Lim et al. (2023) found that personalized scaffolding based on real-time analysis significantly enhances students' SRL—while comparatively little attention has been paid to the reverse pathway, namely how differences in students' own AI literacy shape their capacity for self-regulated learning (Ng et al., 2021b; D. Wang et al., 2026). Although SRL has been shown to improve students' AI literacy competencies, few studies have investigated this intersection in the opposite direction (Bećirović et al., 2026; K. Wang et al., 2025).

To address these identified research gaps, this study will investigate the impact of AI Literacy (Independent Variable) on Academic Misconduct (Dependent Variable) through the mediating effects of Academic Motivation and Self-Regulation Learning (Mediator Variables) in a serial mediation research model. Based on Ng et al.'s (2021b) AI Literacy Model and Molenaar's (2022a, 2022b) Hybrid Human-AI Regulation Model, this study aims to bridge these gaps by investigating the interrelationships between these constructs among Chinese university students.

1.2. Literature Review and Hypothesis

1.2.1. Perceived GenAI Interactivity(PGAII)
The increasing incorporation of AI into teaching and learning environments has sparked interest in AI literacy (Al-Abdullatif & Alsubaie, 2024; B. Wang et al., 2023). One approach to AI education is to improve AI literacy (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Chung et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the effective classroom integration of AI demands that student be AI-literate, encompassing understanding, utilization, monitoring, and critical reflection on AI applications in teaching (Ding et al., 2024).

AI literacy is a newly emerging concept that seeks to define, teach, and evaluate the knowledge and skills related to AI. It refers to an individual's ability to comprehend, utilize, monitor, and critically evaluate AI applications and AI-generated content, even without programming knowledge or the ability to develop AI models (Long & Magerko, 2020; Wu et al., 2024).

The term “AI literacy” was first coined by Burgsteiner et al. (2016) and Kandlhofer et al. (2016), who described the competencies required to understand the basic knowledge and concepts of AI and to use AI technologies. In addition, Long and Magerko (2020) defined it as a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate, communicate, and collaborate effectively with AI and use it as a tool, online, at home, and in the workplace. Furthermore, Ng et al. (2021b, 2021a) added AI to every student’s AI literacy in work settings and everyday life and proposed it as a fundamental skill for everyone, not just computer scientists. He incorporated the model of Bloom’s Taxonomy, Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK), and AI concepts, practices, and perspectives into the instructional design of AI literacy education (Ng et al., 2021b, 2021a).

Carolus et al. (2023) stated that AI literacy covers “competencies needed to interact with AI technology in a self-determined and rational manner.” Pinski and Benlian (2023) provided a definition of General AI literacy, describing it ashumans “socio-technical competence consisting of knowledge regarding human and AI actors in human-AI interaction, knowledge of the AI process steps, that is, input, processing, and output, and experience in AI interaction.” Laupichler et al. (2023) define the concept of AI literacy as “competencies that include basic knowledge and analytical evaluation of AI, in addition to the critical use of AI applications by ordinary non-expert individuals.” They did not include (programming skills) in their definitions because they considered them a separate set of competencies beyond AI literacy (Laupichler et al., 2023).

Recently, several studies have developed AI literacy frameworks for education (Kong et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2021b; Wong et al., 2020). AI literacy does not require detailed technical expertise in AI applications. However, it spans four dimensions: understanding and comprehension of AI, practical application of AI, assessment of AI applications, and ethical considerations of AI (L. Zhao et al., 2022).

Long and Magerko (2020) provided a conceptual framework of AI literacy composed of five overarching themes derived from the literature (Long & Magerko, 2020).Wang et al. (2023) proposed a theoretical framework to conceptualize AI Literacy, comprising four constructs: Usage, defined as the ability to exploit AI technology to proficiently perform tasks; Awareness, the ability to identify and comprehend AI systems during interactions; Evaluation, the capacity to critically analyze and assess AI applications and their outcomes; and Ethics, which involves recognizing responsibilities and risks associated with using AI systems. 

1.2.2. GenAI-Assisted Creativity(GAIAC):
Academic misconduct refers to behavior that violates the standards of honesty, fairness, and responsibility expected in academic work (Stephens, 2019). It has been operationalized in the literature as a multidimensional behavioral construct spanning collusion (e.g., unpermitted collaboration on individual assignments), misuse of resources (e.g., plagiarism, unauthorized use of materials), and fraud and contract cheating (Perry et al., 2026; Rettinger et al., 2024; Stephens et al., 2024). The emergence of GenAI tools has introduced new and complex forms of misconduct—including submitting AI-generated content as one's own work and using AI paraphrasing tools to disguise plagiarism—that blur the lines between legitimate AI assistance and academic fraud (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024).

Scholars have approached academic misconduct from multiple perspectives. Stephens (2019) characterized the problem as epidemic in scale, noting that the majority of tertiary students globally report having cheated at least once during their studies, with this rate remaining remarkably stable across five decades and numerous countries. From a moral-psychological perspective, Stephens (2018) demonstrated that misconduct is driven not primarily by moral ignorance but by moral disengagement—students who cheat frequently possess accurate moral knowledge but use self-exculpatory rationalizations, such as blaming the instructor or normalizing the behavior among peers, to neutralize their ethical standards in specific situations. At a multi-level theoretical level, Perry et al. (2026) found that moral attitudes, institutional climate, and peer norms each contribute unique variance to misconduct, and that no single explanatory factor is sufficient on its own, confirming that academic misconduct is a multiply determined behavioral outcome.

Prevalence data underscore the scale of the problem. A nationwide New Zealand survey of 4,493 undergraduates found that approximately two-thirds (64.8%) reported at least one act of academic misconduct in the preceding year, with unauthorized collaboration being the most common behavior (Stephens et al., 2024). That survey incorporated the first items capturing AI-facilitated misconduct and found an initial 14.8% self-reported prevalence rate—likely a significant underestimate given that data collection concluded just two months before ChatGPT's public release (Stephens et al., 2024). Subsequent studies have confirmed that AI-facilitated misconduct is rapidly increasing and that many students do not recognize unauthorized AI assistance as a breach of academic integrity, highlighting the urgent need for research on the individual factors that shape these misconduct decisions (Gruenhagen et al., 2024; Lund et al., 2025).

Despite extensive research, significant gaps remain. Most existing studies predate the widespread availability of GenAI tools and therefore do not capture its most emergent forms of dishonest behavior (Lund et al., 2025; Perkins et al., 2024). Moreover, while moral attitudes and peer norms have been identified as predictors, the role of AI literacy—specifically whether higher AI literacy leads to more ethical decision-making around GenAI use—has not been empirically tested (Ng et al., 2021b; Stephens et al., 2024). Research from Chinese higher education contexts is particularly sparse, leaving open how cultural assessment norms and institutional factors moderate the AI literacy–misconduct relationship (Brown & Wang, 2016; Zhao et al., 2020). The most relevant instrument for the present study is the RAINZ-S scale (Stephens et al., 2024), developed from a nationwide survey of 4,493 New Zealand undergraduates and among the first instruments to incorporate AI-facilitated misconduct items, making it well suited to capturing both traditional and GenAI-related forms of academic dishonesty.

1.2.3. Flow Experience(FE)
Academic motivation refers to the internal and external factors that drive students to learn and perform academically (Ryan & Deci, 2020). Academic motivation—whether intrinsic, driven by internal satisfaction and interest, or extrinsic, driven by external rewards like grades and recognition—also significantly influences how engaged students are in their academic work (Ryan & Deci, 2020; Shin & Bolkan, 2021).

Vallerand et al. (1992) operationalized academic motivation along a self-determination continuum comprising three types of intrinsic motivation (to know, to accomplish things, and to experience stimulation), three types of extrinsic motivation (external regulation, introjected regulation, and identified regulation), and amotivation—the relative absence of any motivation toward academic work. This taxonomy provides the basis for examining how AI literacy relates to the quality, rather than merely the quantity, of students' academic motivation, which in turn is theorized to shape their engagement in academic misconduct (Murdock & Anderman, 2006).

Despite these well-established literatures, a direct empirical link between AI literacy and academic motivation has rarely been tested. Motivational achievement goal structures have long been shown to predict academic misconduct—with mastery-oriented motivation reducing, and performance-avoidance motivation increasing, students' likelihood of cheating (Anderman & Midgley, 2004; Murdock & Anderman, 2006)—yet this literature predates the widespread availability of GenAI tools and does not account for how students' competence in using these tools might itself shape their underlying motivational orientation. 

This gap is particularly consequential in the Chinese higher education context, where GenAI tools have been rapidly adopted but where empirical research on their motivational consequences for students remains scarce (A. Zhao et al., 2020). Understanding whether AI-literate students sustain stronger academic motivation—or, alternatively, experience reduced motivation as they offload cognitive effort to AI tools—has direct implications for how institutions design AI-integrated curricula and assessment policies aimed at preserving academic integrity in the GenAI era (Huang & Derakhshan, 2025; Shin & Bolkan, 2021).

1.2.4. Cognitive Engagement(CE)
Self-Regulation Learning refers to learners’ ability to actively set goals, monitor progress, and adjust strategies to achieve desired learning outcomes (Zimmerman, 2002). Pintrich (2000) articulated SRL as “an active, constructive process whereby learners set goals for their learning and then attempt to monitor, regulate, and control their cognition, motivation, and behavior, guided and constrained by their goals and the contextual features in the environment” (p. 453). GenAI-SRL describes how learners use GenAI systems to plan, monitor, and evaluate their learning while also managing their interactions with these intelligent tools (Banihashem et al., 2025; Chang & Sun, 2024).

SRL, involving the strategies students use to plan, monitor, and evaluate their learning, further enhances academic engagement by empowering students to take control of their educational journeys (Schunk & Zimmerman, 2012; Teng & Zhang, 2020). Students with strong SRL skills not only demonstrate greater persistence and stress management in learning (Namaziandost et al., 2023) but also eﬀectively leverage external support—such as teachers, peers, or technological tools—when dealing with complex or unfamiliar learning tasks (Xia et al., 2023). 

With the rapid proliferation of AI, particularly generative AI, AI tools are increasingly used to support students’ SRL processes. Empirical studies show that AI applications such as chatbots, adaptive feedback systems, and intelligent tutoring systems can provide personalized support during the forethought, performance, and reﬂection phases of SRL (Ng et al., 2024c; Wang and Lin, 2023). For example, the SRLbot developed by Ng et al. (2024b) offers dynamic learning suggestions based on students' real-time learning states, while a separate study demonstrates that ChatGPT can significantly enhance learners' planning and self-monitoring during the reflection stage (Fan et al., 2025).

A small but growing body of research has begun to examine the direct relationship between AI literacy and SRL. Ng et al. (2021) emphasized in their review that AI literacy involves not only technical operation skills but also the abilities to critically evaluate and ethically use AI—dimensions closely associated with SRL sub-processes such as goal setting, monitoring, and strategy adjustment. Furthermore, Zhang and Chen (2025) identiﬁed four learner proﬁles with diﬀerent patterns in a survey of 1,704 Chinese university students. Their ﬁndings revealed that “AI-Inclined” learners, despite having relatively high AI literacy, tended to over-rely on AI due to weaker SRL, whereas “Potential” learners displayed a mutually reinforcing developmental pattern between AI literacy and SRL. These results highlight that diﬀerences in AI literacy are crucial for understanding students’ SRL performance.

Recent studies have also examined the potential synergy between SRL theory and AI (Jin et al., 2023; Molenaar, 2022; Molenaar et al., 2023), though this literature has focused primarily on how AI tools support SRL rather than on how students' own AI literacy shapes their capacity for self-regulation—the reverse pathway examined in the present study.

1.2.5. AI Literacy Model and Molenaar's Hybrid Human-AI Regulation Model
This study's theory is rooted in two models that jointly explain the pathways linking AI literacy to academic misconduct: Ng et al.'s (2021b) AI Literacy Model and Molenaar's (2022a, 2022b) Hybrid Human-AI Regulation Model. Together, these provide the theoretical architecture for the proposed serial mediation model without reference to moral-psychological mechanisms, consistent with this study's focus on the motivational and self-regulatory pathways of AI-related academic conduct.

Molenaar's (2022a, 2022b) model conceptualizes learning with AI as a process of shared, fluctuating regulatory control between the learner and the AI system, in which the degree of control transferred to the learner depends on the learner's own regulatory skill and understanding of the AI tool. This framework accounts for why students with differing levels of AI literacy engage in fundamentally different patterns of self-regulated learning when using GenAI tools: students with higher AI literacy are better positioned to retain regulatory control—setting goals, monitoring progress, and evaluating AI-generated output—rather than ceding this control to the AI system, a pattern associated with reduced academic motivation and eroded self-regulation.

This study adopts the AI Ethics and Evaluating and Creating AI dimensions of Ng et al.'s (2021b) framework as the conceptual link to Molenaar's hybrid regulation model: students with greater competency in evaluating and ethically applying AI are hypothesized to retain greater regulatory control over their learning process, thereby sustaining stronger academic motivation and more effective self-regulated learning, and consequently engaging in less academic misconduct. According to these two integrated frameworks, AI Literacy has a significant impact on Academic Misconduct through the sequential mediation of Academic Motivation and Self-Regulation Learning (Ng et al., 2021b; Molenaar, 2022a, 2022b).

1.2.6. The Impact of Perceived GenAI Interactivity on GenAI-Assisted Creativity: The Mediating Role of Flow Experience and Cognitive Engagement
AI literacy is hypothesized to exert a direct effect on academic misconduct: students with higher AI literacy are more competent in understanding, using, and evaluating GenAI tools, making them less reliant on unauthorized AI assistance to complete academic work (Lund et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023), consistent with Gruenhagen et al.'s (2024) finding that students with lower AI literacy were more likely to use AI tools in ways that constituted misconduct.

Beyond this direct effect, AI literacy is expected to shape academic misconduct indirectly through academic motivation. Higher AI literacy is expected to strengthen students' academic motivation, consistent with evidence that learning motivation significantly influences college learners' use of AI in self-directed learning (Huang & Derakhshan, 2025); in turn, stronger academic motivation is expected to reduce academic misconduct, consistent with Murdock and Anderman's (2006) integrated motivational model of student cheating, which holds that students' pursuit of mastery- rather than performance-avoidance goals reduces their likelihood of engaging in dishonest behavior.

AI literacy is similarly expected to shape academic misconduct indirectly through self-regulated learning. Empirical research shows that AI literacy can significantly predict self-regulated learning (Bećirović et al., 2026; He & Wang, 2026; X. Wang & Zhang, 2026), and students with more advanced AI literacy profiles consistently demonstrate stronger SRL competencies (D. Wang et al., 2026). In turn, stronger self-regulated learning is expected to reduce academic misconduct: students who effectively monitor and regulate their own learning process while using GenAI tools retain control over their academic work rather than ceding that control to the AI system, reducing their reliance on AI-facilitated shortcuts (Molenaar, 2022a; Zhang & Chen, 2025). Stephens's (2018) study emphasized the causal effect of motivation and self-regulated learning on academic misconduct and the need to investigate the mediating role of motivation and self-regulated learning in AI literacy and academic misconduct. Self-regulated learning and learning motivation are interdependent and cannot be understood separately (Huang & Derakhshan, 2025). Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H1. Perceived GenAI Interactivity has a significant positive effect on GenAI-Assisted Creativity.
H2. Perceived GenAI Interactivity has a significant positive effect on Flow Experience.
H3. Perceived GenAI Interactivity has a significant positive effect on Cognitive Engagement.
H4. Flow Experience has a significant positive effect on GenAI-Assisted Creativity.
H5. Cognitive Engagement has a significant positive effect on GenAI-Assisted Creativity.
H6. Flow Experience has a significant positive effect on Cognitive Engagement.
H7. Flow Experience mediates the effect of Perceived GenAI Interactivity on Cognitive Engagement.
H8. Flow Experience mediates the effect of Perceived GenAI Interactivity on GenAI-Assisted Creativity.
H9. Cognitive Engagement mediates the effect of Perceived GenAI Interactivity on GenAI-Assisted Creativity.

1.3. Methodology
This study will adopt a quantitative research design to examine the effect of AI Literacy on Academic Misconduct through the serial mediating roles of Academic Motivation and Self-Regulated Learning among Chinese undergraduate students, using a three-wave longitudinal survey design and structural equation modeling (SEM) in RStudio. (As an alternative or supplement to the quantitative mediation design, a quasi-experimental component could be incorporated—for example, comparing academic misconduct rates before and after an AI-literacy or self-regulated-learning classroom intervention across matched course sections—to provide causal evidence that goes beyond correlational mediation analysis.)

1.3.1. Sample
Hancock and Feng’s (2026) nmax calculator was applied to evaluate whether the sample size was sufficient for SEM. Assuming a minimum standardized path coefficient of γ = 0.20 and moderate multicollinearity among predictors (ρc = 0.30), the required minimum sample size is 213. Considering an anticipated longitudinal attrition rate of 20% across the three waves of data collection, consistent with prior three-wave AI literacy research (Liu et al., 2026), the final required sample size at T1 will be at least 267 participants to ensure a fully matched, valid sample of no fewer than 213 by T3.

1.3.2. Demographics
A total of X university students from various universities in China will be recruited to voluntarily participate in this study.

1.3.3. Measures
Student AI literacy will be measured using Zhao et al.'s (2022) scale, which includes 20 items assessing four dimensions of student AI literacy: Knowing and Understanding AI (KUAI), Applying AI (AAI), Evaluating AI Application (EAIA), and AI Ethics (AIE). To capture both permitted and prohibited forms of student AI use, this measure will be supplemented with the Academic AI Usage Inventory, retaining the Constructive usage and Irresponsible usage subscales and the Overreliant usage subscale as indicators of ethical versus unethical academic AI use (Elhami Athar, 2025). The AAUI is a 17-item self-report measure originally developed by Elhami Athar (2025) to assess three distinct profiles of academic AI usage: Constructive, Overreliant, and Irresponsible. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("Strongly Disagree") to 5 ("Strongly Agree") and load onto three subscales of Constructive (4 items), Overreliant (7 items), and Irresponsible (6 items).

Academic misconduct will be measured using the RAINZ-S scale (Stephens et al., 2024), developed from the nationwide Research on Academic Integrity in New Zealand (RAINZ) survey of 4,493 undergraduates. Participants will be asked to indicate how often, in the past 12 months, they engaged in a variety of academically dishonest behaviors, supplemented with AI-facilitated misconduct items (e.g., submitting AI-generated content as one's own work) consistent with the original instrument's later adaptations.

Academic motivation will be measured using the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS; Vallerand et al., 1992), which assesses intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, and amotivation toward academic work. The AMS has been widely validated across diverse student populations with adequate reliability and construct validity.

Self-regulated learning will be measured using GenAI-SRL, adapted from Lai and Gu (2011) and Lai et al. (2024) to reflect how learners engage in self-regulated learning with GenAI tools. Six items will be used to measure this construct (e.g., “When using GenAI tools for academic work, I constantly monitor my learning progress”).

1.3.4. Data collection
Data for this study will be collected using a three-wave longitudinal tracking design. A self-reported method will be employed to survey students' AI literacy, academic misconduct, academic motivation, and self-regulated learning at three time points. The research team will implement strategies such as securing college support, providing guidance, and offering appropriate incentives to effectively control attrition rates. To reduce social desirability bias and encourage honest responding on self-reported measures, no identifying information will be collected across the three waves. Participants will be provided with private spaces to complete the questionnaires, sufficient time for completion, and assurance that the data will be used solely for research purposes, thereby ensuring anonymity, confidentiality, and data validity. Sample attrition in this study is expected to be primarily due to invalid responses. Invalid questionnaires will be excluded based on criteria such as substantial missing data, patterned responses, abnormal completion time, or illogical answers.

This study will employ a time-lagged research design for data collection and will conduct three waves of questionnaire surveys to reduce the potential influence of common method bias to some extent (Yu et al., 2021). The interval between each wave of data collection is two months. Each questionnaire will be distributed and collected on-site through an online survey platform. To ensure longitudinal matching across the three waves of data collection, participants will be assigned a unique anonymous identification number (ID), which will be retained in T2 and T3 to ensure rigor and traceability.

During data processing, only questionnaires that are successfully matched across all three waves and fully complete will be retained for analysis. Responses that could not be matched longitudinally or contained missing data will be excluded. Based on the anticipated attrition rate, the final valid, fully matched sample retained across all three waves is expected to meet or exceed the required minimum sample size of 213 participants determined by the nmax approach. Finally, we will use a three-wave time-lagged design to keep a temporal separation between predictors and outcome (Podsakoff et al., 2012).

1.3.5. Data Analysis Procedure
This study will employ a structural equation modeling (SEM) approach using RStudio (version 2026.07.1) to analyze the data, including the measurement model, structural model, and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA).

First, the variables will be examined for outliers using z-values. To describe the data, we will utilize the “psych” and “dplyr” packages (Revelle, 2026; Wickham et al., 2014). For the main analysis, which involves Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), we will employ the “lavaan” package and the “semPlot” package (Epskamp, 2015; Revelle, 2026). The Lavaan package will be used to define the measurement model and to conduct CFA and the main effects SEM model, while the Modsem package will be used to test any moderated (interaction) effects specified in the model (Rosseel, 2012; Slupphaug et al., 2 Model fits will be evaluated with the robust comparative fit index (CFI), robust root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR).

CFA will be conducted with initial data collected from students to assess the structural or factorial validity of the Questionnaire. CFA is chosen because of its unique ability to test whether the hypothesized factor structure adequately represents the relationships between latent constructs and their observed indicators in pre-defined, theory-derived models (Tavakol & Wetzel, 2020). CFA will be conducted from the lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012). Before conducting CFA, data screening procedures will be conducted, including the examination of missing data, outliers, and univariate and multivariate normality.

SEM will be conducted through the following steps: model specification, model identification, model estimation, model testing, and model modification (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). The model will be specified and identified based on the conceptual framework outlined in the literature section. A bootstrapping method with robust maximum likelihood estimation will be used for model estimation. Robust maximum likelihood estimation with bootstrapped standard errors was selected, rather than PLS-SEM, because this combination maintains stable and unbiased parameter estimates even at moderate sample sizes, without the interpretive limitations associated with PLS-based composite modeling (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). Initially, the measurement model estimations will be examined to verify whether all factor loadings are significant. Subsequently, model fit indices will be examined. We will use Chi-square, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR as fit indices. For a good model fit, CFI and TLI values should be 0.90 or above, while RMSEA and SRMR values should be below 0.08 (Schumacker & Lomax, 2015). The mediation analysis using structural equation modeling (SEM) with the lavaan package in RStudio will be utilized to examine the direct, indirect, and total effects. After evaluating the path coefficients, the results will be reported.
