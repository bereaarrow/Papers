ABSTRACT
Attentional control theory indicates that concentration is considered an
important variable that contributes to learning. There are some devices
for players to practice their concentration, but there are few virtual
reality (VR) designs which can increase the level of di ﬃ culty for
students to discipline their mental concentration with incongruent
hands-on movement. To address this gap, a VR system named Tracking
Fun was designed, which requires hand-eye coordination to move a ball
to a target hole. To explore the e ﬀect of playing Tracking-fun on
players’ concentration performance, the study explored the relationship
among entity beliefs of concentration ability (EBCA), intrinsic cognitive
load (ICL), failure attribution of VR operation (FAVRO), ﬂow experience,
and gameplay performance. Students from a skills-based senior high
school took part in this experimental research, and 259 valid
questionnaires were returned. The research results showed that: EBCA
can positively predict cognitive load; EBCA can positively predict
FAVRO; ICL and ﬂow have a negative correlation; FAVRO can negatively
predict ﬂow experience when interacting with VR; and ﬂow can
negatively predict gameplay performance. Based on the results,
enriching the opportunity and di ﬃ culty level of Tracking-fun can
enhance players ’ concentration. Follow-up research suggestions are
proposed.

Introduction
Virtual reality (VR) technology can be used to quickly construct simulations to associate environ-
mental elements to provide immersive experiences. The virtual environment is brought closer to
the real environment, which is bene ﬁcial for the learners ’ cognitive development (Çakiro ğlu &
Gökoğlu, 2019; Passing et al., 2016). In order to understand the e ﬀect of VR on cognitive develop-
ment, Meyer et al. ( 2019) found that immersive VR learning was very useful for developing partici-
pants’ cognition and a ﬀect. With the advancement of VR technology, in recent years, an
increasing number of companies and schools have been using VR to train and develop the
domain-speciﬁc ability, for example, of their employees and students (Fowler, 2015). Some scholars
have also used VR modeling to study visual cognition and behavior mechanisms in sports practice
(Kim & Ko, 2019), pediatric emergency training (Matthews et al., 2020), car-detailing training (Tai

et al., 2022), and dental surgery (Al-Saud et al., 2017). However, it remains unclear whether the
reported bene ﬁts from VR practices include (1) priming the perception and motor skills; or (2)
increasing concentration on the upcoming task (Harris et al., 2020). Thus, we developed a VR
program including Labyrinth tasks, named Tracking Fun, for participants to practice, and to study
their cognitive and a ﬀective development.
In the Tracking Fun Labyrinth tasks, players have to move the ball from the bottom to the top
designated hole, focusing on avoiding dropping the ball along the way. Two trial types were inter-
leaved in the Tracking Fun Labyrinth tasks: (1) incongruent, where moving goal ≠ response direction
(i.e. moving the ball up by holding the rope down); or (2) congruent, where moving goal = response
direction (i.e. using the right hand to hold the rope down, so the ball moves to the right). A task can
control the cognitive processing which introducing con ﬂict between the stimulus location and the
response eﬀect direction (Hommel, 2011; Simon, 2011). When visual stimulus and the direction of the
movement require incongruent movement against the goal direction, it will slow the movement
compared with congruent movement (Hilchey et al., 2011). According to the Attentional control
theory (Eysenck et al., 2007), the congruent and incongruent movements require players to pay
more attention to the operation compared to other VR operations. Moreover, structural learning the-
ories suggest that cognitive and a ﬀective learning can be generated if an individual learns with con-
centration that connects a class of related movements (Harris et al., 2020). In line with this, the
present study aimed to have participants play Tracking Fun and to explore the practicing e ﬀect.
According to the achievement emotion model (Pekrun, 2006), emotion can be divided into acti-
vated emotion and deactivated emotion. Activated positive emotions (e.g. interest or ﬂow experi-
ence) can maintain cognitive ability to achieve the purpose of the task, which will have a
beneﬁcial impact on learning achievements; on the contrary, deactivated negative emotions (e.g.
anxiety or boredom) will reduce the cognitive ability of learning, thus adversely a ﬀecting the per-
formance of the learning tasks (Pekrun, 2006). In addition, the interaction between players ’ self-
belief and learning cognition in the game will trigger positive and negative emotions (Mullins & Sab-
herwal, in press). As such, the activated and deactivated emotion may a ﬀect players’ gameplay per-
formance; however, how players ’ emotional factors could be activated or deactivated has not yet
been explored in-depth in the context of playing VR (Shi et al., 2019). To understand how players
can achieve their goals while playing, this study used Tracking-fun to explore their emotion acti-
vation or deactivation with motion tracking.
Self-concept of ability can facilitate learning and achievement by promoting interest and justify-
ing learning strategies, fostering future learning and motivation (Clem et al., 2021; Pekrun et al.,
2017). For example, taking implicit belief of intelligence as one self-concept of ability, students
who have an entity belief of intelligence positively predicted negative emotions including anger,
anxiety, shame, and boredom (King, 2012). Accordingly, the present study conceptualized a
meaning system to explore the link between implicit belief of intelligence, achievement emotion,
and game performance. Taken together, the present study aimed to explore the correlates
between entity belief of concentration ability, deactivated factors (cognitive load and failure attribu-
tion of VR operation), activated factor ( ﬂow) and gameplay performance. It was expected that the
results of this study could enhance the usage of di ﬀerent types of Labyrinth game to discipline
players’ concentration.
Theoretical background
Entity belief of concentration ability (EBCA)
Dweck (1986) and Dinger and Dickhäuser ( 2013) deﬁned two types of implicit belief of intelligence:
entity belief and incremental belief. Entity belief means that intelligence is ﬁxed, and it cannot
change due to internal or external factors, while incremental belief considers that intelligence is
not ﬁxed, but can be improved through one ’s own e ﬀorts or training. Karlen et al. ( 2019) indicated

that learners with incremental belief regard intelligence as incremental, and believe that it can be
improved via their learning e ﬀort. On the other hand, Haimovitz and Dweck ( 2017) argued that lear-
ners with entity belief regard intelligence as innate, stable, and unable to change easily. The categ-
orization of people ’s perceptions of intelligence can be divided into those that are ﬁxed, and so
cannot be changed through e ﬀort and those that can be developed through e ﬀort and challenge.
Past research has indicated that those learners with entitlement beliefs are likely to avoid doing
diﬃ cult tasks as they consider failure as a threatening outcome or punishment instead of as an
opportunity to learn (Satchell et al., 2017). Considering avoiding di ﬃ cult tasks, players of Track-
ing-fun have to avoid dropping the ball into holes as they move the ball upward; thus, entity
belief is applied in this study.
Moran ( 2004) proposed that concentration means focusing on the task at hand while ignoring
other non-task factors, whereas visual selective concentration means that when information
enters the brain, only part of it will be considered. Ommundsen ( 2001) noted that learners with
entity belief will have their concentration ability and their information processing e ﬀorts
impeded. Learners with entity belief will perform poorly in terms of concentration and will lack
control, and there will be no external interference in VR that will cause cognitive stimulation
(Selzer et al., 2019). To play Tracking Fun, participants have to concentrate on their movement to
avoid the ball dropping into the wrong holes. However, the current research on implicit belief of con-
centration ability is still quite limited in terms of exploring hands-on movement. Based on this, this
study proposed EBCA as the basis for measuring learners ’ EBCA while playing Tracking Fun.
Cognitive load when using VR
There are three di ﬀerent aspects of cognitive loading: intrinsic cognitive load (ICL) (Sweller, 1988)
which is related to the task ’s structure, extraneous cognitive load (Sweller & Chandler, 1994) which
is the result of cognitive resources being allocated to working memory in order to perform oper-
ations unrelated to the task, and third, germane cognitive load (Sweller, 2010), characterized as
the load resulting from the learning process itself which is related to procedural thinking and
action. Considering playing Labyrinth VR, which involves avoiding dropping ball into holes, partici-
pants engage in some sorts of procedural thinking and action that require intensive mental e ﬀort;
therefore, ICL was considered in this study.
The use of VR technology involves individuals generating self-experience, and having bodily
immersion and sensation experiences (Armougum et al., 2019; Murray & Gordon, 2001). Additionally,
in most studies, cognitive load has been investigated in static contexts, for example, when using a
computer (Chen et al., 2017) or smartphone (Hochberg et al., 2018) to perform cognitive tasks.
However, this static con ﬁguration does not re ﬂect the VR environment where we move around
and explore the content in VR. This raises the question to be explored in this study: how does ICL
vary during navigation in the immersive context of Tracking Fun?
Failure attribution of VR operation (FAVRO)
The antecedents and consequences of intrapersonal attributions (i.e. how one sees oneself) and
interpersonal attributions (i.e. how one sees others) are addressed by the attribution theory
(Graham, 2020). People tend to attribute successes as happening due to the self, while failures
are often thought of as being due to others (Levine et al., 2017). In VR, students need to recognize
3D objects, manipulate 3D components, and use spatial –temporal abilities to observe and control
the steps needed to face the challenges posed by the user interface design (Sun et al., 2019).
However, if the visual observation and movement control step by step, players may attribute the
failure to a “dysfunctional” UI design (Rascle et al., 2015). Those who attribute failure to uncontrol-
lable situational causes experience unfavorable behavioral consequences (e.g. Le Foll et al., 2008;
Rascle et al., 2015). Graham ( 2020) stressed the power of the context (situation) in in ﬂuencing

attribution thinking. In line with this, how players perceived the dysfunction of the UI design in
Tracking-fun and how they attributed failure were explored in this study.
Flow experience
Flow is de ﬁned as an optimal experience, and has been experienced by people in di ﬀerent work or
learning settings (Csikszentmihalyi, 1975, 1990). This optimal experience of ﬂow is characterized by
cognitive absorption, enjoyment, and time distortion (Chen, Woolcott, & Sweller, 2017). In academia,
such optimal experiences have been examined using games to study ﬂow experience (Erhel & Jamet,
2019). The primary function of VR is to provide users with an immersive experience by enhancing
their telepresence. A previous study indicated that VR technologies can amplify users ’ ﬂow experi-
ences via vividness, interactivity, and telepresence to a greater extent than traditional media (Kim &
Ko, 2019). Another study indicated that simulation-based learning enables students to achieve suc-
cessful learning by concentrating on the activities that can generate ﬂow (Yoo & Kim, 2018).
However, few studies have focused on identifying the mechanism of playing VR that informs such
user ﬂow experiences in the context of Labyrinth. Thus, considering Csikszentmihalyi ’s( 2000) asser-
tion that ﬂow experience relies on the individual ’s perception of task di ﬃ culty, players’ ﬂow experi-
ence when playing Tracking-fun was explored.
Gameplay performance
Digital learning environments a ﬀect students’ activity-related achievement emotions and learning
performance. Using technology enables online assessment of students ’ progress while they are
learning (Schrader & Grassinger, 2021). For example, to identify the learning e ﬀects of VR, Choi
et al. ( 2020) designed a VR game to train operators to improve their safety-oriented behavior.
Based on this practice, students can improve their learning performance by receiving adaptive,
timely, and instant feedback during learning or after su ﬃ cient eﬀort (Tu et al., 2020). Škola and Liar-
okapis ( 2018) found that the group which learned using embodied VR had signi ﬁcantly higher
average accuracy in their motor actions than the control group which did not use embodied VR.
As Labyrinth VR is a kind of motor action with mental imagery, improving the players ’ bodily move-
ment may lead them to have higher accuracy to pass those holes. Thus, the improvement of the par-
ticipants when playing Tracking-fun was explored in this study.
Research model and hypotheses
Research model
Baker et al. ( 2010) observed ﬁrst that learners use multimedia to learn about cognitive conditions
(e.g. cognitive load, excessive information received, decentralized concentration), then observed
the learner ’s emotional conditions (e.g. emotion, mood, and feeling), and ﬁnally the impact on
human–computer interaction. Based on Attentional Control theory (Eysenck et al., 2007), this
study integrated the scholars’ deﬁnition of the variables of EBCA, cognitive load, FAVRO, ﬂow experi-
ence, and gameplay performance based on the theory of multimedia learning cognitive sentiment
proposed by Moreno ( 2006). The proposed research framework and research hypotheses according
to the relationship of each variable are shown in Figure 1, and the correlation between the various
constructs was veri ﬁed with structural equations.

EBCA and FAVRO
Implicit beliefs about how stable or malleable human attributes have an impact on personal percep-
tions and attribution (Hughes, 2015). Entity theorists place a greater emphasis on attributing failure
or success in a task. Individuals who believe that traits are ﬁxed tend to also believe that entity belief
causes behavioral failure (Hughes, 2015). Learners with intelligence entity belief will blame the
problem on external factors when facing failures (Blackwell et al., 2007). Molden and Dweck
(2006) suggested that when someone ’s implicit beliefs are known, it is possible to understand
and even predict how they will perform and deal with problems when performing a task. Research
on implicit beliefs about intelligence and human attributes has provided support for this claim
(Hughes, 2015). How EBCA is related to FAVRO when playing Tracking Fun was therefore hypoth-
esized as follows:
H2: EBCA is positively related to FAVRO.

FAVRO and ﬂow experience
Heider ( 1958) stated that an individual ’s perceptions of success or failure in activity will have an
impact on how much e ﬀort they invest in that activity. For instance, attributions of a positive per-
formance can result in greater willingness in the future to perform similar tasks. In other words,
this helps to explain how individuals explain learning activities, and how their attributions a ﬀect

bution bias can result in increased negative perceptions of the activity, which can then lead to
increased levels of criticism and blame of the external factors. In line with this cycle, the more
failure attribution to external factors one has, the greater the loss of controllability one has in hand-

H4: FAVRO is negatively related to ﬂow experience.
Flow experience relevant to gameplay performance
As new interface technologies have been introduced, controllers have been considered as a variable
in game play and a ﬀect players’ ﬂow experience (Erhel & Jamet, 2019). Previous studies have found
that playing with new devices when learning has evoked frustration, and inhibited ﬂow (Hong et al.,
2020). Williams ( 2018) found the importance of ﬂow that has developed over time through gaming
experience. However, adding VR immersive experience to a science lab simulation causes more pres-
ence but less learning (Makransky et al., 2019). Thus, how ﬂow experience is related to performance
when playing Tracking Fun was hypothesized as follows:
H5: Flow experience is positively related to gameplay performance.
Method
The tracking-fun VR game
The features of tracking fun
To perform the Tracking Fun Labyrinth tasks, the players need to combine action observation and
motor imagery (AO + MI) with hand-eye coordination, where action observation (AO) means delib-
erate and structured observation of movement (Neuman & Gray, 2013), and motor imagery (MI) is
the mental rehearsal of movement, which is typically accompanied with body or hand movements
(Wright et al., 2018). The di ﬃ culty levels increase by increasing the density of the holes from level 1
to level 5 (level 1 as shown in Figure 2, and level 5 in Figure 3). Players have to move a ball from the
bottom to the top and prevent it from dropping into the holes on the way to the ﬁnal goal.
How to play
The player needs to grasp the rope through the handle of the game, and use an up and down move-
ment to control the ball in the left and right directions, to send the ball into the hole speci ﬁed by the
arrow. However, if the player operates it too quickly, the ball will fall o ﬀ the wooden board, as shown
in Figure 3 .
Experiment design and procedure
The Hawthorne e ﬀect can occur in experimental studies which embed technology-enhanced learn-
ing and can in ﬂuence the outcome of the experiment (Tai et al., 2022). To avoid the Hawthorne

eﬀect, this study adopted a single-group quasi-experimental design. At an initial meeting with the
contact principal and teacher, the researcher ’s role was discussed. The purpose of the study was
explained as being about classroom interactivity from a psychological perspective. Formal
consent was given by the teachers who taught advertising design through the teacher-contact at
the school. A standard con ﬁdentiality form was signed by the researcher, guaranteeing that no sen-

initial meeting. Moreover, participants were informed that they had the right to end the experiment
(i.e. using VR and ﬁlling in the questionnaire) at any time.
The 297 participants were advertising majors who were expected to need concentration in their
project work. They took part in the study by playing Tracking Fun. First, the subjects were given 5

Participants
A total of 297 students from a senior technical high school in New Taipei City, Taiwan participated in
this study, but there were only 259 valid samples used for the statistical analysis. In terms of gender,
50 (19.3%) were males and 209 (80.7%) were females. For school grade, 135 (52.1%) were ﬁrst grade
and 124 (47.9%) were second grade, and regarding age, 117 (45.2%) were 15 years old, 93 (35.9%)
were 16 years old, and 49 (19.9%) were 17 years old. There were 49 (18.9%) who had experience of
using VR and 210 (81.1%) who did not.
Questionnaire
The development of the questionnaire
The questionnaire items were adapted from previous studies and were translated into Chinese. To
ensure the face validity of the questionnaire, ﬁve domain experts reviewed and revised the items,
and a high level of agreement was reached. A 5-point Likert scale was adopted to assess the
degree of agreement for each item, where 1 stands for strongly disagree and 5 stands for strongly
agree. After the questionnaires were returned, the reliability and validity of the items and constructs
were examined by ﬁrst-order con ﬁrmatory factor analysis, and were then subjected to structural
equation modeling.
EBCA
This study adapted the “Intelligence Theory Scale” compiled by Yeager and Dweck ( 2012) to analyze
the degree of the learners ’ entity belief to form the “EBCA Scale ”; six items were designed for this

ICL
Based on the cognitive load theory, this study revised the “Cognitive Load” scale (Hart & Staveland,
1988) to develop the “ICL Scale”; nine items were designed for this construct. Example items include,
“I must pay great attention to using VR to go through the Tracking Fun practice ” and “I must pay

FAVRO
This study was based on the attribution theory proposed by Weiner ( 1976), and it referred to the
“Internet Cognitive Failure Scale ” designed by Hong et al. ( 2017) to develop the “Failure Attribution
Scale”; ﬁve items were designed for this construct. Example items include, “If my performance of
operating VR is not good, I think it is because the operating system is too complicated ” and “If
my performance of the VR operation is not good, I think it is because the system is too sensitive,
and it lets the object fall with a slight touch. ”
Flow experience
This study adapted the “Flow Experience Scale ” designed by Pearce et al. ( 2005), to develop the
“Flow Experience Scale ”; eight items were designed for this construct. Example items include:
“When playing Tracking Fun, I was so attentive that I didn ’t feel any time passing at all ” and
“When playing Tracking Fun, I experienced a state of ecstasy. ”
Item analysis
Using ﬁrst-order conﬁrmatory factor analysis to test the internal validity of the questionnaire items,
this research retained items with factor loadings larger than 0.6, based on the suggestion of Hair
et al. ( 2010). Accordingly, four items of entity beliefs of concentration ability were retained from
six items, seven items of cognitive load were retained from nine items, four items of failure attribu-
tion of VR operation were retained from ﬁve items, and seven items of ﬂow experience were retained
from eight items.
The interpretation range of the study was judged by measuring the external validity of the items
(Cor, 2016), and a t-test was performed on the top 27% and the bottom 27% of the values of all
respondents for each question; if the t-value (critical ratio) was greater than 3 (*** p < .001), then
the external validity was considered to be signi ﬁcant (Green & Salkind, 2004).

Results
Gameplay performance analysis
To assess information processing pro ﬁciency, the speed with which this processing occurs must be
measured. This speed is deﬁned as the time it takes to complete a task, or the amount of work that is
completed within a speci ﬁed period of time (DeLuca & Kalmar, 2008). The performance of playing is
recorded in the Tracking Fun system. In this study, participants can challenge the level inde ﬁnitely
within 20 minutes until they pass. The average number of completed levels is 3, as shown in Table 3.

in line with the scholar ’s recommended standards, so they had a good model ﬁt.

small (Sullivan & Feinn, 2012), and statistically, researchers are also encouraged to indicate the
size of the e ﬀect amount in the report (Kline, 2004; Nakagawa & Cuthill, 2007), and the e ﬀect
amount between the evaluation constructs is divided into three categories: the low e ﬀect amount
is 0.02, the medium e ﬀect amount is 0.15, and the high e ﬀect amount is 0.35 (Hair et al., 2010)
(Figure 5 ).
Discussion
Harris et al. ( 2020) suggested that cognitive and a ﬀective learning can be generated if an individual
learns with concentration that connects a class of related movements. To understand the e ﬀect of
cognitive and emotional eﬀect on playing Tracking Fun, the present study adapted the achievement
emotion model to form the research model to discuss the correlates between belief-emotion-
outcome, and veri ﬁed the hypotheses as follows.
EBCA can positively predict ICL
One possible interpretation for intelligence being the most strongly connected working memory
that might limit or enhance cognitive re ﬂection (Dorigoni et al., 2022). Although it has been
suggested that there is a link between motor skills and cognitive load changes while processing a
task (e.g. Adolph & Joh, 2007), Klupp et al. ( 2021) indicated that the evidence of a link between
motor and cognitive skills is a result of intelligence to develop trajectories. Considering playing
Tracking-fun which embodied cognition, motor and cognitive abilities are profoundly intercon-
nected, players have to use motor and spatial ability together. How ICL is associated with EBCA

was veriﬁed in H1. By avoiding dropping the ball into holes while playing Tracking Fun, the player ’s
attention was diverted from the cognitive task. That is, a task modulates the cognitive load can intro-
duce con ﬂict between the stimulus location and the response e ﬀect direction (Sankaranarayanan

operations. This result is consistent with Hong et al. ’s study ( 2019) in which they found that
entity belief is associated with ICL.
EBCA can positively predict FAVRO
According to Attentional Control Theory (Eysenck et al., 2007), the in ﬂuence of stimulus-driven pro-
cessing on goal-directed regulatory processes is increased by anxiety, which also reduces processing
eﬃ ciency by a ﬀecting the cognitive or attentional resources that are used to perform tasks. In the
incongruent condition, the individual has a low chance of correct responses due to the great
diﬃ culty of the task (Pan et al., 2020). Some individuals may not have enough attentional control,
which means that they are easily distracted, and their intelligence a ﬀects on perceptual reasoning
and processing speed (Klupp et al., 2021). Considering this, how players ’ EBCA a ﬀects their
FAVRO while playing Tracking-fun was veri ﬁed according to H2. That is, the learner ’s intelligence
concept is that if someone adopts an entity belief theory, he or she will avoid facing challenges
and only accomplish things under his or her control, while avoiding encountering things that
cannot be controlled (Ehrlinger et al., 2016). Consequently, entity theorists place greater emphasis
on attributing failure or success in a task, and individuals who believe that personal traits are
ﬁxed are also likely to cause behavioral failure (Hughes, 2015). If the VR scenes had transferred so
that they could not track and move quickly, students may have blamed the usability of the VR.
ICL can negatively predict ﬂow experience
Choi and Baek (2011) found that a multimedia virtual learning environment without external distrac-
tion can enhance levels of ﬂow. When the learner is participating in the game, the higher the internal
cognitive load, the lower the ﬂow experience (Mahfouz et al., 2020). However, a previous study indi-
cated that the relationship between ﬂow experiences and germane cognitive loads was positive
(Chang et al., 2017), but Hong et al. ( 2019) pointed out that the use of game learning had a negative
association with ICL and ﬂow experience. In playing Tracking Fun via VR, incongruent information
and motor control appear simultaneously; however, when playing Tracking Fun, most players per-
ceived low levels of ICL ( M = 2.599, SD = 0.826), but they had high levels of Flow ( M = 3.249, SD =
0.859). To test the correlate between ICL and ﬂow experience, the present study found that H3
was negatively supported, indicating that most students can be immersed in playing Tracking Fun.
FAVRO can negatively predict ﬂow experience
The ﬂow experience can measure the participant ’s feelings of participating in the activity, whether it
is full of joy or aversion to the activity (Leung, 2019). The attributions theory can explain how these
attributions in ﬂuence their perceptions and in ﬂuence their ﬂow experience (Jackson, 2019).
However, the more failure attribution to external factors one has, the more loss of controllability
one has in handling the activity (Williams, 2010) and the more ﬂow experience will decrease
(Conard & Marsh, 2014). When playing Tracking Fun, the player needs to grasp the rope and use
an up-and-down movement to control the ball in the left and right directions to avoid dropping
the ball into the holes on the board. If the player operates the rope too quickly, the ball will fall
oﬀ the board. If the ball drops into a hole and/or falls o ﬀ the board too often, players may attribute
their failure to the design of Tracking Fun and so cannot become immersed in the game to

experience ﬂow. Therefore, in testing the prediction of FAVRO to ﬂow experience, the present study
found that H4 was negatively supported.
Flow experience can positively predict gameplay performance
Williams ( 2018) found the importance of mental models that are dynamic, malleable, and can be
adapted to new and incoming information. Such models have received considerable attention in
relation to controller e ﬀects (McGloin et al., 2016). Related research shows that high ﬂow experience
has a signi ﬁcant inﬂuence on learners ’ game performance (Erhel & Jamet, 2019; Hong et al., 2020).
“Challenge” and “interactivity” serve as representatives of ﬂow experience in games and have posi-
tive eﬀects on game outcomes (Alan et al., 2022). When playing Tracking Fun, ﬂow experience can
make participants experience more challenge and interactivity and be more focused on the activity,
in turn resulting in higher performance. When testing whether participants ’ ﬂow experience in VR
learning had a positive impact on their gameplay performance, the present study found that H5
was positively supported; this ﬁnding is consistent with the research of Erhel and Jamet ( 2019).
Conclusion
Achievement emotion is de ﬁned as an emotion directly related to achievement activities or out-
comes (Pekrun, 2006). Achievement emotion is thought to occur when individuals feel in control
or have no control over academic activities and outcomes that are important to them (Harley
et al., 2019). Players’ achievement emotion states will be a ﬀected by the ease or di ﬃ culty of control-
ling the activity, which will then in ﬂuence their game performance (Yeh et al., 2016). VR has the role
of stimulating learners, but VR learning may also cause learners to experience cognitive load and
concentration problems, and may therefore in ﬂuence their gameplay performance. The results of
this study con ﬁrm that an increase in participants ’ EBCA will also increase their ICL and FAVRO
when playing Tracking Fun; an increase in ICL and FAVRO will decrease their ﬂow experience
when playing Tracking Fun; and increasing ﬂow experience when playing Tracking Fun will increase
their gameplay performance.
Implication
According to Attentional Control theory, this research aimed to explore the correlates between
EBCA, ICL, FAVRO, and ﬂow experience when using VR with hand movements. The results
advance our understanding of how EBCA can inhibit the ﬂow experience, implying that students
with a high level of EBCA should be trained to reduce their entity belief and ﬁnd more enjoyment
in interacting with Tracking Fun.
The present research is the ﬁrst study to design a VR game which includes congruent and incon-
gruent movements in hand-eye coordination to explore the correlation between cognition and
aﬀect. Meyer et al. ( 2019) studied the e ﬀect of pre-training by learning through immersive VR.
The results can be applied to concentration training with hand movements, such as that required
by bus drivers.
Limitations and future study
Regarding the help of new technology, if the new technology is simply introduced into the learning
environment, there is no guarantee that it can e ﬀectively improve the learner ’s gameplay perform-
ance. If the characteristics and advantages of new technologies cannot be used e ﬀectively, learners
may encounter more barriers in the learning process, causing additional cognitive load (Liu, Cheng, &
Huang, 2011). This study used a “VR concentration system ” to evaluate the concentration perform-
ance of the participants, but not all participants using VR can have the same acceptance, so other

measurement methods related to concentration can also be combined as research tools to verify
whether the results of this study can be replicated.
Quasi-experimental designs usually lack one or two variables that should be controlled for in
experimental studies, and randomization is rarely possible (Thompson & Panacek, 2006). The
present study encountered problems with randomized participant assignment due to the study
size and participant recruitment in the study design. Therefore, it is suggested that subsequent
studies may expand participant recruitment when conducting VR experiments and conduct pre-
testing prior to grouping experiments to ensure homogeneity in participant assignment.
Considering that the design profession needs to cultivate good concentration, this study was con-
ducted to understand the performance of this student group when learning with VR. Therefore, this
study recruited participants from advertising design majors in a technical high school in New Taipei
City, Taiwan. However, the gender imbalance of the participants who were all advertising design
majors is also a problem, and the sample size was too small, which may lead to poor inferencing.
Therefore, it is suggested that subsequent studies could expand the sampling of participants
from diﬀerent design disciplines, or from university design disciplines, and also expand the sampling
cities to increase the ability to infer from the study results.
